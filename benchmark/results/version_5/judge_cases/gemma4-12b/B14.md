The core of the problem lies in three critical, interdependent bottlenecks: - Advanced Packaging: The demand for Nvidia's accelerators is fundamentally a demand for TSMC's Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate (CoWoS packaging). Each H100 or B100 requires a large silicon interposer and complex assembly that TSMC is struggling to scale. While TSMC is aggressively expanding CoWoS capacity, aiming to more than double it year-over-year, industry demand is growing at an even faster rate. We estimate current demand exceeds supply by a factor of 1.4x to 1.6x, a gap that will likely persist through the next 18-24 months. - High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM): Modern AI accelerators are memory-bandwidth bound. The move to HBM3 and HBM3e has created a severe shortage. SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron are the only suppliers, and their capacity is fully booked for the next 12-15 months. The manufacturing process for HBM is complex, involving Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs) and stacking multiple DRAM dies, leading to lower yields and longer cycle times compared to traditional DRAM. - Leading-Edge Wafer Fabrication: While not as acute as packaging, capacity at TSMC's 4nm and 3nm nodes is exceptionally tight. Nvidia, Apple, AMD, and others are all competing for a finite number of wafers. The cost of these wafers continues to rise, with 3nm wafers commanding prices in the $17k-$22k range. This high baseline cost trickles down through the entire bill of materials (BOM), setting a high floor for the final GPU price. ASSISTANT: The primary reason for the recent surge in NVIDIA GPU prices is a fundamental shift in the company's business model and the resulting "demand spillover" from the artificial intelligence boom. Essentially, the massive demand for AI data center chips is cannibalizing the supply chain for consumer electronics.

There are three specific factors in the supply chain causing this:

First, there is a severe shortage of high-bandwidth memory. Manufacturers like Samsung and SK Hynix are prioritizing the production of HBM, which is used in high-margin AI accelerators, over the GDDR memory used in consumer gaming cards. Because these memory components share manufacturing resources, the consumer market is being deprioritized.

Second, there is a bottleneck in advanced packaging. Producing high-end chips requires a specific process called Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate. Because demand for AI chips is growing faster than the capacity to produce this packaging, it has created a shortage that affects all high-end GPUs, including those intended for gamers.

Finally, there is a "demand spillover" effect. Because large enterprises and AI researchers cannot get enough high-end datacenter chips, they are buying up high-end consumer cards, like the RTX 5090, to use as a substitute. This has created a situation where professional buyers are competing directly with consumers for the same limited stock, driving prices well above the original manufacturer's suggested retail price.
