Understanding Fed Interest Rate Decisions The federal funds rate is the main lever the Federal Reserve uses to manage the economy. In keeping with its mandate, the Fed prefers inflation to be 2% and for the labor market to be as fully employed as possible without causing inflation to go beyond that target rate. Should prices rise too slowly (like in the 2010s), the Fed will lower rates to spur economic growth. The theory is that lower rates incentivize more economic activity because banks are more likely to lend cash than let it sit on the sidelines earning meager returns. Conversely, when prices rise too quickly (like in the aftermath of the pandemic), the Fed will raise rates in order to stymie economic activity. The unemployment rate will tend to rise (as has happened over the past year or so), as businesses find it harder to invest. The Fed, though, doesn’t just rely on inflation and employment metrics to devise its monetary policy. It considers all sorts of indicators, from housing starts to consumer spending, to gauge just how well the economy is performing. It will act quickly in the face of unforeseen events, such as a global pandemic or terrorist attack. While Congress holds the purse strings, market participants look to how and why the Fed acts when placing their bets on the future.
