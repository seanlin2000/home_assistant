Quantizing stores each weight in fewer bits than the usual 16. Weights alone: 16-bit about 140 GB; 4-bit 70 billion times half a byte, about 35 GB (real 4-bit files run roughly 38 to 42 GB because of scales and mixed precision); 2-bit about 17.5 GB (about 20 to 25 GB in practice). ASSISTANT: Quantizing means compressing each weight from full precision, like 16 or 32-bit floating point, down to fewer bits, so the model needs less memory to load and run. For a 70-billion-parameter model, 4-bit storage is four bits per weight, or half a byte, which works out to roughly 35 gigabytes of memory just for the weights. At 2-bit, that's a quarter byte per weight, about 17.5 gigabytes. The trade-off is quality: fewer bits means coarser representation of each weight, so outputs can lose accuracy, reasoning ability, or nuance. The loss is usually modest at 4-bit, often barely noticeable for general chat, but more noticeable on specialized tasks. At 2-bit, degradation can be significant, though newer methods like low-rank compensation or group quantization recover a fair amount. It's fundamentally an accuracy-versus-memory bargain.
