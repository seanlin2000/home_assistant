Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Quantizing stores each weight in fewer bits than the usual 16. Weights alone: 16-bit about 140 GB; 4-bit 70 billion times half a byte, about 35 GB (real 4-bit files run roughly 38 to 42 GB because of scales and mixed precision); 2-bit about 17.5 GB (about 20 to 25 GB in practice). The context cache and activations come on top. Trade-offs: some loss of quality, small at 4-bit and marked at 2-bit (reasoning and factual accuracy suffer most); faster generation, because inference is limited by memory bandwidth; careful methods (GPTQ, AWQ, importance-weighted quantization) reduce the loss. Wrong memory figures are an error.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means representing its weights with fewer bits instead of the usual 16-bit floating point, so each parameter takes up less space. A 70-billion-parameter model at 16-bit needs about 140 gigabytes for its weights alone. At 4-bit, that drops to roughly 35 gigabytes—four times smaller—and at 2-bit, about 17.5 gigabytes. The trade-off is accuracy: the weights can only take on a limited set of values, so fine-grained differences are rounded away. Four-bit usually costs only a little quality, while two-bit is aggressive and can noticeably degrade coherence or reasoning. The upside is that smaller weights mean lower memory, cheaper inference, and the possibility of running on consumer hardware.
