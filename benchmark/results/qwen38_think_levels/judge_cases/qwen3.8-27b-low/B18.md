For your 16 gigabytes, the sweet spot is the 14-billion dense model, Qwen3 14B. At the common Q4 quantization it's roughly nine or ten gigabytes of weights, so it fits entirely on the GPU with several gigabytes left over for the context window. That headroom matters: it means you can keep a long conversation or a big document in memory without the speed tanking, and it generates fast. This is the "comfortable daily driver" pick.

If you want to push to the bigger 27-billion models — the recent Qwen3.6 27B and Qwen3.8 27B — you're right at the edge. At Q4 they're about 17 to 18 gigabytes, which nominally exceeds your card. In practice Ollama and llama.cpp will offload a slice of the layers into your system RAM, so it does run, but you'll be slower than if it all sat on the GPU. One independent test showed Qwen3.8 27B running a full 262,000-token context on a 16 gigabyte card at around 35 to 42 tokens per second, so it's genuinely usable, not just theoretically possible. The catch is that longer context eats more memory for the key-value cache, which squeezes that offloaded setup even tighter. So the 27B is the "max quality" option on your hardware, but you trade some speed and headroom.

The 32-billion dense model at Q4 sits around 20 gigabytes, which is too much to fit, so you'd be leaning on a lot of RAM offload and the speed drop is more noticeable. That's not where I'd go on 16 gigabytes.

Now, the interesting modern option is the mixture-of-experts variants, like the Qwen3-Coder 30B-A3B. These have around 30 billion total parameters but only about 3 billion active per token, so they compute fast. The trap is that memory isn't about active parameters — you still have to hold all the experts' weights, which lands around 19 gigabytes. That spills over 16 gigabytes, but llama.cpp has a specific trick, the CPU-MoE flags, that keeps the expert weights in your system RAM and leaves the attention layers on the GPU. That lets a 19 gigabyte model run on a 16 gigabyte card at lower but reasonable speed. If you care a lot about coding, this MoE route is worth testing because you get near-27B-level quality with MoE-level speed, at the cost of that partial RAM dependency.

On quantization, this is where the quality-versus-memory dial really lives. Q4, the K-mixed four-bit version, is the default recommendation — roughly five percent quality loss for a big memory saving, and it's what most of the sizes above assume. Q5 is a middle ground if you have a little headroom. Q8 is nearly indistinguishable from full precision but roughly doubles the memory, so on 16 gigabytes it only becomes realistic for the 8 or 14-billion models. Full floating-point is server territory and irrelevant to you.

So to summarize the tradeoff in one line: for a balance of quality, speed, and context headroom, the 14-billion at Q4 is the safe choice; if you want the best possible quality and can tolerate some offload slowdown, the 27-billion at Q4 is the ceiling for your card; and if you're coding-focused, the 30-billion-A3B MoE with the CPU-experts trick is a strong wildcard. And the 8-billion is the fast, low-memory floor if you ever want instant responses over capability. One thing to keep in mind regardless of model: every model ships under Apache 2.0, so there are no licensing gates if you want to self-host or use it commercially.
