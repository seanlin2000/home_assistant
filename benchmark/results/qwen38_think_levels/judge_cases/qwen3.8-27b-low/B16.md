Now to the part you asked about that's easy to trip on: why you should be suspicious of using advertised rents as your comparison. The core problem is that an asking price is not a rent. Landlords list a sticker number and negotiate it down, so effective rents — what people actually pay at signing — sit several percent below advertised rents, and that gap widens exactly in a soft market, when there's leverage on the tenant's side. So if you take "advertised rents are X today" and say "therefore X plus four percent next year is competitive," you're doing two things that both work against you. First, your baseline is inflated, because it's asking rent, not effective rent. That lag makes your four percent look far more reasonable than it really is, because you're comparing it to a number that's already overstating the market and falling behind it.

A few more specific reasons advertised listings mislead in this comparison. Turnover and renovation effects mean freshly turned units carry a premium that doesn't reflect the steady state of the neighborhood.
