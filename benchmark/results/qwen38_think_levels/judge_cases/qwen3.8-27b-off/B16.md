## Methodology
The median contract rent used in this report is sourced from the ACS 1-year estimate. For 2025 and 2026, the median contract rent is forecasted by taking the lower of two growth rates: the average annual growth rate observed between 2021 and 2024, or the year-over-year growth rate in market asking rent. Now the risks of using advertised rents as your comparison, which is exactly what those numbers are:

First, asking rent is not contract rent. In New York City there's a well-documented and widening gap between what landlords list and what existing tenants actually pay — one report put the median asking rent at over $3,600 citywide against an estimated median contract rent of under $1,900. That gap exists precisely because long-term renters don't move; they're locked in below market. So "the market is growing 4%" really means new leases are pricing at 4% above last year's new leases, not that your current or comparable units will actually jump 4%.

Second, advertised rents skew high and toward the top of the market. Listings reflect what landlords hope to get, often in buildings with amenities, recent renovations, or prime locations. The median asking rent can be pulled up by a wave of luxury listings even while typical working-class units are flat. A 20% surge in sales above $2 million in LIC alone this year shows how the mix of what's on the market distorts the headline number.

Third, these figures lag and get revised. Most reports you'll find are one to two months old — the StreetEasy data I'm citing is from September, RentCafe's from late October.
