Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth the cash it will pay out in the future, discounted back to today's dollars. The discount rate is built on interest rates, so when the Fed raises rates, the rate used to discount future cash goes up and every future dollar is worth less today. The effect is not equal across companies. A mature company producing steady profits now gets most of its value from near-term cash, so a higher rate hurts it only a little. A growth tech company may earn little today and promise big profits ten or twenty years from now, so most of its value sits far in the future. That makes it long duration, like a long-term bond, and a small rise in the discount rate shrinks its present value a lot, because the discounting compounds over many years. Higher rates also hurt in other ways. Safe assets like Treasuries pay more, so investors demand more from stocks to compensate for the risk. Borrowing costs rise for companies that depend on cheap capital, and a slower economy can reduce earnings. That is why rate fears often hit unprofitable, high-growth tech stocks the hardest.
