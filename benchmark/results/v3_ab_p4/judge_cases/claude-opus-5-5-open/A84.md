Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: In theory, a stock is worth all the cash it will ever generate, with each future dollar discounted back to today. The discount rate starts from the safe interest rate, like Treasury yields, plus a premium for risk. When the Fed raises rates, Treasury yields usually rise, so the discount rate rises and every future dollar is worth less today. The key is that the penalty compounds with time. A dollar arriving next year barely changes in value; a dollar arriving in fifteen years shrinks a lot. That's why growth tech stocks are called long-duration assets: much of their value comes from profits expected far in the future, so they behave like long-term bonds and are very sensitive to rates. A mature company paying steady cash now, like a utility, is short-duration and hurt less. Higher rates also hit tech in other ways: borrowing to fund growth costs more, safe bonds become a more attractive alternative, and investors pay lower multiples of earnings. The rate hikes of twenty twenty-two, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, are the classic example.
