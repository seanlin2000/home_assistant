Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: It comes down to sunlight being a mix of all colors, and the air scattering some colors more than others. Sunlight looks white, but it contains every color of the rainbow. Each color travels as a wave, and blue light has short, choppy waves while red has long, lazy ones. The tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen in the air are much better at bouncing short waves around, so blue light gets scattered in every direction across the whole sky. Wherever you look, some of that bounced blue reaches your eye, so the sky looks blue. It isn't violet mostly because there's less violet in sunlight and our eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the sun is low, so its light has to travel sideways through far more air to reach you. By the time it arrives, most of the blue has been scattered away along the route, and what's left is the long-wave reds and oranges. Dust, smoke and moisture near the horizon can deepen those colors even further, which is why some sunsets are especially vivid.
