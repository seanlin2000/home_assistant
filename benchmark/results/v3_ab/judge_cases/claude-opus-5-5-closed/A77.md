Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight is a mix of every color, and each color is a wave: blue waves are short, red waves are long. As sunlight crosses the air, it hits tiny gas molecules, much smaller than the waves. Those molecules are far better at bouncing short blue waves around than long red ones, so blue gets scattered all across the sky. Wherever you look in daytime, blue light is coming at you from every direction, so the sky looks blue. Violet scatters even more, but sunlight has less of it and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so we see blue rather than purple. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light travels sideways through many times more air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives, leaving the oranges and reds. Picture a long hallway where people grab the blue balloons as you pass: by the end, only the red ones are left.
