Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: In theory a stock is worth all the cash it will produce in the future, translated into today's dollars, and interest rates set that translation. When the Fed raises rates, safe returns like Treasury yields go up, so investors demand a higher return from risky stocks too, and the discount rate rises. A higher discount rate shrinks the present value of future cash, and it shrinks distant cash the most, because the discount compounds every year. That is why growth tech stocks are called long-duration: like a thirty-year bond, most of their value comes from profits expected many years out, so a rate hike cuts their value far more than it cuts a utility or a bank that earns most of its money now. There are second-order effects too. Unprofitable companies that rely on raising money find funding more expensive, bonds become a more attractive alternative, and slower growth can trim corporate tech budgets. It is a tendency, not a law: if a company's earnings grow faster than expected, that can outweigh higher rates.
