Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question! The secret is in how sunlight interacts with the air around us. Sunlight looks white, but it's actually made up of all the colors of the rainbow mixed together. Each color travels as a wave, and they have different wavelengths: blue and violet are short, bouncy waves, while red and orange are long, lazy ones.

Now, when sunlight hits the atmosphere, it runs into billions upon billions of tiny air molecules. The short blue waves are so small that these molecules scatter them in every direction—like a pebble thrown into a pond sending ripples everywhere. That scattered blue light bounces around so much that no matter where you look up during the day, some of it finds its way to your eyes. That's why the whole sky looks blue.

The red and orange waves are too long for those tiny molecules to scatter much, so they mostly just sail straight through. During the day, they're mixed in with all that scattered blue, so you don't really notice them.

But at sunset, everything changes because of geometry. The sun is low on the horizon, so its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you—maybe ten times as much air as it does when the sun is overhead. All that extra distance means the blue light gets scattered away almost completely before it ever reaches your eyes. What's left to make it through? The long red and orange waves, which weren't bothered by the air in the first place. So the sky near the setting sun glows with those warm colors.

A fun bonus: dust, smoke, or even volcanic ash in the air can make sunsets even more dramatic, because those bigger particles scatter a little of the red light too, deepening the oranges and purples you see.
