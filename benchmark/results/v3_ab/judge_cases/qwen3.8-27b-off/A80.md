# Exchange 1

USER: What does it mean to quantize a 70-billion-parameter language model to 4-bit or 2-bit precision? How much memory would the weights require in each case, and what trade-offs does quantization introduce?

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means replacing each weight's full-precision value—usually a 16-bit or 32-bit floating-point number—with a lower-precision one. Instead of storing an exact decimal, you store a small integer code plus a shared scale factor, so the weight is snapped to the nearest point on a coarser grid. Four-bit precision stores sixteen possible values per weight; two-bit only four. The arithmetic happens at higher precision after dequantizing, so the model runs almost as usual but occupies far less memory and moves data around faster.

For the math: 70 billion parameters at 4 bits is 70 times 10 to the 9 times 4 bits, which works out to about 35 gigabytes of weights alone. At 2 bits it drops to roughly 17.5 gigabytes. For comparison, full 16-bit precision would need around 140 gigabytes, so 4-bit is a four-fold reduction and 2-bit an eight-fold one. Those figures are just the weight tensors; you still need room for activations, the KV cache during generation, and whatever overhead your runtime adds, so real memory use lands somewhat above these numbers.

The trade-offs fall into a few buckets. First, accuracy: every quantization step introduces rounding error, and at 4 bits that's usually modest—well-designed schemes like GPTQ or AWQ lose maybe a point or two on standard benchmarks, often imperceptible in conversation. At 2 bits you're much closer to the edge; quality degrades more noticeably, especially for reasoning-heavy tasks, factual recall, and code, though recent research has pushed 2-bit to be surprisingly usable for chat. Second, calibration: most good quantizers need a representative sample of data to tune the scale factors, which takes time and means the result depends on how well that sample matches your actual use. Third, tooling: you're locked into runtimes that support the format—llama.cpp, vLLM with GPTQ or AWQ kernels, bitsandbytes for 4-bit, and so on—and some formats don't port cleanly across hardware. Fourth, there are subtle failure modes: quantized models can be slightly worse at following long instructions or maintaining consistency over very long outputs, since small per-weight errors compound through many layers.

In practice, 4-bit has become the default sweet spot for running large models on consumer hardware—a 70B model at 4-bit fits in a 48-gigabyte machine with room to spare. Two-bit is more of a research frontier: genuinely interesting for squeezing a huge model into limited memory, but you should expect measurable quality loss and test it on your actual workload before committing.
