Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a great question about financial theory, and it doesn't really require any current data since it's asking about the general mechanism rather than what's happening right now in the market. Let me think through this carefully.

The core of it comes down to how investors value companies. When you buy a stock, you're buying a claim on all the profits that company will generate from now until forever. To figure out what those future profits are worth today, investors use something called discounted cash flow analysis. You take each year's expected profit and divide it by a discount rate raised to the power of how many years away it is. That discount rate is tied directly to interest rates.

Here's where the magic happens. When the Fed raises rates, that discount rate goes up. And because the math works with exponents, the impact gets dramatically bigger the further out in time you're looking. A dollar of profit next year loses a little value when rates rise. But a dollar of profit ten years from now loses a lot more value, and a dollar fifty years out loses even more. The discounting compounds against you.

Now connect that to tech stocks specifically. Most technology companies, especially the growth-oriented ones, are valued heavily on profits they expect to earn far in the future. A young software company might not be profitable today at all, or barely profitable, but investors are betting on what it'll look like a decade from now when its products dominate their markets. That's what makes them "long-duration" assets in finance speak. Duration here doesn't mean time in the way you'd think of a bond's duration, though it borrows the concept. It means how far out in time the bulk of the value sits.

So when rates go up, those distant future profits get discounted more aggressively, and since that's where most of a tech company's value lives, its valuation gets hit hard. A mature utility company or a bank, by contrast, earns steady profits now and soon, so a rate hike affects it much less. In fact, some financial stocks actually benefit from higher rates because they earn more on the money they lend out.

There's also a second channel at work. Higher rates make safe assets like bonds more attractive. If I can get a solid five percent yield on a Treasury with almost no risk, why would I chase a tech stock trading at forty times earnings? That shifts money away from risky growth stocks and toward safer income, which adds another layer of downward pressure on tech valuations.

One nuance worth mentioning: the relationship isn't perfectly one-to-one in practice. Sometimes tech stocks rally even when rates rise if the company's growth is accelerating fast enough to outpace the higher discount rate. But all else being equal, and especially during periods when the Fed is hiking aggressively, the DCF math and the bond-yield competition both point the same direction: higher rates, lower valuations for long-duration growth stocks.
