Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth the present value of the cash it will produce in the future. Analysts estimate those future cash flows and discount them back to today using a discount rate, which is built on interest rates. When the Fed raises rates, risk-free yields rise, so the discount rate rises, and every future dollar is worth less today. That hits technology stocks hardest because they are long-duration assets. Their profits are expected far in the future, so a large share of their value sits in distant years, and distant cash flows shrink the most when the discount rate goes up. A mature company paying cash today loses less. Think of it like a long bond: the longer the maturity, the more its price falls when rates rise. Higher rates also make safe alternatives such as Treasuries more attractive, and they raise borrowing costs for growth companies that depend on cheap financing. So it is not that the businesses suddenly get worse, it is that the math used to value their far-off earnings gets harsher. The effect is a tendency, and strong earnings can offset it.
