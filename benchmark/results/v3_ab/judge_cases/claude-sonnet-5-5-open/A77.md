Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of all the colors. When that light hits the gas molecules in the air, the short wavelengths, blue and violet, get bounced around much more than the long wavelengths, red and orange. That bouncing is called scattering. During the day the sun is high, so you look up and see blue light that has been scattered all across the sky toward your eyes. Violet scatters even more, but the sun gives off less of it and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so the sky looks blue. At sunset the sun is low, so its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered out and sent off in other directions, away from you. What is left to reach your eyes directly is the reds and oranges. Dust and haze can make that even more vivid. So it is the same effect both times: blue is scattered easily, and at sunset it has been scattered away before it gets to you.
