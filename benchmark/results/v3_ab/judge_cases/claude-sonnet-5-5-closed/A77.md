Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of all the colors. When it enters the atmosphere, it bumps into air molecules, and those molecules bounce short wavelengths, the blues, around far more than long wavelengths, the reds. So during the day, blue light gets scattered all across the sky and reaches your eyes from every direction, which is why the whole sky looks blue. Violet scatters even more, but the sun gives off less of it and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so we see blue. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light has to travel through much more air to reach you. By the time it gets to you, most of the blue has been scattered out of the direct beam, off toward other people's sky. What is left is the longer wavelengths, the oranges and reds, so the sun and the clouds near it glow warm colors. Same effect, just a longer trip through the air.
