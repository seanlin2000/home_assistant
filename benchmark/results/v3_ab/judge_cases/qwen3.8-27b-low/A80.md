ASSISTANT: Quantizing a language model means representing its weights with fewer bits than the precision they were trained in. A typical large model is trained and stored in 16-bit floating point, so every parameter takes up two bytes. When you quantize to 4-bit, you round each weight to a value that can be stored in just four bits, which is one quarter of the original space. 2-bit pushes it further, to an eighth.

For a 70-billion-parameter model, the numbers work out like this. At the original 16-bit precision the weights alone take up about 140 gigabytes. At 4-bit that drops to roughly 35 gigabytes, and at 2-bit it goes down to about 17 and a half gigabytes. I'm speaking in decimal gigabytes here, so divide by 1.074 if you want binary gibibytes.

The trade-offs are mainly about quality. Going from 16-bit to 4-bit loses a lot of the fine-grained information the network learned, and the model can become noticeably worse at subtle tasks: nuance in reasoning, precise factual recall, and handling unusual prompts. In practice, 4-bit is the sweet spot that most people use. It's a reasonable compromise, and techniques like group quantization or using a slightly higher-precision calibration pass can recover a fair amount of the lost quality. The overhead also matters. Even at 4-bit you need some bookkeeping for scale factors and, in a real inference setup, a separate copy of the original higher-precision weights for the arithmetic, so the "35 gigabytes" is the weight storage, not the total memory footprint.

Two-bit is a different story. The precision is so low that the model loses a substantial amount of capability, and you often need more aggressive techniques to keep it coherent. It's useful in niche situations, like running on very memory-constrained devices or as part of a larger pipeline where the loss is acceptable, but it's not something you'd reach for as a general-purpose model.

There's also a speed consideration. Quantized weights are smaller, which means they fit in memory faster and are more cache-friendly, so inference can actually be quicker even if the arithmetic is done at higher precision. But that speed benefit comes with the quality cost, and the more you push the bit count down, the steeper that cost becomes.
