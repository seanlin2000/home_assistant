Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sure, this one is a classic.

Think of sunlight as a mix of all the colors you can see, packed together so they look white. When that light hits the air around us, the molecules in the air bump into it and send it bouncing in every direction. That bouncing is what we call scattering.

Here's the key: blue light scatters way more than red light. You can think of it a bit like how a small pebble throws off a stream of water more than a big boulder would. Blue light is the "small pebble" of the visible spectrum — it's a short, energetic wave — so the tiny air molecules knock it around a lot. Red light is a long, lazy wave that mostly just plows through.

During the day, the sun is overhead, so its light has to punch through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere to reach your eyes. All that blue light gets scattered in every direction, and some of it ends up in your eyes no matter where you look. That's why the whole sky looks blue.

At sunset, the sun is right at the horizon, so its light has to travel through a much, much thicker layer of air to get to you. By the time it arrives, almost all of the blue has been scattered away in every direction. What's left to reach your eyes is mostly the longer wavelengths — reds, oranges, and a little yellow. That's the light you actually see streaming in from the horizon.

So the whole show is really just one mechanism — short light scatters more than long light — and the difference is simply how much air the sunlight has to push through. Daytime is a short trip, sunset is a long one, and the long trip filters out the blue.
