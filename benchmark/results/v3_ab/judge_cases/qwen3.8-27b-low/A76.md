Dissolution of the Soviet Union
On 26 December 1991, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved and ceased to exist as a sovereign state. It also brought an end to the Soviet Union's federal government and to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's efforts to reform the Soviet political and economic system in an attempt to stop a period of political and economic stagnation.
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 resulted from several factors: chronic economic stagnation, the unsustainable financial burden of the arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, intense ethnic nationalism and separatism within its union republics, and the destabilizing effects of Gorbachev's reforms (particularly glasnost and perestroika).
The process began with growing unrest in the country's union republics developing into an incessant political and legislative conflict between them and the central government. Until its final years, the Soviet Union was made up of 15 union republics that served as the homelands of different ethnic groups. Estonia was the first union republic to declare state sovereignty on 16 November 1988. Lithuania was the first republic to declare independence, in March 1990.
## Timeline
### 1991
#### August coup attempt
On 19 August 1991, Gorbachev's vice president, Gennady Yanayev, Prime Minister Valentin Pavlov, Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, KGB chief Vladimir Kryuchkov and other senior officials acted to prevent the union treaty from being signed by forming the "General Committee on the State Emergency", which put Gorbachev – on holiday in Foros, Crimea – under house arrest and cut off his communications. The coup leaders issued an emergency decree suspending political activity and banning most newspapers.
After three days, on 21 August 1991, the coup collapsed. The organizers were detained and Gorbachev was reinstated as president, albeit with his power much depleted.
#### August-December transition period
The Soviet Union collapsed with dramatic speed in the last quarter of 1991. Between August and December, 10 republics seceded from the union, largely out of fear of another coup. By the end of September, Gorbachev no longer had the ability to influence events outside of Moscow. He was challenged even there by Yeltsin, who had begun taking over what remained of the Soviet government, including the Kremlin.
On 12 December, the Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR formally ratified the Belavezha Accords, denounced the 1922 Union Treaty, and recalled the Russian deputies from the Supreme Soviet of the USSR. The legality of this ratification raised doubts among some members of the Russian parliament, since according to the 1978 RSFSR Constitution consideration of this document was in the exclusive jurisdiction of the Congress of People's Deputies of the RSFSR. Additionally, the Soviet Constitution did not allow a republic to unilaterally recall its deputies. However, no one in either Russia or the Kremlin objected. Any objections from the latter would have likely had no effect, since what was left of the Soviet government had effectively been rendered impotent long before December. A number of lawyers believe that the denunciation of the union treaty was meaningless since it became invalid in 1924 with the adoption of the first constitution of the USSR (in 1996 the State Duma had voiced the same position). Later that day, Gorbachev hinted for the first time that he was considering stepping down. On the surface, it appeared that the largest republic had formally seceded. However, this is not the case. Rather, Russia apparently took the line that it did not need to follow the secession process delineated in the Soviet Constitution because it was not possible to secede from a country that no longer existed.
Gorbachev's speech, as well as the replacement of the Soviet flag with the Russian flag, symbolically marked the end of the Soviet Union. However, the final legal step in the Soviet Union's demise came on 26 December, when the Soviet of Nationalities, the upper chamber of the Supreme Soviet of the Soviet Union, ratified the Belavezha Accords, effectively voting the Soviet Union out of existence (the lower chamber, the Soviet of the Union, had been unable to work since 12 December, when the recall of the Russian deputies left it without a quorum). The following day Yeltsin moved into Gorbachev's former office, though the Russian authorities had taken over the suite two days earlier. The Soviet Armed Forces were placed under the command of the Commonwealth of Independent States, but were eventually subsumed by the newly independent republics, with the bulk becoming the Armed Forces of the Russian Federation. By the end of 1991, the few remaining Soviet institutions that had not been taken over by Russia ceased operation, and individual republics assumed the central government's role.

Fall of the Berlin Wall
The Fall of the Berlin Wall and the fortified inner German border occurred after a Politburo press conference on 9 November 1989 prematurely announced that East Germans could travel without restrictions after, in the decades before, hundreds had been killed by Border Troops of the German Democratic Republic when trying to sneak through the Iron Curtain.
Soon, people gathered at Berlin border crossings, citing the news that were broadcast in East and West, and puzzled border guards eventually let them pass in the late evening hours, with live television broadcasting worldwide. The next days, thousands travelled across the border, also in other places less covered by press. After this symbolic fall, obstacles were removed, former crossings were opened again, and following demolition, L-shaped concrete segments of the Berlin wall were actually falling and spread around the world.
## Background
### Protection of the wall
The Berlin Wall, officially the "Anti-fascist protection wall", was made up of two concrete walls, one facing the West and covered with graffiti. Both walls were 4 metres tall and had a length of 155 kilometres. They were separated by a mined corridor called the death strip. This strip was heavily guarded and included 302 watchtowers (by 1989). Guards had authorization to shoot people who attempted to go through this strip.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended not with a single decisive moment but with a cascade, and it's worth separating the deep causes that made collapse possible from the specific triggers that actually brought it down in 1991.

The causes were slow-burning and structural. The Soviet economy had stagnated for well over a decade, and the state was bleeding money it no longer had into an arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, most painfully in Afghanistan. On top of that sat a system built from fifteen union republics that were really distinct national homelands, and once nationalism was allowed to surface, the glue holding them together dissolved. Finally, Gorbachev's own reforms, glasnost and perestroika, opened political space and free discussion that the party could never close again. So by the late 1980s the system was already rotten; what was missing was something to make it break.

The three most important events that finally did it were these.

First, the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989. This was the psychological trigger. A botched press conference in East Germany mistakenly signaled that people could travel freely, crowds gathered at the border crossings, and confused guards let them through, live on television for the whole world to see. It was a symbolic moment as much as a political one, but it shattered the belief that the Soviet bloc was permanent and set off a wave of revolutions across Eastern Europe that the Soviet Union, in a historic break, did not crush.

Second, the failed coup of August 1991. Hard-line officials in the Kremlin put Gorbachev under house arrest in an attempt to block a new union treaty and hold the empire together. The coup collapsed within about three days, but that short window did enormous damage. It discredited the old leadership, made the republics terrified of a renewed hard line, and pushed ten of them toward declaring independence in the following months. At the same time it handed real power to Boris Yeltsin in Moscow, so that after the coup Gorbachev effectively had no way to influence events anymore.

Third, the Belavezha Accords of December 12, 1991. The leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus met and declared the Soviet Union effectively defunct, replacing it with the Commonwealth of Independent States, a loose association they themselves controlled. Russia, the largest republic, then formally backed this, and the Soviet flag was lowered from the Kremlin in its place. The last legal step came on December 25 and 26, when the remaining Soviet legislature ratified the accords and Gorbachev resigned, formally ending the state.

So the clean distinction is this. The causes were the economy, the cost of the arms race, the buildup of nationalism, and the reforms that let all of it come into the open; those created the conditions. The triggers were the concrete events of 1989 and 1991, the wall falling, the coup failing, and the three republic leaders deciding the union was over. The causes made the collapse possible and, in a sense, inevitable; the triggers are what actually pulled it down and, once it began, made it collapse with dramatic speed in just the final quarter of 1991.
