Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a good question, and the answer comes down to how we actually price stocks in the first place.

The foundational idea is that a stock's value is the present value of all the cash it will ever generate for its owners. To get that number, you take each future dollar of profit and discount it back to today using a rate that reflects the time value of money and the risk of the business. That discount rate is built on top of the risk-free rate, which tracks Treasury yields, and Treasury yields move with what the Fed does.

So when the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and the present value of every future dollar drops. That's the first-order effect, and it applies to all stocks.

The reason tech stocks get hit disproportionately is the concept of duration, borrowed from bond math. Duration tells you how far out in time your value is concentrated. A utility company that pays a steady dividend tomorrow and for the next decade has short-duration cash flows. A tech company whose profits are tiny today but are projected to be enormous in fifteen or twenty years has very long-duration cash flows. Most of that company's value lives in the far tail.

Here's where the math does the damage. The present value of a dollar is that dollar divided by one plus the rate, raised to the number of years. The exponent is the killer. A one-point rise in the discount rate barely dents a cash flow due next year, but it can shave off a quarter or more of the value of a cash flow due in twenty years. So when rates go up, the long tail of a growth stock's value gets compressed hard, while a mature company's near-term earnings are almost untouched. That's why you see growth stocks sell off more than value stocks in a rate-hike cycle.

There's a useful analogy to bonds. A high-growth tech stock, when you strip out the risk premium, behaves a lot like a very long-dated zero-coupon bond: it pays nothing now and promises a big payoff later. Bond traders know that long-duration bonds fall more in price when rates rise than short-duration bonds do. The same logic applies to the stocks. The longer the maturity of your "promise," the more sensitive the price is to the rate.

A few secondary effects reinforce the first one. Higher rates raise the opportunity cost of holding risky stocks. If you can get four or five percent risk-free in a treasury, you're less willing to accept the uncertainty of a company that might not be profitable for years, so you demand a higher return, which means you'll pay a lower multiple for each dollar of expected earnings. Higher rates also raise borrowing costs, which matters more for capital-heavy tech companies that are still spending their way to growth. And there's a macro channel: tighter monetary policy can slow the economy, which can lower the growth forecasts themselves, so you get a double hit, both the discount rate and the numerator go the wrong way at once.

The short version is this: tech stocks are priced on the promise of distant future cash, and the value of distant future cash is the most interest-rate-sensitive number in finance. When the Fed tightens, it's effectively telling the market that the far future is less valuable than it was last month, and that's a direct headwind to the thing that makes a growth stock worth what it's worth.
