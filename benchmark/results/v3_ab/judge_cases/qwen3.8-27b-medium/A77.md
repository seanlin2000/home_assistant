Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and the answer comes down to how sunlight mixes with the air.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a blend of all the colors you'd see in a rainbow. Each color has a different wavelength: blues and violets are short and wiggly, while reds and oranges are long and stretchy. Now, the air in our atmosphere is full of tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, and when light bumps into them, it scatters in all directions. Here's the key: the short, wiggly blue light gets scattered much more easily than the long red light. So as sunlight passes through the sky, the blue bounces off in every direction and lands on your eyes from all over, which is why the whole daytime sky looks blue.

Sunset is the same physics, just a different angle. When the sun is high overhead, the light only has to travel through a thin slice of air, so the blue scatters to you and you still see that bright blue sky. But at sunset, the sun is low on the horizon, which means its light has to push through a much, much thicker layer of atmosphere to reach you. By the time it gets through all that extra air, most of the blue has already been scattered away, out of your line of sight. What's left is the red and orange, which stubbornly keep going straight, so the sky and the sun itself glow in warm colors.

You can actually see this in action on a really clear day. The further away something is, the more atmosphere its light passes through, which is why mountains and distant city skylines often look bluish or hazy, and why a sunset looks the most dramatic when the air is full of dust, smoke, or moisture, because all that extra stuff scatters even more of the blue away and leaves behind an even redder sky.

So the short version is: blue light scatters easily, so it fills the daytime sky, and at sunset the long trip through the air strips the blue away before it reaches you, leaving the reds and oranges behind.
