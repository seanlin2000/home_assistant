## Consecutive days or Flex?
The standard pass runs on consecutive days: an 8-day pass covers 8 days in a row. The Swiss Travel Pass Flex (from CHF 289) gives you the same number of travel days to use on any dates within one month — you activate each day before your first ride.
The honest rule: if you are touring Switzerland town-to-town, consecutive days is simpler and cheaper. Flex only wins when you will stay put for several days (a week in one ski resort, a work trip with weekend travel) and genuinely won’t ride on the in-between days.
## How many days do you need?
- Trip of 3–5 days: a 3 or 4-day pass matching your travel days — or compare with the Half Fare Card, which can win on short trips with few train rides.
- One-week trip: the 8-day pass is the sweet spot — it costs only CHF 40 more than 6 days and covers arrival and departure days, including your airport trains.
- Two weeks: the 15-day pass at CHF 499 works out under CHF 34 per day — less than a single Zurich–Lucerne return.

ASSISTANT: Here's a relaxed five-day plan built around the trains and the water, with the big summits done by cogwheel and cable car so you barely break a sweat.

Day one, arrive in Lucerne and let the jet lag melt. If you want your first mountain, Pilatus is the classic from Lucerne: boat over the lake to the foot of the mountain and up by the steepest cogwheel railway in Europe, with great views over the lake and the Alps. One timing note: if you're traveling in late November, the Kriens gondola and the Dragon Ride cableway were closed for maintenance until the end of that month, so the railway via Alpnachstad is the way up. Otherwise, an evening walk along the lakeside and the old town is plenty.

Day two, take the Lucerne to Interlaken train — it runs through the Brünig Pass country, one of the most beautiful standard rail lines in the country, and then the lakes open up as you approach. Spend the afternoon on Lake Thun and Lake Brienz; a boat ride on Brienz to the little village of Brienz or along to Interlaken is a zero-hike way to see the water from the water side.

Day three is your mountain day in Interlaken. With minimal hiking, Grindelwald First is the easier pick: a short train to Grindelwald, a funicular up, and a ridge walk or cliff walk with views of the Eiger north face. Jungfraujoch is more spectacular but much pricier and more of a production — I'd save it only if the rest of the trip goes badly by weather.

Day four, the long scenic move to Zermatt: the train runs via Spiez and Thun and down the Rhone valley to Visp and Täsch, and you can catch the views of the Matterhorn from the train before you ever get off it. Zermatt is car-free, so the village itself is a pleasant stroll.

Day five, up Gornergrat by cogwheel train for the classic Matterhorn panorama and the viewpoint over the glacier — this is as low-effort as mountain scenery gets. Then the short train back down to Täsch for the connecting trains home.

Now the money question. I priced the main options against the 2026 fares, and here's the short version. The Swiss Travel Pass for four days in second class is three hundred nine francs. It covers every train, boat and local bus on this whole itinerary, plus fifty percent off the mountain tickets on Pilatus, Grindelwald First and Gornergrat — and notably only twenty-five percent off Jungfraujoch. A three-day pass at two hundred fifty-four would work if your travel days line up, but you'd be paying separately for arrival and departure day moves.

The alternative is the Half Fare Card at one hundred fifty francs, plus point-to-point tickets at half price. On this specific itinerary it comes out slightly cheaper — my rough estimate, since I couldn't verify the exact 2026 fare for the Interlaken to Zermatt leg, is that the three main train rides run about fifty to sixty francs after discount, and the mountain rides are the same fifty percent off as with the full pass, putting you in the mid-three-hundreds all in versus the pass at around three hundred ninety including mountains. The catch is more booking and every ticket is separate; the Travel Pass buys you the ease of just walking onto trains and boats all week.

My honest recommendation: if you like the simplicity, the four-day Travel Pass in second class. If you want to spend less and don't mind booking tickets as you go, the Half Fare Card — and it's clearly the better deal if you decide to add Jungfraujoch, since it halves that ticket instead of only cutting it by a quarter.
