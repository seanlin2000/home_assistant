Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: That relationship comes down to how most of the value of a growth company gets calculated. When analysts price a tech stock, they usually take the cash flow the business is expected to generate over the coming years and discount it back to today, and the rate they discount at is built around prevailing interest rates, especially short-term rates that the Federal Reserve sets.

The core idea is that future money is worth less than money today. A dollar you get five years from now is worth less than one you get next month, because you could have invested what you have in the meantime. The discount rate is basically the interest you are forgoing by waiting. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and every future cash flow gets knocked down a bit more when it is pulled back to the present. That mechanically lowers the present value, and therefore the fair value, of the company.

Now, the second part is why it hits tech hardest. A growth company's value is unusually front-loaded on the far future. Think of a young software company trading at a huge multiple. A meaningful share of what you pay is not for profits it makes this year, but for profits five, ten, even fifteen years out. Investors in mature, cash-rich companies, like utilities or consumer staples, get most of their returns from dividends and steady earnings right now, so they are much less sensitive to the discount rate. A high-growth tech stock is like a very long bond, which is why people talk about long duration. The longer the duration, the more sensitive the price is to a change in the discount rate.

There is a compounding effect on the far end. Discounting is not linear, it compounds. Raising the discount rate by even a point or two barely changes the value of cash flow arriving in a year, but it can shave a huge chunk off cash flow arriving in ten or fifteen years, because that rate has been applied repeatedly. So a company whose value lives mostly in those distant years loses value disproportionately. If a company's worth is a third in ten years and the discount rate climbs, that far-out third gets compressed the most.

There is also a second, more market-level channel. Higher rates make safe assets like Treasury bonds and savings accounts more attractive, so investors demand a higher return from risky stocks to hold them instead, which pushes the equity risk premium and the discount rate up again. At the same time, higher rates raise a company's own cost of borrowing, which can slow growth and hurt margins, and they tend to cool the broader economy, which can hurt demand. So you get the pure valuation effect, the competition with bonds, and real damage to earnings and growth expectations all working in the same direction at once.

One honest caveat: this explains the general, first-order pressure, not the whole story. Tech stocks can rise even when rates are being hiked if growth is accelerating faster than the discounting effect, and a company's own fundamentals and the market's mood can swamp the mathematical effect. But all else equal, the direction of the force is what I described: the Fed pushes the discount rate up, distant cash flows get knocked down the hardest, and the long-duration growth stocks that live mostly in those distant cash flows are the ones that get hit the most.
