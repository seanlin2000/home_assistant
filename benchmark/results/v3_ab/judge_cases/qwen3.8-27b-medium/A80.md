ASSISTANT: Quantization means replacing each parameter's high-precision floating-point number — typically 16-bit, so 65,000 possible values per weight — with a small integer that has far fewer levels, plus a few extra numbers that record the scaling range for a group of weights. A 4-bit quantized model stores each weight as one of just 16 values instead of 65,000. So you're not storing the original number; you're storing a coarse snapshot of it, and at inference the value gets scaled back up before use.

On memory, the math is simple: each parameter takes up as many bits as the quantization level. For 70 billion parameters, 16-bit precision would need about 140 gigabytes — that's why the unquantized model doesn't fit on a single consumer card. At 4-bit, 70 billion parameters times 4 bits divided by 8 gives roughly 35 gigabytes of weights. At 2-bit, half of that, so about 17.5 gigabytes. For comparison, 8-bit would be 70 gigabytes. Two caveats: those figures are weights only — you still need memory for the computation buffers and, at inference, the attention cache, which grows with how long a context you process. And quantized models carry a little extra bookkeeping, the scale factors, typically adding a few percent on top.

The trade-offs fall into a few buckets. First, accuracy loss: fewer levels means more rounding error per weight, and it compounds through the network. 4-bit with a good method like GPTQ or AWQ is usually within one to two percent of the original on most benchmarks — barely noticeable in casual use. 2-bit is where it starts to hurt; models can lose meaningful ability, produce garbled outputs on harder tasks, or fail outright on some tasks, which is why 2-bit is mostly for research or squeezing into very small memory. Second, not all weights are equally quantization-friendly — large outlier values in certain layers break naive rounding, so real methods do calibration on sample data, smooth the outliers, or keep sensitive layers like the embedding table at higher precision. Third, you lose flexibility: a quantized model has fixed precision, and some quantization formats are slower or only supported on certain hardware or backends. The upside, obviously, is that 4-bit brings a model that needs 140 gigabytes down to a 35-gigabyte card, and 2-bit down to a modest machine — which is the entire point of the exercise.
