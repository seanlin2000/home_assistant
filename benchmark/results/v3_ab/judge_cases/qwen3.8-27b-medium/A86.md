ASSISTANT: Here's the setup I'd recommend, and I'll be upfront that a couple of these details are based on how Hue has worked for years rather than something I just re-verified, so treat the voice-offline part especially carefully.

**Core architecture: everything routes through the bridge on your local network**

The bridge is the heart of the system. It runs Zigbee, the low-power radio your Hue bulbs actually use. The bulbs don't talk to the internet at all, and they don't need your Wi-Fi. They only talk to the bridge, and the bridge has an Ethernet cable to your router. So the bridge's local IP address is what every controller actually talks to.

The best structure for several people is:

Each person installs the Hue app on their own phone and connects it to the same bridge as a "user." Hue supports multiple users per bridge out of the box. In the app, the person who owns the account goes to settings, then user management, and adds the others. Each of you then gets your own view, your own favorite rooms, your own light scenes, while sharing the same physical lights. That's the clean multi-user story and it requires no extra hardware.

**Voice control**

The way to do voice is to connect Hue to your chosen assistant, typically through the Hue skills/integration in the Alexa, Google Home, or Apple Home apps, which log in through the Hue service and then command the lights via the bridge on your network. For multiple people, each person can just use their own assistant account on their own device; the commands still funnel through the same bridge. If you're using Apple Home specifically, HomeKit also gives you per-person access through the Home app's sharing feature.

**What happens when the internet goes down**

This is where it's genuinely good news, but with one caveat. Your lights and your local control basically keep working. The bulbs and bridge live entirely on your local network over Zigbee and LAN, so the Hue app on your phone (as long as it's on the same Wi-Fi, even over a cellular-less network) can still turn things on, off, and change scenes. Same for voice: Alexa and Google have an offline local mode for many smart devices through local voice processing, and Hue is one of the better-supported ones, so on-device "on" and "off" often still work without a connection.

The caveats, stated plainly: you still need your local Wi-Fi and your router powered, so this isn't a full power-outage solution, only an internet-outage one. And not every voice command is guaranteed to work offline. Basic on/off and simple room commands are the reliable ones. Complex phrasing, scenes you've named, and anything that has to hit Philips' cloud services may fail until the internet returns. I'd plan on "lights are controllable, fancy stuff might not be" rather than assuming full parity.
