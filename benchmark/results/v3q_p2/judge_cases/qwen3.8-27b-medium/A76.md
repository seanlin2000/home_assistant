# Exchange 1

USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that the Soviet Union didn't lose a war so much as it ran out of the ability to hold itself together, and the last eighteen months gave the process its speed. To answer your question properly, I'll separate the long causes from the triggering events, because that distinction is where most of the real story sits.

The causes were accumulated over two decades. First, the economy. By the 1970s Soviet growth had slowed to roughly one or two percent a year, the command economy could not adapt, and the state was spending a quarter of its budget on the military, much of it on an arms race it could not afford to sustain. Second, the war in Afghanistan, which from 1979 to 1989 bled the country in money, lives, and credibility. And third, the political system itself: a one-party state with no legitimate way to change course, in which bad news never traveled upward. When Gorbachev arrived in 1985 he diagnosed all of this and introduced perestroika and glasnost, economic restructuring and political openness, and those reforms are arguably the deepest cause of the end, because glasnost made it impossible to suppress the national movements in the Baltic states, Ukraine, and the republics, and perestroika's partial liberalization made the economy worse before it could make it better. So the causes were structural: an exhausted economy, an unmanageable political system, and the reforms meant to fix them.

The triggers were the events that turned a slow decline into a collapse. Three stand out.

The first, in November 1989, was the fall of the Berlin Wall and the chain of revolutions across Eastern Europe. The crucial trigger there was not a protest but a decision: Gorbachev refused to send troops, unlike the Soviet invasions of Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, and his deputy Shevardnadze called this the Sinatra Doctrine, doing it my way. Without the Eastern bloc, the Soviet Union lost the satellites, the strategic buffer, the legitimacy, and a large share of its oil and markets that the empire had depended on. A communist empire without its vassals was no longer believable even at home, and the national republics immediately began demanding their own version of freedom.

The second, in August 1991, was the failed coup against Gorbachev. A group of hardline ministers and security officials tried to seize power on the grounds that he was giving away the Union, and held Moscow for three days. Boris Yeltsin, then head of the Russian republic, stood on a tank outside the Russian parliament and outlasted them. The coup's failure was decisive for a subtle reason: it destroyed the last credibility of the central Soviet government. Gorbachev, the man who had tried to reform the system, was now politically dead, and Yeltsin's Russia became the effective center of power. The Baltic states and other republics responded within weeks by declaring full independence, and the Union was, in practical terms, already over.

The third, in December 1991, was the formal act of dissolution. In mid-December, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belovezh Accords, declaring the Soviet Union no longer to exist and replacing it with the Commonwealth of Independent States. Gorbachev, left out of it, resigned as president on December 25th, the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin, and the legal fiction of the Union was gone. Ukraine's later vote in a December referendum to stay independent sealed the point, because a Soviet Union without its second largest republic was a contradiction in terms.

So to draw the line you asked for: the causes were the two-decade economic stagnation, the cost of the arms race and Afghanistan, and the reform policies that released forces the system could not control. The triggers were the three moments when those accumulated pressures actually broke: the refusal to use force in 1989, the coup that killed the center's legitimacy in August 1991, and the formal partition of the state in December. The causes built the fracture over twenty years; the triggers, in the space of three years, turned a weakened empire into a series of independent countries.
