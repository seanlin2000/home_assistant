The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

ASSISTANT: Here's the architecture I'd recommend, in layers.

The core is the Hue Bridge itself. It's a Zigbee hub that lives on your local network, so the important thing is to plug it into your router with an Ethernet cable, and ideally reserve a fixed IP address for it in the router settings so DHCP renewals don't cause dropouts. All your bulbs and any Hue switches or sensors pair to the bridge over Zigbee. If the apartment is big or has thick walls, a powered Hue smart plug or sensor in another room works as a Zigbee repeater to keep the mesh reliable.

For multiple people, the simplest setup is: each person downloads the Hue app, creates or signs in to their own Hue account, and adds the same bridge by entering its code. That gives everyone full local app control on their own phone without sharing credentials, and scenes and rooms stay in sync because they live on the bridge. For voice, pick one assistant ecosystem rather than mixing several. If you go with Alexa or Google, each person ideally has their own speaker and links Hue under their own account. If you go with Apple Home, the bridge shows up in the Home app, and each person can have a HomePod or use Siri on their own phone, with shared access via Home sharing.

Now the interesting part is what happens when the internet goes down. The bridge and Zigbee side never needs the internet. The Hue app on your local Wi-Fi still controls the lights, Hue wall switches, motion sensors, and any scenes or automations stored on the bridge keep firing — a motion sensor turning on a light at night works fine in a blackout. What dies is anything that routes through the cloud: remote access from outside the apartment, and with Alexa and Google, voice commands, because their Hue integration talks to the bridge through their cloud services, so cloud voice control stops until the internet returns.

That last point is the main architecture decision. If offline voice control matters to you, Apple Home is the most resilient choice: HomeKit talks to the bridge over your local network, and a HomePod mini can run Siri locally, so voice commands keep working with no internet. Alexa and Google lose voice control in an outage, even though the lights themselves stay fully controllable from the app and Hue accessories.

So my summary: bridge on Ethernet with a reserved IP, everyone on their own Hue account, and pick your voice assistant around how much you care about outages — HomeKit plus HomePod if offline voice matters, Alexa or Google if you only need it when the internet is up. One practical tip: when you do lose power to the internet, the lights keep working, but don't be alarmed if the app says things look weird until the router comes back; give the bridge a minute after power returns before assuming anything is broken.
