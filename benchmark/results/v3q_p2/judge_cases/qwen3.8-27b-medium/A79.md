Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

ASSISTANT: Good question, and one worth unpacking in layers.

Why GPUs are so effective at AI inference. At the core, inference is mostly enormous matrix multiplication, plus a flood of memory traffic. A GPU is thousands of small arithmetic units that can execute exactly that kind of work in parallel, and modern data-center GPUs like NVIDIA's Blackwell and the Rubin chips shipping now pair that compute with huge high-bandwidth memory — roughly two hundred to three hundred gigabytes per chip at eight to twenty-two terabytes per second. Inference has two phases that stress different things: prefill, where the whole prompt is processed at once and compute matters most, and decode, where tokens come out one at a time and memory bandwidth is the bottleneck. GPUs handle both reasonably well, and on top of that they come with CUDA, a software ecosystem so deep that most of the world's AI code already runs on them. That generality is the real superpower: the same hardware works for training, research, new model architectures, and serving, with no redesign needed.

Now the differences. A GPU is a general-purpose parallel machine — flexible, but paying for that flexibility with wasted transistors and some inefficiency. An ASIC is a chip designed for one narrow job. Google's TPUs and Amazon's Trainium are the classic examples: they're purpose-built for tensor math, so for the workloads they're designed around they can be more efficient per dollar and per watt, but they're much less flexible, they typically lock you into one cloud, and their software stacks are younger. Then there's a third camp: specialized inference processors like Cerebras, which builds a processor the size of a whole silicon wafer with a massive on-chip memory, or Groq, which pipes inference through a fully deterministic architecture to stream tokens at very low latency. Note that this line is blurring right now — NVIDIA actually licensed Groq's inference technology in late 2025, and the biggest buyers, like Anthropic, run frontier workloads across NVIDIA GPUs, TPUs, and Trainium simultaneously rather than betting on one.

So when can a specialized chip beat an NVIDIA GPU? A few circumstances. First, when your workload is stable and high-volume — you're serving one or a few known models, millions of requests a day, and you can tune a chip specifically for them. At that scale, cost per token and power efficiency can matter more than flexibility, and that's where custom silicon tends to win. Second, when the workload is heavily memory-bandwidth-bound, like streaming out long responses. Chips with big on-chip memory or near-memory compute — Cerebras, Groq, or the new Google TPU 8i with its large on-die SRAM — can outperform GPUs there even with less raw compute. Third, when extreme low-latency or deterministic streaming is the product itself, which is Groq and Cerebras' niche. Fourth, at cluster scale, interconnect matters enormously: Google's TPU pods link more than nine thousand chips with their own fast fabric, versus NVIDIA's seventy-two GPU rack today, which matters when a model has to span many chips. And fifth, simple economics: if you're locked into one cloud and the hourly price is much lower, the answer can be the price tag.

The flip side is why GPUs remain the default. If your models change often, you do research, you train frontier models, or you want to move workloads between providers, the flexibility and software ecosystem of NVIDIA hardware are worth the premium. The honest summary is that no single chip wins every metric in 2026 — it depends on whether you're measuring compute, bandwidth, price, portability, or cluster scale, and whether your workload is fixed or fluid.
