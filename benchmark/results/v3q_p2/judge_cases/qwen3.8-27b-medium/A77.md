Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short version: sunlight actually contains all the colors of the rainbow mixed together, and the air in our atmosphere is good at scattering the shorter, bluer colors away from their original path.

During the day, when the sun is high overhead, its light only has to punch through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere to reach your eyes. Blue light scatters easily, so it bounces off and gets spread across the whole sky by air molecules. That's why when you look up, no matter which direction you face, you see blue everywhere. The red and orange wavelengths mostly pass straight through with less scattering.

At sunset, the sun is right at the horizon, and its light has to travel a much longer path through the atmosphere to reach you. Over that extra distance, nearly all the blue light has already been scattered out of the way before it gets to your eyes. What's left is the longer wavelengths, which are red and orange, so that's what you see streaming in from the sun.

It's like a filter that keeps getting thicker the lower the sun sits. And the occasional extra drama at sunset, when you get deep reds, happens when there's more dust, smoke, or moisture in the air to scatter even more of the blue away.

One fun detail worth knowing: the moon and planets look dimmer and a little redder when they're low in the sky for exactly the same reason. The light has traveled through more air, and more of the blue got scattered away.
