Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is really about how stock prices are calculated in the first place. The core idea is that a stock's fair value is the present value of the cash it will generate in the future, and every dollar a company earns in the future is worth less than a dollar today, by an amount that depends on the discount rate. The Fed's policy rate doesn't set that discount rate directly, but moving rates up pushes the whole yield curve higher, including the long-term Treasury yields that investors use as their baseline for what they demand to be paid to hold a risky asset. So when rates rise, the math gets harsher on future cash flows.

Now here's why that hits tech hardest. Growth and tech companies are what people call long-duration assets. That's not about bonds, though the concept borrows the word. It means a large share of their value is expected to come from cash flows far in the future. A utility or a mature consumer goods company might pay you real cash this year. But a lot of the value in a high multiple tech stock is cash it might generate in five, ten, or fifteen years. The further out a cash flow is, the more it gets shrunk by discounting, because you compound that discount rate over more years. A discount rate going from, say, four percent to five percent barely changes the present value of next year's earnings, but it noticeably reduces the present value of earnings ten or fifteen years out. So a company whose value is mostly in the distant future loses more value when rates rise, all else equal.

There are a few reinforcing channels on top of that. First, valuation multiples compress. High rate environments make investors less willing to pay fifty or sixty times earnings for a company whose profits are still ahead of them, so you get both the discount rate effect and a multiple contraction happening together. Second, the cost of capital rises, which directly makes tech companies' own future investment projects less attractive and raises the hurdle for growth. Third, it's a risk appetite effect. Tech growth stocks are a classic place for speculative, growth-oriented money, and when risk free yields are low, that money chases higher risk assets, but when safe bonds are paying decent returns, a meaningful amount of that capital rotates back toward bonds and slower growing value stocks.

One nuance worth being honest about. This relationship is strongest at the margin and depends on what the rate move signals. If rates rise because the economy is booming and company earnings are rising fast, tech stocks can still go up. The classic painful episodes, like 2022, were when rates rose sharply and simultaneously the market realized growth companies were burning cash they could no longer raise cheaply, so all three of those channels hit at once. But the mechanics I described, the discount rate applied to distant cash flows, is the structural reason tech valuations are more rate sensitive than most of the market.
