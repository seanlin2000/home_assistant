Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Here's the idea in plain terms.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet — all mixed together. White is what your eyes see when all those colors arrive at once.

Now, sunlight has to pass through Earth's atmosphere, which is full of tiny gas molecules — mostly nitrogen and oxygen. When light hits those tiny molecules, it gets bounced around in every direction. Physicists call this scattering, and it's the whole story behind sky color.

Here's the key detail: the bounce isn't equal for all colors. Blue and violet light have short, choppy waves that bump into those gas molecules easily and get scattered all over the sky. Red and orange light have longer, smoother waves that mostly sail right through without getting knocked off course. You can picture it like this — imagine a bowling ball rolling through a field of dandelions. The ball (red light) plows straight through without much disturbance. A ping-pong ball (blue light) gets knocked every which way by the same dandelions.

So during the day, when the sun is high overhead, you're looking up through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere. Sunlight hits the gas molecules a little way up, the blue wavelengths scatter in all directions — including toward your eyes — and the whole sky glows blue. The blue gets spread across the dome of the sky like a glow-in-the-dark paint, and that's what you see everywhere you look.

At sunset, everything changes because of the geometry. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a much, much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach your eyes — maybe ten times more air than when the sun is overhead. On that long journey, the blue light gets knocked around so many times that it scatters out of the beam entirely before it can reach you. What survives the long trek is mostly red and orange light, the stubborn long-wave stuff that keeps plowing forward. That's why the sun itself, and the clouds and haze near it, look deep orange or red.

The reason sunsets get so dramatic is that the remaining colors paint the underside of clouds and dust particles at different distances, giving you bands of yellow, orange, and crimson. And sometimes after sunset you can even see a brief green flash on the horizon — the very last sliver of green light peeking over the edge of the Earth just before it, too, is scattered away.

The whole explanation comes down to one simple fact: the atmosphere filters sunlight by wavelength. Short waves scatter easily and paint the sky blue; long waves push through. The angle of the sun just decides how much air the light has to cross, which decides which colors make it to your eyes.
