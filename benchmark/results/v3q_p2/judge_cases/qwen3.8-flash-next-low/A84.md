Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock is worth the present value of every dollar it will ever earn, and the interest rate is the discount you apply to get from those future dollars to today's dollars. Raise the rate and every future dollar is worth less right now. The catch — and this is the whole story for tech — is that the penalty is much bigger the further out the dollar sits.

Here's the arithmetic. Suppose a company promises to pay you a hundred dollars, once, at some point in the future, and you discount it at a required return of three percent. Wait two years and that promise is worth about ninety-four dollars today. Wait ten years and it's worth about seventy-four. Now raise the discount rate to seven percent. The two-year promise falls to about eighty-seven dollars, a hit of roughly seven percent. The ten-year promise falls to about fifty-one dollars, a hit of about thirty-one percent. Same rate increase, four times the damage, purely because the cash is further away.

That's why the effect lands hardest on high-growth technology names. A mature business like a utility or a consumer staples company earns a fairly steady stream of cash starting almost immediately, so most of its value sits in the near-term part of the cash flow stream, the part that's barely sensitive to the discount rate. A hypergrowth software or biotech company, by contrast, may burn cash for years and expect most of its profit to arrive in the second half of the decade, sometimes beyond any explicit forecast period at all. Its valuation is then dominated by a terminal value — an estimate of everything after the forecast horizon — and that single far-off number is the most rate-sensitive asset on the page. Tech is what bond investors call a long-duration equity, the equity equivalent of a thirty-year bond, and long-duration assets fall the most when yields rise.

A few amplifiers make it worse than the pure discounting math suggests. First, the discount rate used in the models is built off the risk-free rate, so when the Fed tightens and Treasury yields follow, the whole required-return hurdle moves up, and equity investors typically demand an extra premium on top because a higher rate also brings a greater chance of a recession that hits discretionary tech spending. Second, the cost of capital matters on the income statement, not just in the model: unprofitable companies funding themselves with debt or with successive rounds of venture and growth equity find that money more expensive and harder to raise, which slows the very growth the valuation assumed. Third, higher yields make the safe alternative genuinely competitive. A five percent Treasury with a guaranteed return is a credible substitute for an unprofitable growth story, and as cash and short bonds pay more, money rotates out of the most speculative positions. Finally, the rates that hurt valuations often correlate with the stronger dollar and tighter financial conditions that slow the cloud, advertising and hardware demand those companies sell into, so the discount rate rises at the same time the earnings estimate gets revised down.

Worth saying, though, that the relationship is a tendency, not a law. A rate rise caused by an unexpectedly strong economy can come alongside rising earnings forecasts, and companies that are already profitable and cash-generating — the large, mature platform businesses — are far less exposed than the unprofitable long-duration names. That's exactly the divergence you saw in 2022, when the sharp tightening cycle cut deep into unprofitable growth and small-cap tech while the cash-rich mega-caps, though they fell, fell much less. What's actually the case now, as of today, is a somewhat gentler setting than the 2022 comparison implies: the Fed's target range is three and three-quarters to four percent, and it left it unchanged at its October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth meeting after a twenty-five basis point increase in September, which most observers read as a pause in tightening rather than a new leg up.

One practical implication: because valuations of growth stocks are this sensitive to a number that moves with inflation prints and Fed communication, they get repriced on expectations, not on the policy itself. The stock moves when the market's path for rates moves, which is why a single hot inflation report can knock ten percent off a growth index before the Fed ever votes.
