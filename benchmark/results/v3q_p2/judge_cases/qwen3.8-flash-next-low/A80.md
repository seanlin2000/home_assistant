Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Quantizing stores each weight in fewer bits than the usual 16. Weights alone: 16-bit about 140 GB; 4-bit 70 billion times half a byte, about 35 GB (real 4-bit files run roughly 38 to 42 GB because of scales and mixed precision); 2-bit about 17.5 GB (about 20 to 25 GB in practice). The context cache and activations come on top. Trade-offs: some loss of quality, small at 4-bit and marked at 2-bit (reasoning and factual accuracy suffer most); faster generation, because inference is limited by memory bandwidth; careful methods (GPTQ, AWQ, importance-weighted quantization) reduce the loss. Wrong memory figures are an error.

View PDF HTML (experimental) Abstract:Increasing the number of parameters in large language models (LLMs) usually improves performance in downstream tasks but raises compute and memory costs, making deployment difficult in resource-limited settings. Quantization techniques, which reduce the bits needed for model weights or activations with minimal performance loss, have become popular due to the rise of LLMs. However, most quantization studies use pre-trained LLMs, and the impact of quantization on instruction-tuned LLMs and the relationship between perplexity and benchmark performance of quantized LLMs are not well understood. Evaluation of quantized LLMs is often limited to language modeling and a few classification tasks, leaving their performance on other benchmarks unclear. To address these gaps, we propose a structured evaluation framework consisting of three critical dimensions: (1) knowledge \& capacity, (2) alignment, and (3) efficiency, and conduct extensive experiments across ten diverse benchmarks. ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means replacing its weights, normally stored as 16-bit floating point numbers, with numbers that take far fewer bits. Instead of a continuous range of values, each weight gets snapped to the nearest entry in a small fixed set. A 4-bit weight can represent only 16 distinct levels; a 2-bit weight only four. Since the values in a layer aren't all the same size, quantizers divide the weights into groups, usually a hundred and twenty-eight weights per group, and store a scale and an offset for each group so the tiny set of levels can be stretched to fit that group's range. At load time the weights are dequantized back to higher precision for the actual arithmetic, which is why the activations and the key-value cache usually stay in 16 bits even when the weights don't.

Here's the memory math for seventy billion parameters. At 16-bit precision that's a hundred and forty billion bytes, about 130 gibibytes just for the weights. At 4-bit it's half that: thirty-five billion bytes, about 32.6 gibibytes. At 2-bit it's a quarter of the original: seventeen and a half billion bytes, about 16.3 gibibytes. Those are clean theoretical figures. In practice the group scales and offsets add a few percent, so a typical 4-bit file for a 70-billion model lands somewhere in the high thirties of gigabytes on disk, and you need room on top of that for the key-value cache and the framework's own overhead. One 2026 benchmark roundup put a 4-bit AWQ 70B model at roughly 38 gigabytes.

The trade-offs are asymmetric, and that's the important part. Four-bit is widely described in the literature as the sweet spot: measured perplexity typically rises by one to three percent versus the 16-bit baseline, and on benchmark suites 4-bit models often perform comparably to the unquantized original. Eight-bit is essentially indistinguishable. Going below four bits is where things get ugly. Three bits shows real regression, on the order of five percent perplexity and a noticeably larger drop on reasoning benchmarks. Two bits is commonly called a performance cliff. Plain 2-bit GPTQ on a 70-billion model can send perplexity soaring into the tens of thousands and produce incoherent text; more careful 2-bit schemes like GGUF's Q2_K land around eleven to fourteen percent worse perplexity, with something like a fourteen percent absolute drop in grade-school math accuracy. A 2026 ACL paper argues the failure modes are genuinely different at the two levels: at four bits you get signal degradation, where the model's computation still works but precision is smeared, while at two bits you can get computation collapse, where components in the early layers stop functioning and no amount of compensation fixes it.

Two more things worth knowing. Reasoning, coding and tool-use tasks degrade faster than average perplexity suggests, so a model that looks fine on perplexity can still fall apart on multi-step math; the practical advice that comes out of the benchmarks is not to go below 4-bit for agent work. And quantization doesn't automatically make generation faster. Weight-only quantization cuts the bytes you have to read from memory, which helps because generation is memory-bandwidth-bound, so 4-bit gave roughly a nearly four-fold speedup in one M5 Max comparison. But if the software's dequantization kernels aren't well optimized, the extra work of unpacking odd bit-widths can make inference slower than the full-precision model, and the arXiv evaluation paper flagged exactly that risk. Specialized formats with hardware support, and methods that allocate more bits to the layers that need them, exist largely to fight that trade-off.

So the short version: 4-bit roughly quarters the memory for a modest, often tolerable quality cost. 2-bit quarters it again but crosses into territory where models frequently become markedly worse, sometimes unusable, and where the savings stop buying you speed.
