ASSISTANT: Quick correction first, because it changes the whole question: the Fed did not cut rates to zero last month. At its October 28 meeting the FOMC held the federal funds target range at 3.75 to 4 percent, by a 10-to-2 vote, and the two dissenters actually wanted a quarter-point increase. In fact the last change the Fed made was the opposite of a cut — it raised rates by 25 basis points in September 2026, after holding steady through the first half of the year.

Mortgage rates reflect that. As of this week, surveys put the average 30-year fixed refinance rate somewhere in the low-to-mid 7 percent range, roughly 7.16 to 7.45 percent depending on whose survey you read, and 15-year rates are around 6.5 percent. Those numbers have been ticking up, not down, in recent days, and Fed funds futures are pricing a fairly high chance of another hike at the December 9 meeting. So this is not a refinancing window driven by cheap money from the central bank.

That said, the Fed's rate is not what decides whether a refinance is worth it. What matters is your existing rate versus today's, your remaining balance, how many years are left, and your closing costs, since the fed funds rate mostly pushes mortgage rates through longer-term expectations rather than setting them directly.

So, to give you a real answer: what rate are you paying now, roughly how much is left on the loan, and how long have you been in the house? With those three things I can tell you whether refinancing at around 7 percent would actually save you money, and if so how long it would take to earn back the closing costs.
