Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that inference, especially the part where a model generates a token at a time, is not really a math problem so much as a plumbing problem, and the specialized chips are the ones trying to fix the plumbing rather than add more arithmetic.

Start with why GPUs work at all. A transformer forward pass is almost entirely a sequence of very large matrix multiplications and a handful of elementwise operations. That is the ideal workload for a GPU: thousands of simple arithmetic units doing the same thing to different data at once, no branches, no control flow, and tensor cores built specifically to chew through 16-bit and 8-bit matrix products. Modern transformer layers are also naturally parallel along the width dimension, so you can slice one matrix product across many processors and stitch the result back together. What a GPU does not have, in proportion, is memory bandwidth. An H100 or H200 has enormous flops, but its HBM delivers single-digit terabytes per second, and during autoregressive decoding that bandwidth is the bottleneck: to produce one token, the chip must read essentially every weight in the model once, while the number of multiply-accumulate operations per byte read is low. So at small batch sizes a GPU is idle on compute and starved on data movement, and a lot of its silicon, its rasterization and texture hardware, its wide instruction issue, its warp schedulers, its caching hierarchy, is doing nothing useful for the task. That gap is the opening every competitor attacks.

An ASIC narrows the chip down to the operation it is meant to perform. Attention and feed-forward matrices get wired into the datapath, the schedule for moving data is decided at compile time instead of by hardware schedulers at run time, and there is no general instruction pipeline to feed. Etched is the purest example: a transformer-only ASIC, with its Sohu-2 reportedly taped out in April this year, claiming on the order of five hundred thousand tokens per second on a 70 billion parameter Llama model per eight-chip server. Those numbers come from the company, and as far as I can tell there is no independent public benchmark confirming them, so treat them as vendor claims.

Specialized inference processors sit in between: still programmable, but with the architecture bent hard toward this workload. Google's TPUs use systolic arrays and a compiler-first stack. AWS Trainium and Inferentia are optimized for their own serving stack, with Trainium3 reportedly shipping around 2.5 petaflops of FP8 and 144 gigabytes of HBM3e per chip, and Amazon running a very large Trainium deployment for Anthropic. Groq's LPU takes the most radical approach: no external high-bandwidth memory at all, everything in on-chip SRAM, with the compiler scheduling every cycle so execution is deterministic. On-chip SRAM gives roughly an order of magnitude more aggregate bandwidth than HBM, which is precisely what decode needs, and the tradeoff is capacity. A Groq-class chip cannot hold a frontier model's weights comfortably, so it needs many chips to hold one model and it cannot do training. Cerebras goes the other way, with a wafer-scale engine carrying trillions of transistors and a great deal of on-chip memory to avoid chip-to-chip hops. SambaNova and Graphcore offer reconfigurable dataflow designs, and Graphcore is a cautionary tale about the software problem, with reported revenue around fifty million dollars in 2025 against NVIDIA's hundred-thirty billion or more in data center sales.

So when does a specialized chip actually win? When the workload is a dense, standard transformer that the chip was designed for, and the architecture is not going to change during the chip's useful life. When the metric that matters is time to first token and inter-token latency at modest batch, because an agent loop, a voice assistant, or a coding autocomplete is latency-bound and the deterministic streaming design removes jitter and scheduling overhead. When you are serving enormous, steady volumes of that same model and unit economics dominate, since you are not paying for graphics hardware, general-purpose flexibility, or a training pipeline you do not use on that silicon, and the cost per token can drop sharply. And when you do not need on the same box to fine-tune, run multimodal or mixture-of-experts variants, or re-plan the serving stack every few months when a new attention scheme arrives.

A specialized chip loses when the model outgrows its memory, when the architecture shifts faster than the silicon can be respun, when the software stack means months of porting rather than an afternoon, or when your traffic is spiky and mixed enough that flexibility is worth its premium. Notably, NVIDIA has conceded the point: multiple outlets report that it paid roughly twenty billion dollars to license Groq's LPU technology late last year, and at GTC this year unveiled a Groq 3 LPU positioned as a decode co-processor alongside Rubin GPUs, with the GPUs handling the compute-heavy prefill and the LPU handling the bandwidth-bound generation. That is the clearest signal that inference and training have become genuinely different hardware markets, with inference now estimated by analysts at around sixty to seventy percent of accelerator spending.
