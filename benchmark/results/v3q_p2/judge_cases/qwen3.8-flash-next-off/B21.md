## Analysis: why the Fed’s rate matters beyond Wall Street
This section is Peter’s World’s view, based on the official figures above.
The dollar and allies. A Fed that raises rates while other central banks hold tends to support the dollar. That makes U.S. imports cheaper but raises the cost of dollar debt for emerging economies, including several on the front lines of current conflicts.
Trade. The Fed’s June and July statements pointed to “supply shocks” in energy and other sectors. Tariffs can add to the same price pressure. Higher rates cannot fix a supply shock, so trade policy and monetary policy now pull on the same inflation number from different directions.
Defense budgets. Higher rates raise the interest cost of federal debt, which competes with defense and other spending in every budget cycle. A higher-for-longer path, as the September projections suggest, would keep that pressure on.
Markets. Mortgage, auto and credit card rates key off expectations for the fed funds rate. The vote count and statement wording often matter as much as the decision itself, because they signal the next move.

The Fed did not cut rates to zero last month. On October 28 it held the federal funds target range at 3.75 to 4 percent, and that hold came right after a quarter-point *increase* on September 16 — the first hike in this cycle, with two governors dissenting because they wanted to raise rates again. If anything, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said earlier this month that he plans to support at least another quarter-point hike at the December 8 to 9 meeting. So the direction of policy is tightening, not easing.

Mortgage rates reflect that. As of this week, the national average on a 30-year fixed is somewhere in the neighborhood of 7 percent — sources ranged from about 7.05 to 7.23 percent for purchase loans, and LendingTree put refinance averages a bit higher, around 7.1 to 7.4 percent. Rates actually ticked *up* after last week's CPI report. So there's no rate-drop tailwind pushing you toward refinancing right now.

The rule that actually decides this has nothing to do with the Fed headline: refinance when your existing rate is meaningfully above today's market rate, and when the monthly savings pays back your closing costs before you sell or move. Closing costs typically run two to three percent of the loan balance, so a short remaining stay in the home can wipe out the benefit even when the rate spread looks attractive.

To give you a real number rather than general advice, I'd need three things: the rate you're paying now, roughly how much you owe, and how many more years you plan to stay in the home. Give me those and I'll work out your monthly savings, total interest saved, and how many months it takes to break even on the closing costs.

One thing worth flagging either way: with officials' September projections showing a median fed funds path around 4.1 percent for this year and only slowly drifting down after that, plus inflation still running above target at 3.7 percent in the CPI, betting on a big mortgage-rate drop in the next year or two looks like a bet against what the Fed itself is signaling. If your current rate is well below 7 percent, refinancing today probably makes you worse off.
