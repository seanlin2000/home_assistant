Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

## Why Interest Rates Matter So Much for Tech Stocks
### The Discounted Cash Flow Problem
Most professional analysts value growth companies using a discounted cash flow model, or DCF. You project future profits, then discount them using a rate tied to the risk-free Treasury yield plus a risk premium.
When the Fed hikes rates, that risk-free yield climbs. The discount rate climbs with it. Future profits — especially ones ten years out — get chopped down hard in present-value terms.
Aswath Damodaran’s valuation research at NYU Stern is one of the clearest public resources showing exactly how discount rate shifts compress or expand valuations across sectors [1]. Tech, loaded with far-out growth assumptions, feels this more than almost any other industry.
### Growth Stocks vs Value Stocks During Rate Hikes
Not all stocks bleed equally. Growth stocks — think unprofitable SaaS companies or early-stage AI plays — lean almost entirely on distant future earnings. Value stocks, like a dividend-paying hardware giant, generate real cash now.
When rates climb, growth names typically underperform value names. It’s not politics or sentiment. It’s just where the cash flow sits on the timeline.

## Why Growth Stocks Feel It Harder
A growth company’s valuation leans heavily on profit that’s expected years from now, think an early-stage tech or biotech name burning cash today on the promise of a much bigger business later. A value company’s valuation leans on cash it’s already earning this year, think a utility or an established bank, priced mostly off current, near-term profit. Raise the discount rate, and the growth company’s far-future profits shrink far more in today’s-dollar terms than the value company’s near-term profits do, for the exact same rate change, the same mechanism as bond duration, where a longer-dated bond swings harder on a rate move than a short-dated one.
Worked intuition: if rates push the discount rate up by 2 percentage points, a dollar of profit expected next year barely changes in present value. A dollar of profit expected in 15 years, discounted at 2 points higher for all 15 of those years, loses a meaningfully larger share of its today’s-value, compounding the way interest does, just working in the direction that shrinks the number instead of growing it. That compounding gap is why growth stocks, as a group, tend to sell off harder than value stocks when rates rise quickly, and why they tend to rally harder when rates fall.

ASSISTANT: The core of it is arithmetic, not sentiment, and it comes down to one idea: a stock's value today is the sum of all the cash it will produce in the future, discounted back to today's dollars at some required rate of return. That discount rate starts with the risk-free Treasury yield and adds a premium for risk. When the Fed hikes, short rates rise, Treasury yields rise, and the whole discount rate rises with them.

Here's why that hits tech harder than other sectors. A mature bank or utility earns most of its profit right now, so most of its value sits in cash flows arriving next year or the year after. Discounting a dollar you'll get in one year at 8 percent instead of 6 percent barely changes anything. But a high-growth software company is deliberately spending today's profits on growth, which means a big share of its value lives in earnings projected ten or fifteen years out. That same discount rate now compounds against that dollar for fifteen straight years, and the shrinkage is severe. Analysts call this "equity duration," by direct analogy to bonds: a thirty-year bond falls far more than a two-year bond on the same rate move, and a growth stock with most of its value in distant cash flows behaves like the thirty-year bond.

Two more things make tech especially exposed. First, the alternative investment gets better. When Treasury yields are near zero, investors accept low expected returns from stocks because there's nothing else to buy. When they're higher, a genuinely risk-free return competes for the same money, and the risk premium investors demand from equities rises too, pushing the discount rate up further than the Fed's own move. Second, cheap money is itself a business input: unprofitable growth companies fund themselves with debt and venture capital priced off those same rates, so hikes raise their cost of capital, slow their expansion, and shrink the earnings stream they're valued on at the same time valuation multiples compress.

The 2022 episode is the clean illustration. As the Fed hiked aggressively, the Nasdaq Composite fell about 33 percent while the S&P 500 fell about 19 percent, and the ARK Innovation ETF, a proxy for high-multiple, often unprofitable growth tech, dropped roughly two-thirds. The companies' businesses weren't collapsing at that rate; their multiples were being repriced. Profitable giants with enormous current free cash flow, Microsoft being the standard example, fell much less because near-term earnings anchor the valuation.

One important caveat, and it matters for interpreting any given rate move: rates rise for a reason. If yields climb because the economy is genuinely strong, corporate earnings often rise enough to offset the higher discount rate, and stocks can absorb it fine. The damage is worst when rates rise because inflation is stubborn and the Fed has to squeeze demand, since then both the discount rate goes up and the earnings it's discounting go down. Analysts also generally watch real, inflation-adjusted yields and the ten-year Treasury rather than the fed funds target itself, because long-term yields are what actually discount distant cash flows.
