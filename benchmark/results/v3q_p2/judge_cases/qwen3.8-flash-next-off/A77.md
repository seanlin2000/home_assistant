Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and the air in our atmosphere sorts those colors out by scattering them around.

Here's how that works. Sunlight travels through space and reaches Earth, then has to pass through the atmosphere — a blanket of gas, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. When light hits those tiny gas molecules, some of it gets knocked off course and flung in a new direction. That's called scattering. And here's the key part: not all colors scatter equally. Blue light has short, choppy waves that get bounced around very easily. Red light has long, smooth waves that mostly just punch straight through without bothering much.

So during the day, when you look up at the sky away from the sun, you're seeing all that scattered blue light raining down on you from every direction. The blue gets flung around and spread across the whole sky, which is why the sky looks blue rather than white. It's a little surprising that it isn't violet, since violet scatters even more strongly than blue, but our eyes are much less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up in the atmosphere, so what we perceive is blue.

Now for sunset. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach your eyes — dozens of times more air than when the sun is overhead. Along that long path, the blue and violet light gets scattered away so thoroughly that it never makes it to you in a straight line. What survives the trip are the longer wavelengths: reds, oranges, and yellows. So the sun itself, and the clouds near it, look orange or deep red.

There's one more piece that makes sunsets especially vivid: dust, smoke, pollution, and tiny water droplets in the air. These bigger particles scatter all colors more evenly, so they add a soft glow and can deepen the reds and oranges. That's why sunsets after a big wildfire or a volcanic eruption are famously spectacular — there's simply more stuff in the air to bounce light around.

And the same physics explains a couple of other everyday things. The sky looks pale whitish near the horizon because the scattered light gets mixed and dimmed by the extra haze you're looking through. And the sky at night goes dark because that part of Earth is turned away from the sun, so there's no sunlight left to scatter.
