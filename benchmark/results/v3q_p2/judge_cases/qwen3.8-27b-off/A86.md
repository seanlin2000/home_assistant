The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

# Exchange 1

USER: I have Philips Hue lights connected to a Hue Bridge. I want multiple people in my apartment to control the lights, including through voice commands. What is the best architecture for this, and what happens if the internet goes down?

ASSISTANT: The short version: you don't need any new hardware, but you do need to set up a couple of accounts correctly so everyone can get in, and then decide how much you want to rely on the cloud.

Here's the architecture that works well for your situation.

First, the foundation is the Hue Bridge itself, which is a local hub that talks Zigbee to your bulbs over radio and Ethernet to your network. Every Philips Hue app connects to that bridge, so the simplest way to let multiple people control the lights is just to have each of them install the official Hue app on their phone and pair it with your same bridge. That gives every person full local control from their phone without any extra setup.

For voice, you have two layers. The most reliable option is a smart speaker or display sitting in the apartment connected to the same Wi-Fi, running either Alexa or Google Assistant, with the Hue skill linked to your Philips account. Because that device talks to the bridge directly over your local network, "turn off the living room lights" works even if the internet goes down. That's the key architectural point: keep a local voice endpoint in the apartment rather than depending on calling out to Amazon or Google from each person's phone.

If you also want voice commands from people's phones, that goes through the cloud. When someone says "Alexa, turn off the lights" from their phone outside the house, the command is processed by Amazon's servers and then routed back to your bridge over the internet. That works fine when connectivity is up, but it depends on the internet in both directions.

Now, what happens if the internet goes down. The bridge and bulbs keep working with each other locally, so any control that stays inside your apartment keeps functioning: the Hue app on a phone connected to your Wi-Fi still works, automations and schedules stored on the bridge still run, and a local smart speaker's voice commands still work. What stops is anything that needs the cloud: remote control from outside the house, voice commands from phones, firmware updates, and any third-party integrations that route through the internet like IFTTT or home automation platforms hosted in the cloud.

A couple of practical notes. Make sure everyone's Hue app account is properly added as a user on the bridge rather than just sharing one login, because Philips lets you have multiple users per bridge and it keeps permissions cleaner. And if you want fancier multi-room automations beyond what the built-in Hue app does, the common move is to run Home Assistant locally on something like a Raspberry Pi in the apartment; that gives everyone local control plus voice through a local assistant, and it stays up during internet outages too. But for most people, multiple Hue apps plus one or two local smart speakers covers everything you described without extra hardware.
