Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: That's one of those questions where the answer feels almost too simple once you hear it, but it all comes down to how sunlight interacts with the air around us.

So here's the setup: what looks like white sunlight is actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow bundled together. Each color has a different wavelength — blue and violet are short and wiggle quickly, while red and orange are long and lazy. When that light hits the molecules in our atmosphere, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, it scatters around in every direction. And here's the key: the shorter the wavelength, the more it scatters. Blue light gets bounced all over the sky about ten times more than red light does. So when you look up at any patch of daytime sky that isn't the sun itself, you're mostly seeing blue light that got scattered into your eyes from every direction. That's why the sky is blue.

You might wonder, "Wait, violet has an even shorter wavelength than blue — shouldn't the sky be violet?" And that's a fair question! Two things happen: our sun actually emits less violet light to begin with, and more importantly, our eyes are much less sensitive to violet than they are to blue. So we perceive the mix as blue.

Now for sunset. During the day, the sun is high overhead, so its light only has to punch through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere to reach you. But at sunrise or sunset, the sun is right on the horizon, and its light has to travel through many times more air — think of it as the long diagonal route compared to the short straight-down route. Along that long journey, all the blue and violet light gets scattered away completely, stripped off before it can reach your eyes. What's left to make it through is the red and orange stuff, which scatters less and survives the trip. That's why the sun itself looks deep orange or red at the horizon, and why the sky around it glows in those warm colors.

There's a little bonus effect too: clouds, dust, and even volcanic ash can make sunsets especially vivid, because they scatter the remaining light even more dramatically. That's why a clear, crisp day after a storm often gives you the most spectacular sunset of your life.

So in short: blue sky is scattered short-wavelength light filling the dome above you, and red sunset is what's left over after the long trip through the atmosphere has filtered everything else out.
