Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core mechanism is that a company's value is fundamentally the present value of all the cash it will generate in the future, and when you calculate that present value, you use the prevailing interest rate as your discount rate. Think of the interest rate as the price of time itself: the higher it is, the less a dollar received next year or next decade is worth today compared to a dollar in your hand right now.

When the Fed raises rates, two things happen simultaneously that hit tech stocks hard. First, the mathematical effect on long-duration assets. Technology companies, especially high-growth ones, derive a massive portion of their valuation from cash flows projected years or even decades into the future. They often reinvest heavily today with the promise of explosive earnings later. When you discount those distant future cash flows back to today at a higher rate, their present value shrinks dramatically. This is why they're called "long-duration" stocks—they behave like long-term bonds in this regard. A 1% increase in the discount rate can shave off tens of billions from a company's valuation if most of its value is expected in 2035 or beyond, whereas a mature utility company earning steady cash today is much less affected because those near-term dollars aren't discounted as heavily.

Second, there's the opportunity cost and risk appetite shift. When rates are low, cash in the bank earns almost nothing, so investors are forced to take on more risk in growth stocks to get any return. They'll happily pay a 40x earnings multiple for a company promising future profits because the alternative is a 1% yield on bonds. But when the Fed hikes rates and you can suddenly earn 5% on a risk-free Treasury bill, that "risk-free" option becomes attractive. Investors start pulling money out of volatile, high-multiple tech stocks and rotating into safer assets or companies with more immediate profitability. This reduces demand for tech shares specifically, pushing their prices down further beyond just the DCF math.

It's also worth noting that higher rates increase borrowing costs for tech firms themselves, especially those funding expansion through debt, which can squeeze margins and slow growth—the very thing investors were buying them for. So you get a double whammy: the theoretical value of their future earnings drops due to discounting, while the actual near-term business environment gets tougher, making those future earnings projections less certain to begin with.

The nuance is that not all tech stocks react equally. Mature, cash-generative companies like Microsoft or Apple with strong current dividends and buybacks are more resilient than pre-profit AI startups whose entire valuation rests on distant future milestones. And this pressure isn't permanent—if rates eventually fall again, those long-duration valuations can re-rate upward quickly, which is why tech often leads recoveries after rate-cutting cycles begin.
