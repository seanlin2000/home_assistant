ASSISTANT: So the weights alone would be roughly 130 gigabytes in 16-bit, about 35 gigabytes at 4-bit, and around 17 gigabytes at 2-bit. In practice you need a bit more than that for the KV cache during generation and for activations, but the weights dominate, so those figures give you a good sense of scale.

The trade-offs come down to accuracy versus efficiency. At 4-bit, modern methods like GPTQ or AWQ are quite good. They typically use per-group scaling factors and sometimes keep a few sensitive layers at higher precision, so quality loss is often small enough that most tasks barely notice. That's why a 70B model at 4-bit can run on a single high-end consumer GPU with around 48 gigabytes of memory, something impossible at full precision.

At 2-bit you're getting into genuinely aggressive territory. The information lost per weight is large enough that quality degradation becomes noticeable, especially on tasks requiring precise reasoning or factual recall. You'll see more hallucinations and worse performance on benchmarks. Some newer methods do better than older ones, but 2-bit is generally considered the edge of practicality for a model this size.

There are other trade-offs worth knowing about. Quantization makes loading faster since there's less data to move from disk, and inference can be faster because the arithmetic units handle smaller numbers more efficiently, though that speedup depends on your hardware. The main thing to keep in mind is that quantization is a one-way street. Once you've thrown away precision, you can't recover it, so if you might want full quality later, keep the original weights around.
