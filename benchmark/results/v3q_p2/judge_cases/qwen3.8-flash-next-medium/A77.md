Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to one idea: sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow, and the air in our atmosphere scatters some of those colors much more readily than others.

Sunlight arrives as a beam containing red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet light. Light travels as a wave, and each color has a different wavelength — red is long and lazy, blue and violet are short and jittery. As sunlight passes through the atmosphere, it bumps into the tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen that make up the air. These molecules are so small that they scatter the short, choppy wavelengths very efficiently and barely bother with the long ones. Blue and violet light gets knocked out of the direct beam and flung off in every direction almost immediately.

That scattered light is what you see when you look up. No matter which direction you glance, some of that blue light that was knocked sideways is heading toward your eyes from all across the sky, so the whole dome above you glows blue. Violet actually scatters even more strongly than blue, but two things work against it: the sun emits less violet to begin with, and our eyes are far less sensitive to violet, so we perceive the mixture as blue.

At sunset, the geometry changes. When the sun is high overhead, sunlight crosses only a relatively shallow layer of atmosphere to reach you. But when the sun is near the horizon, its light has to travel through a much thicker slab of air — potentially dozens of times more atmosphere than at midday. Every stretch of that long journey strips more and more of the blue out of the beam, scattering it away before the light ever gets to you. By the time the beam arrives, most of the blues and greens have been filtered out, leaving the long wavelengths that the air can't easily touch: reds, oranges and yellows. That's why the sun itself and the clouds it's still shining on look like fire.

There's a nice way to see the same effect on the moonless parts of the sky. The reddish glow near the horizon at dusk is actually the collective scattered light of all the sunsets happening around the world, faintly tinting the atmosphere. And after the sun dips below the horizon, the band of deep blue just above it — sometimes called Belt of Venus if you see the pinkish glow alongside it — is the last of that scattered light fading out.

So the short version: blue dominates the daytime sky because the air throws short wavelengths around in all directions, and sunsets go red because light crossing that huge thickness of atmosphere has all its blue scattered away before it reaches your eyes.
