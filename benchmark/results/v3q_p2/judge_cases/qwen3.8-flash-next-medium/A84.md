Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock price is the present value of the cash a business will produce in the future, and interest rates are the discount rate you use to bring those future dollars back to today. A dollar promised ten years from now is worth less when interest rates are high, because you could instead put your money in something safe and earn more on it. So when the Fed raises rates, every future cash flow is worth a little less in today's dollars, and the price you'll pay for the stream of cash falls.

The effect is much bigger for technology stocks because of the timing of their cash flows. Investors think of long-duration growth stocks as exactly what the name implies: their cash flows sit far out in the future, so they have a long duration, in the bond sense. If a company pays you most of its value in year ten, doubling the discount rate hits it far harder than it hits a mature business generating cash right now.

I ran the arithmetic to show the magnitude. Suppose a company will pay you a hundred dollars ten years from now. Discount it at four percent a year and it's worth about sixty-seven fifty today. Discount the same hundred dollars at eight percent and it's worth about forty-six thirty. That's a decline of roughly thirty-one percent, from a rate change that is only four percentage points. Now do the same exercise with cash arriving next year, at four percent versus eight percent: the value falls from about ninety-six to about ninety-three, a drop of about three percent. Same rate change, ten times the damage, purely because the cash is further away.

That duration effect is the whole story, but three related channels amplify it. First, the discount rate investors apply is not the Fed's rate itself but the risk-free rate plus a risk premium. When the Fed lifts the safe short-term rate, the safe alternatives to stocks, money market funds and Treasuries, start paying respectable yields, which both raises the denominator in the valuation and makes bonds a credible competitor for the same dollars. That's why high-growth equity and long-duration bonds tend to fall together when rates rise.

Second, technology valuations are unusually dependent on assumptions about the far-out tail, the growth rate and margins in year twelve and beyond. A model whose value is mostly terminal value is sensitive to the discount rate precisely because that terminal value gets divided by the rate. Cut the assumed growth rate a little and raise the discount rate a little, and a large multiple collapses to a small one. Low multiples, like a utility or a bank, are comparatively insensitive because their value comes from near-term earnings rather than assumptions about 2040.

Third, cheap debt was part of how a lot of these businesses got built and scaled. Higher rates raise borrowing costs, slow the venture and private credit funding that supported unprofitable growth, squeeze the leveraged buyout and IPO pipelines, and often coincide with a stronger dollar and slower spending, all of which bite into the very growth the high multiple was assuming. Higher rates also tend to compress the price-to-earnings multiples investors will pay even for the profitable ones, which shows up in the P/E rather than the earnings line.

One honest caveat about the phrase "generally." The relationship is real and well documented through the 2022 tightening cycle, when rate expectations moved sharply and growth-oriented tech fell far more than value or energy. But it is not a law of nature. If the Fed is hiking because the economy is running hot and demand is strong, earnings can rise at the same time and offset some of the discounting effect, and if a sector has genuine pricing power and fast-growing cash flows, as the largest platform companies have had, it can absorb a higher discount rate better than the theory suggests. A rate rise that starts from a low level also matters more than one that starts from an already high level, because it is the percentage change in the discount rate, not the absolute level, that drives the revaluation.
