ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means throwing away the precision of the numbers in it. A 70-billion-parameter model's weights are normally stored as 16-bit floats — either FP16 or bfloat16 — one number per parameter. Quantization replaces that continuum with a small set of discrete levels, and instead of storing "0.0197..." you store an index into a short list of allowed values, plus a scale and offset for a group of maybe 64 or 128 weights that share them. So a 4-bit weight is one of 16 levels in its group, and a 2-bit weight is one of 4. The model architecture, the forward pass and the activations stay the same; only the stored weights get coarser, and they get dequantized back to floats as they're used.

The memory math is straightforward. Seventy billion weights at 16 bits each is 140 gigabytes — about 130 gibibytes, which is why the published weights of a 70B model are usually a little under 130 GB of files. At 4 bits each, that's half a byte per parameter: 35 GB, roughly 32.6 GiB. At 2 bits it's a quarter of a byte: 17.5 GB, about 16.3 GiB. In practice the real numbers are a bit higher, because good quantizers don't use a flat bit width. They keep some layers — embeddings, the very sensitive ones — at higher precision, and add the bookkeeping scales. So GGUF's popular Q4_K_M build of a 70B model works out to about 4.6 bits per weight and lands near 40 GB, and the 2-bit levels typically come in around 2.5 to 3 bits per weight, or 22 to 26 GB on disk. And you always need room beyond the weights for activations and the KV cache, so a 35 GB model wants a card or cards with comfortably more than 35 GB.

The trade-offs are worth being precise about, because 4-bit and 2-bit are very different experiences. A 4-bit quantized 70B model is, by most accounts, nearly indistinguishable from the original for ordinary use. The benchmark roundups I found put perplexity — how surprised the model is by real text — up by roughly 1.2 to 2 percent versus full precision: one table for Llama 4 70B shows 3.47 at Q4_K_M against 3.42 in FP16, and 3.46 for AWQ 4-bit. General knowledge barely moves; a comparison of Llama 3 70B builds showed MMLU at about 80.5 percent for a 4-bit build against the full model's similar score, while an 8-bit build is effectively lossless.

Two-bit is a different story. That same table has Q2_K adding about 11 percent perplexity on a 70B model, and around 70 percent of the original quality in one estimate. On MMLU, Llama 3 70B drops to roughly 77 percent at IQ2_M and 76 percent at IQ2_S, from about 80 percent at 4-bit. Reasoning tasks break first: one 2026 benchmark set notes that math accuracy at 3-bit falls about three times as far as perplexity does, so for anything involving multi-step reasoning, code, tool use or agents, going below 4-bit is where the failure shows up. The general pattern people report is that degradation shrinks as models get bigger — a 70B model tolerates 4-bit much better than a 7B one — and that 2-bit only makes sense when you're genuinely out of memory and just need the model to fit.

There are a few other costs. Quantization is usually done post-training against a small calibration dataset, so a bad calibration set or a sensitive model can bite you, and the lost information can't be recovered by tweaking. Outlier weights — a small fraction that dominate activations — are the main source of damage, which is why methods like AWQ and GPTQ exist: AWQ identifies those weights and protects or rescales them, and it generally beats GPTQ by roughly half a percent to one percent of perplexity at the same bit width. Quantized weights are also hard to train further, which is why fine-tuning usually means adapters on top of a frozen 4-bit base, as in QLoRA. On the upside, a smaller model moves fewer bytes per token, so generation is faster on the same hardware and you get more memory left for a longer context window. The exception is throughput serving, where an unquantized or FP8 model with proper kernels on real GPU memory will beat a 4-bit GGUF file. And if you're measuring model quality for real, benchmark the full-precision model, because quantization adds a variable you don't want in the experiment.
