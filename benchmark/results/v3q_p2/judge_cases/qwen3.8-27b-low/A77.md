Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Okay, here's the idea, and I think it's actually kind of beautiful once you see it.

Sunlight looks white to us, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow bundled together. When that sunlight hits the molecules in our air, the air kind of bumps the light around and scatters it in every direction. Here's the key: the air scatters the short, fast colors like blue and violet way more than the long, slow colors like red and orange. Think of it like how small pebbles in a stream get kicked around by the current while big bouldles just sit there. The blue light is the pebble, the red light is the boulder.

So during the day, when the sun is high up, its light takes a relatively short trip through the air to reach you. All that blue light gets scattered everywhere in the sky, and from your point on the ground, you're looking up through all of it. That's why the whole sky glows blue. You're basically seeing the scattered blue light coming from every direction.

Now at sunset, the sun is right on the horizon, and its light has to squeeze through a much, much longer slice of atmosphere to get to your eyes. It's like the difference between peering through a window and peering through the whole wall of a house. Over that long path, the blue light has been scattered away so many times that almost none of it makes it through to you. What's left is mostly the reds and oranges, which the air doesn't scatter much. So you're left staring at that warm leftover glow.

One small footnote: violet actually scatters even more than blue, so you might wonder why the sky isn't violet. The short answer is that our eyes aren't very good at seeing violet, and a lot of it gets absorbed by the upper atmosphere before it matters. So blue wins out.

It's the same physics, just a longer road for the light to travel at sunset, and the road filters out the blue before it gets to you.
