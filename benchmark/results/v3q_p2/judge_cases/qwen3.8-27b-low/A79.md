Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

Why GPUs work so well for AI inference. A transformer model, whether you're training it or running it, is overwhelmingly big matrix multiplications. GPUs have thousands of small cores that can crunch many of those multiplies at once, so they match the shape of the work. Modern ones also add dedicated tensor cores that are tuned exactly for those dot products, and they pair that with huge amounts of fast high-bandwidth memory. That second point matters more than people think: running a model is usually limited not by how fast you can multiply, but by how fast you can stream the model's weights through memory. So a GPU that pairs big compute with lots of high-speed memory fits inference well. On top of all that sits CUDA, which has been tuned for over a decade, so the whole software stack just runs on it with almost no rework.

The three kinds of hardware. A GPU is a general-purpose parallel processor. It can do graphics, video, simulation, and AI, which is what makes it flexible and easy to adopt, but that generality is also a tax. It spends logic, power, and area on things AI doesn't need, like handling unpredictable control flow and supporting arbitrary applications.

An ASIC, or application-specific integrated circuit, throws that flexibility away on purpose. It is hard-wired for one narrow job, and in exchange it does that job with much less power and at much lower cost per unit of work. Google's TPUs are the flagship example, and now Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta all build their own ASICs for exactly their own AI workloads. Because the silicon does only one thing, it doesn't carry the overhead a general-purpose chip has.

A specialized inference processor is a middle category. Companies like Groq, Cerebras, and SambaNova build chips aimed specifically at running models and squeezing out high token rates and low latency. They're not general-purpose, and they're not always a fully custom in-house design the way a hyperscaler's ASIC is, but they are purpose-built for inference rather than for everything else. In practice the line between this and an ASIC gets blurry, so you can think of it as "any non-GPU chip designed around the inference workload."

When a specialized chip beats an NVIDIA GPU. The honest answer is: on raw cost per token and power per token, for a workload you can pin down. A few specific situations make this likely.

First, when your workload is stable and well understood. If you know you will run the same model, at the same size, with the same shapes, for a long time, an ASIC can be co-designed around exactly that and win on efficiency. The more varied or changing your workloads, the more the GPU's flexibility is worth and the less the ASIC's advantage shrinks.

Second, when you are the one making the chip, or locked to a vendor who is. This is the big real-world catch. Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta don't sell their custom silicon to outsiders; each uses its own chips inside its own cloud. So a hyperscaler running millions of identical inference requests can genuinely beat a GPU on cost, but an ordinary customer who needs to rent compute mostly still lands on GPU clouds, with AMD as a real second option. The custom chips simply aren't available to rent in most cases.

Third, when you care about peak throughput or latency on a fixed model. Specialized inference chips can hit very high token-per-second numbers because they route the whole pipeline for that task. For a batch of predictable inference at scale, that can translate into more tokens per dollar and per watt than a GPU.

Where the GPU keeps winning is flexibility. If your models change, you move between training and inference, you need a broad ecosystem and mature software, or you want one platform to do many things, the NVIDIA chip is hard to beat because nothing else gives you that CUDA ecosystem. So it's not that a specialized chip is universally better; it's that for a stable, high-volume, well-known inference workload, a purpose-built chip can deliver more performance for less power and money, while the GPU wins on generality and software maturity.
