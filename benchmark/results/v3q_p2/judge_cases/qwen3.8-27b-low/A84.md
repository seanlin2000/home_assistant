Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core of it comes down to how we value any asset whose worth depends on future cash flows, and how sensitive that valuation is to the rate at which we discount those flows back to today.

When you value a stock using a discounted cash flow model, you take all the cash the company is expected to generate in the future and bring each of those dollars back to present value using a discount rate. That discount rate is anchored, in large part, to the risk-free rate, which tracks what the Federal Reserve is doing. So when the Fed raises rates, the denominator in your present value calculation gets bigger, and the result is that every future dollar is worth a little less to you today.

Now here's where it gets specific to tech stocks. A mature utility or a consumer staples company might earn most of its cash within the next two or three years. Those near-term cash flows aren't that affected by a change in the discount rate because you're not discounting them very far into the future. But a high-growth tech company, especially one that's still unprofitable, is often being valued on cash flows that are ten, fifteen, even twenty years out. The bulk of its present value is sitting in the deep future.

That makes it what analysts call a "long-duration" asset, and the term is borrowed deliberately from bonds. A thirty-year bond is much more sensitive to interest rate changes than a two-year bond, and the same math applies. The further out a cash flow sits, the more its present value gets compressed when the discount rate ticks up. A one or two percent increase in the discount rate might shave a modest amount off the value of a cash flow coming in next year, but it can cut the present value of a cash flow coming in fifteen years by a much larger percentage. So growth stocks, which are essentially portfolios of distant future cash flows, take the biggest hit.

There are a couple of secondary effects that reinforce the same direction. Higher rates make bonds and other fixed-income investments more attractive relative to stocks, so some of the capital that was flowing into equities, particularly speculative growth equities, rotates out. And higher borrowing costs can slow overall economic activity, which in turn reduces demand for products and services, making those far-future cash flows slightly less likely to materialize. But the dominant channel, the one that's purely mathematical and doesn't depend on any behavioral assumption, is the DCF effect: higher discount rate, long-duration cash flows, lower present value.

It's also worth noting that this relationship is asymmetric in practice. When the Fed cuts rates, the reverse happens and growth stocks tend to rally hard because their distant cash flows become much more valuable. That's why you often see tech leadership in the first phase of an easing cycle.
