Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: Here's the core idea, and then I'll tie it to where rates actually sit right now.

**The valuation math: a stock is the present value of its future cash flows.**

Any asset is worth the sum of the money it will produce in the future, discounted back to today. The discount rate is essentially the return you could get from a safe alternative — usually anchored to the long-term Treasury yield — plus a premium for the risk that those cash flows don't show up. When the Fed raises rates, it pushes that whole risk-free curve upward, and every future dollar gets divided by a bigger number.

**The catch is *when* those dollars arrive.** A dollar you receive in one year barely feels a rate change: dividing by 1.05 versus 1.02 costs about three percent. A dollar you receive in fifteen years is hammered: at a two percent discount rate it's worth about eighty-three cents today, at four percent it's worth about fifty-five cents — roughly a third less, from the same nominal dollar. So the sensitivity to rates scales with how far out the cash flows sit.

**That's exactly what "long-duration" means for a growth stock.** Duration here is a bond concept borrowed for equities: it measures how much of a company's value lives in cash flows far in the future, not in earnings today. A mature utility or a cigarette company is short duration — most of its value is next year's profit and dividend, which barely move when rates move. A hypergrowth software or biotech company can be profitable almost nowhere in the near term and enormously profitable somewhere around 2035 or beyond. Its entire equity value is essentially one distant payment, so it behaves like a thirty-year bond with no coupon: rate rises hit it disproportionately hard.

**Two amplifiers make it worse than the pure discounting math suggests.**

First, multiples are mechanically linked to growth expectations. A price-to-earnings multiple on a company growing earnings at twenty-five percent a year embeds an assumption about how long that growth persists and what discount rate applies to it. Raise the discount rate and the justified multiple shrinks even if nothing changes about the business — this is multiple compression, and it's why a tech stock can fall twenty percent in a quarter while its revenue guidance stays intact. One of the sources I read frames this concretely: if ten-year yields settle near four and a half percent, tech price-to-earnings ratios could compress fifteen to twenty percent from 2025 levels.

Second, higher rates raise the actual cost of capital for these companies, not just the arithmetic of valuation. Cheap debt is what made aggressive buildouts, buybacks and acquisitions possible; venture funding priced on near-zero risk-free rates collapses in value when those rates move, which starves the pipeline of unprofitable growth companies.
