Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that sunlight looks white, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and the air in our atmosphere sorts those colors out by scattering some of them around more than others.

Here's how that works. Sunlight hits the atmosphere and runs into trillions of tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen. Light travels as a wave, and each color has a different wavelength — red light has long, lazy waves, while blue and violet have short, jittery ones. Short waves bump into those gas molecules much more easily than long waves do. When a blue wave hits a molecule, the molecule absorbs a little of that energy and immediately re-emits it in a random new direction, sending the blue light scattering all across the sky. Physicists call this Rayleigh scattering, and it's why the whole dome overhead glows blue rather than just the sun itself looking bright against a black void, which is what you'd see on the Moon or in space.

Two details make it interesting. First, violet light scatters even more strongly than blue, so you might expect a violet sky. It isn't, mostly because the sun gives off less violet to begin with, and our eyes are simply much less sensitive to violet than to blue — so the mix we perceive comes out as that familiar pale blue.

Second, at sunset the geometry changes. When the sun is overhead, its light only has to push through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere. At sunset, the light arrives at a shallow angle and has to travel through a much longer path of air to reach your eyes — often many times more than at midday. Along that long road, nearly all the short blue and violet waves get scattered out of the beam before it ever gets to you. What survives the trip are the long-wavelength reds, oranges and yellows, which pass through relatively untouched. So the sun itself, and the clouds it's lighting up, appear in warm colors.

There's one more thing that boosts sunset colors: dust, smoke, pollution and tiny water droplets. These bigger particles scatter all colors more or less equally, and they help strip out whatever blue is left while adding a soft glow. That's why sunsets after a wildfire, a volcanic eruption, or a dusty spell can be spectacularly red — there's simply more stuff in the air to knock the other colors out of the way.

So the same sunlight, the same physics, just a different thickness of atmosphere between you and the source: a short trip gives you blue everywhere overhead, and a long trip gives you what's left at the end of the rainbow.
