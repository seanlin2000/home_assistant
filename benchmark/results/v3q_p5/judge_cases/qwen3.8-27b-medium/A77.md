Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: It all comes down to how sunlight interacts with the air in our atmosphere.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and each color travels as a wave of a different length. Blues and violets are short, choppy waves, while reds and oranges are long, lazy ones.

Now, sunlight hits billions upon billions of air molecules on its way to your eyes. Those tiny molecules are much, much smaller than the light waves, but short waves bump into them much more effectively. When blue light hits a molecule, it gets scattered in every direction — some of it bounces back down to your eyes. So when you look up at the daytime sky, you're seeing all that scattered blue light pouring in from every angle. The long red waves just plow through with hardly a bump.

One small thing you might wonder about: violet waves are even shorter than blue, so why isn't the sky violet? Two reasons. Sunlight contains a bit less violet than blue to begin with, and our eyes are just much better at seeing blue than violet.

Now here's the sunset part, and it's the same physics with one crucial difference: the path length. At midday, the sun is overhead, so light only has to travel a short distance through the atmosphere to reach you. Not much gets scattered away before it arrives. But at sunset, the sun is right on the horizon, so its light has to squeeze through a much, much thicker slab of air to get to your eyes — roughly ten times more air.

And that extra distance adds up. Blue and violet light gets scattered out of the beam over and over, and by the time the light has crossed all that air, it's mostly stripped of its blue. What survives the trip and reaches your eyes is the remaining reds, oranges, and yellows — which is exactly what the low sun looks like.

The dust and water droplets in the air can make this even more dramatic, which is why the smoke after a big wildfire or the ash from a volcano can paint some of the most spectacular red and purple sunsets you'll ever see — more particles, more scattering, bluer stripped away, redder glow left behind.

So in one sentence: the sky is blue because air scatters short blue light in all directions, and sunsets are red because that light has to cross so much more air that the blue is scattered away before it ever gets to you.
