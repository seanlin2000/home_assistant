The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

ASSISTANT: Here's the setup I'd recommend, and then what survives a power-and-network outage.

The core idea is that the Hue Bridge is your source of truth. Your bulbs talk to the bridge over Zigbee, and the bridge sits on your local Wi-Fi network. Everything else — phones, apps, voice assistants — is just a client talking to the bridge.

For multiple people on phones, use the Hue app's built-in sharing. In the app, under Settings, Home access, you invite each person by email. They accept the invite while signed into their own Philips Hue account — that part matters, because if they log into your account they inherit everything and you lose the point of separate access. The bridge is required for this, so having one is the right call. Each person can then control the same rooms and scenes from their own phone, and you can revoke anyone's access later from that same screen.

For voice, the bridge now supports Matter with multi-admin, which means you can link the same bridge to more than one ecosystem at once. So if someone in the apartment uses Apple and another uses Amazon, you don't have to pick a single camp — you can expose the bridge to Alexa, Apple Home, Google Home, and SmartThings simultaneously, and each person uses whatever speaker they already have. My practical suggestion: pick one platform per room based on whose device actually lives there — say Echo in the kitchen, HomePod in the bedroom — rather than trying to wire every platform into every room. One thing worth knowing: Google has been retiring classic Google Assistant on phones starting September 2026, with Gemini taking over, though Google Home speakers and displays still run it for now. If voice control on Android phones is central to your plan, I'd weigh Alexa or Apple Home a bit more heavily.

Now the internet-outage part, which is where the bridge really earns its keep. The bulbs don't use Wi-Fi at all — they talk Zigbee to the bridge, and the bridge controls them over your local network. So when your internet drops, the bridge and the Zigbee mesh keep working exactly as before. Here's what survives:

- The Hue app on anyone's phone still works, as long as they're on your home Wi-Fi or local network.
- Any automations that run on the bridge itself — schedules, sunsets, scenes — keep running on time.
- Voice depends on the platform. Apple Home and recent Echo devices support local control of Matter devices, so with a compatible Thread/Matter setup, voice commands like "turn on the kitchen light" can still work on the local network without the cloud. With Alexa, simple local control generally works; with Google Home it's cloud-dependent for most things, so I'd treat that as the weakest of the three for outages.

What definitely does not survive: remote access from outside the home, since that routes through the cloud, and anything the voice assistant needs to look up in the cloud. The one thing to watch for is if your internet outage is actually a whole-router failure — the bridge needs your local network up, so if the power outage takes out the router too, the local apps and voice stop as well. If you want maximum resilience, some people run Home Assistant as a local layer on top of the bridge, which also works fully offline and is a fine option if you enjoy tinkering, but for a small apartment, the bridge plus shared Hue accounts plus one or two Matter-linked voice ecosystems is genuinely enough.
