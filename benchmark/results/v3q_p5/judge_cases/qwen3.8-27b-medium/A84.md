Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is one of the more important relationships in equity markets, so let me walk through it in order.

Start with the foundation: almost every rational model for valuing a stock, including what professional analysts use, comes down to a discounted cash flow calculation. A company's value is theoretically the sum of all the cash it will ever generate, brought back to today's dollars. The problem is that a dollar ten years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because that dollar could be invested and earn something in the meantime. So each future dollar gets multiplied by a discount factor, and the size of that factor depends on the interest rate, usually expressed as a required rate of return.

Now notice the critical asymmetry: the effect of the discount rate is not uniform across time. A dollar earned next year is barely affected by whether the rate is three percent or six percent. But a dollar earned in twenty or thirty years gets hit very hard. The discount factor compounds, so high rates applied over long stretches shrink distant cash flows dramatically. That is the entire engine of the effect.

This is where "duration" enters, and it is the same concept finance uses for bonds, just applied to stocks. A mature company like a utilities firm, which pays a large dividend today and grows slowly, is short-duration: most of its value sits in near-term cash flows. A young technology company is long-duration: it may not be profitable at all for a decade, and its value is almost entirely in cash flows far out in the future. If you model a company whose value is mostly thirty years ahead, then doubling the discount rate can cut its theoretical value by a third or more, while the dividend-paying company might lose ten or fifteen percent. The math is unforgiving to long horizons.

There are two reinforcing mechanisms on top of the pure math. First, the opportunity cost channel: when risk-free Treasury bills pay four or five percent with essentially no risk, investors demand a higher return to accept the risk of a speculative growth stock. The whole required-return line shifts up, and long-duration stocks are the most sensitive to that shift. You start asking why you would hold a company earning zero today when cash pays you well just for waiting. Second, the funding channel: many tech companies, especially younger ones, grow by borrowing or by being valued against a backdrop of cheap capital. Higher rates raise their cost of capital and slow down the venture financing that funds them, which can pressure actual future earnings, not just the discount applied to them.

So the summary: raising rates does not mean tech stocks must fall, but it removes a structural tailwind. It discounts distant profits more heavily, shifts investors toward safer yields, and makes growth capital more expensive. That is why you saw dramatic tech selloffs during the 2022 rate-hike cycle, and why high-quality growth companies with real earnings and near-term cash flow tend to hold up far better than loss-making ones. The longer the runway to profitability, the more the stock cares what the Fed does on any given day.
