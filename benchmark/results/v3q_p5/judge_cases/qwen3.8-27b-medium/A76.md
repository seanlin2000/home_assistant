USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that the Cold War didn't so much end in a single moment as run out of Soviet will and money, and the Soviet Union itself fell apart in the final days of 1991. It's worth keeping two layers separate, because people often blur them: the deep, long-running causes that made the whole system brittle, and the specific events that pushed it over the edge once it was already cracking.

Start with the causes, because nothing that happened in 1989 or 1991 makes sense without them. The Soviet economy had been stagnating since the 1970s. It was propping up an enormous military budget and a system of client states in Eastern Europe while ordinary citizens faced empty shelves and a black market for everything from coffee to clothing. The arms race with the United States, especially under Reagan in the 1980s, made that fiscal strain worse. When Mikhail Gorbachev took power in 1985, he was trying to fix this from the inside. His two signature programs, perestroika, which meant economic restructuring, and glasnost, which meant openness and a loosening of political and censorship controls, were really the root cause of what followed. The problem was that glasnost let people say things that had been forbidden for decades, and perestroika made the economy a little less rigid before making it a lot more unstable. He effectively took the lid off the pot and then couldn't control what boiled over.

The three events I'd call the most important, and the ones where the causes finally met their triggers:

The first is the 1989 wave of revolutions across Eastern Europe, and the symbolic core of it, the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989. For thirty-eight years the whole Cold War arrangement rested on the Soviet promise that it would use force to hold its satellite states in line. Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany all saw governments fall or borders open in that year. When the wall fell and Gorbachev refused to send tanks in, that was the moment the "Soviet sphere" ceased to exist. The West and the Soviet public understood at once that there was no longer a barrier between the two halves of Europe, and it drained the Cold War of its central meaning. That was a trigger set in motion by the deeper cause of Gorbachev's new, non-interventionist stance.

The second, and I think the single most decisive one, is the failed coup in August 1991. A group of hard-line members of the Communist Party, the so-called State Committee on the State of Emergency, tried to haul Gorbachev out of office and reverse the reforms. They miscalculated badly. Instead of intimidating everyone, the coup made the old party look like a bunch of frightened old men, and it threw all the real power to Boris Yeltsin, the leader of the Russian republic, who stood on a tank in Moscow and emerged as the clear winner. After the coup collapsed, the Communist Party was effectively banned and Gorbachev was a figurehead. Everything in the next four months was downhill, because Yeltsin and the republics now had both the reason and the means to tear the union apart. This is the clearest example of a trigger: the underlying weakness was there, but it was the coup that made disintegration irreversible.

The third is the formal legal and political break in December 1991, capped by Gorbachev's resignation on the 25th. In December, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belavezha Accords declaring the Soviet Union effectively dissolved and creating the Commonwealth of Independent States. Ukraine then held a referendum and voted overwhelmingly to be independent. With Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus gone, there was no union left to hold together. So on the 25th of December, Gorbachev announced he was stepping down, the Soviet flag was taken down from the Kremlin, and the next day Russia got its own flag. That was the actual, formal end of the state, not a dramatic battle or a single decision, but the administrative last step of a collapse that the August coup had already made inevitable.

So to keep the two layers distinct: the causes were long-term economic stagnation, the burden of the arms race and Eastern Europe, and above all Gorbachev's own reforms, which opened up a system that couldn't survive being opened. The immediate triggers were the events of 1989 that ended the Eastern European satellite system, and above all the failed August 1991 coup, which handed power to Yeltsin and made the final break in December unavoidable. The Cold War, as a confrontation between two armed blocs, ended when the second bloc stopped existing.
