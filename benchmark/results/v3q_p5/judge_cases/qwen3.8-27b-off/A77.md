Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The reason comes down to how sunlight interacts with the air around us, and it's all about scattering. Sunlight looks white, but it's actually made up of all the colors of the rainbow mixed together. Each color has a different wavelength: blue and violet are short, choppy waves, while red and orange are long, rolling ones.

Here's the key: when sunlight hits the tiny molecules in our atmosphere, the shorter blue waves get scattered in every direction much more effectively than the longer red waves. So during the day, when the sun is high overhead, that scattered blue light bounces around so much that no matter where you look up, your eyes are filled with it. That's why the daytime sky is blue.

Now, at sunset, the sun is low on the horizon, so its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. Over that long journey, most of the blue light gets scattered away completely, out of your line of sight. What's left to punch all the way through to your eyes is the longer red and orange light, which scatters less easily. So instead of a blue sky, you get those warm glowing colors.

A few nice details that make it even more interesting: sunsets can look especially vivid after big dust storms or volcanic eruptions, because extra particles in the air scatter even more blue away and let even deeper reds through. And the reason we don't see the violet light scattered too is a bit of a quirk of our eyes: they're less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed high in the atmosphere before it matters. So the story really is about wavelength, path length, and what's left over when the journey through the air is done.
