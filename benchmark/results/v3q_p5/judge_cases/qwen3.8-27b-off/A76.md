ASSISTANT: The short version: the Cold War didn't end with a bang or a treaty; it ended through a combination of economic exhaustion, reform attempts that backfired, and then a rapid cascade of revolutions in Eastern Europe that stripped the Soviet Union of its empire before the center collapsed. The dissolution happened over roughly four years, from 1985 to 1991, and historians generally point to three events as the most consequential.

The first is the accession of Mikhail Gorbachev to power in March 1985 and his program of Perestroika and Glasnost. This is a cause, not a trigger: Gorbachev inherited an economy that was stagnating and a military spending burden it could no longer afford, and he deliberately tried to modernize the Soviet system with economic restructuring and political openness. The reform itself wasn't fatal, but it removed the old regime's capacity for control. Glasnost allowed previously suppressed grievances in the Baltic states, Ukraine, and elsewhere to be aired publicly, and Perestroika weakened the central planning that had held the union together economically. Without Gorbachev's willingness to let go of the iron fist, the later events would likely have been crushed as they were in Hungary in 1956 or Czechoslovakia in 1968.

The second is the fall of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989, and the broader "Revolution of 1989" that swept through Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria over the following months. This is where cause meets trigger. The underlying causes were Gorbachev's reforms plus the Soviet Union's demonstrated unwillingness to use force to hold its satellites in place — this became known as the "Sinatra Doctrine," a play on the Brezhnev Doctrine, after Sinatra's famous line about doing it his way. But the immediate trigger was a specific bureaucratic accident: an East German official misread a question during a press conference and announced that citizens could cross the border immediately, rather than starting the next day. The crowd showed up at the wall by the thousands, and once people were actually walking through, no one could stop it. From there, revolutions cascaded through the region within weeks. This is a good example of how a long-standing cause (Soviet weakness and reform) needed a small contingent event to release itself.
