Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: Great question — this one's mostly about financial theory rather than current events, so I can explain it from first principles without needing to look anything up. The core idea is that a stock's "fair" value is the present value of all the cash flows it will generate over its life, and those future cash flows get discounted back to today using an interest-rate-based discount rate.

When the Fed raises rates, that discount rate goes up. Mathematically, each dollar promised in the future is worth less in today's terms when you discount at a higher rate. But here's the key part: the effect grows dramatically with time. A dollar ten years from now loses much more value to a higher discount rate than a dollar next year does. That's why we call long-duration assets like tech growth stocks particularly sensitive — their value is concentrated far in the future, often a decade or more out, when companies are expected to scale massively.

Think of it this way: a mature utility company earns most of its value from cash flow in the next few years, so a rate hike hurts modestly. But a high-growth tech firm might derive the bulk of its valuation from cash flows twenty or thirty years down the road. When those distant dollars get discounted more aggressively, the present value — and therefore the stock price — falls disproportionately.

There's also a second channel: higher rates make safer assets like bonds and T-bills more attractive relative to risky stocks. Investors compare the expected return on a tech stock against what they can earn risk-free, so when the risk-free rate rises, the required return on equities rises too, pushing prices down until the yield is again compelling.

And a third practical effect: growth companies are often capital-intensive or unprofitable early on, relying on cheap debt and cheap equity to fund expansion. Higher rates raise their cost of capital, which can slow growth and widen losses — making the future cash flows they're promising less certain, which compounds the valuation pressure.

So it's really a combination: higher discount rates shrink the present value of distant cash flows, bonds become more competitive as an alternative, and the companies' own ability to grow is constrained by more expensive financing. That's why you see tech stocks, especially long-duration growth names, tend to fall hardest when the Fed is hiking.
