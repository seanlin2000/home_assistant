USER: What does it mean to quantize a 70-billion-parameter language model to 4-bit or 2-bit precision? How much memory would the weights require in each case, and what trade-offs does quantization introduce?

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means replacing its full-precision weights — normally stored as 16-bit floating-point numbers — with much coarser, lower-precision values. Instead of letting each weight take any of hundreds of thousands of possible values, you round each one to the nearest value in a small set, like 16 levels for 4-bit or just 4 levels for 2-bit. You also store a couple of extra numbers per group of weights — a scale and a zero-point — so you can convert back to an approximate real number when doing math. The model gets smaller and faster to load, but it's no longer exact; the rounding is controlled noise added to every weight.

For a 70-billion-parameter model, here's the memory arithmetic. At full 16-bit precision, that's roughly 140 gigabytes of weights alone. At 4-bit — half a byte per parameter — you're down to about 35 gigabytes, plus a few percent overhead for the scale and zero-point values. At 2-bit, a quarter byte each, it's roughly 17.5 gigabytes plus similar overhead. So quantizing from 16-bit to 4-bit is a fourfold reduction, and 2-bit is an eightfold reduction.

The trade-offs come in a few flavors. First, quality: the coarser the precision, the more rounding error accumulates through the layers. Going from 16-bit to 4-bit usually costs only a small amount of performance on most tasks — modern methods like GPTQ or AWQ are quite good at this. But 2-bit is much more aggressive; you'll typically see noticeable degradation, especially on harder reasoning tasks, long-context coherence, and anything that depends on fine-grained numerical patterns. Some models hold up better than others at low bit counts.

Second, speed versus capacity: a smaller model fits in less memory, which means it can run on hardware that couldn't otherwise — your laptop instead of a server, say. Less memory to move around also means faster inference in many setups, though the decompression step from 4-bit or 2-bit back to full precision during computation adds its own cost.

Third, method matters: naive uniform quantization works poorly because weight distributions aren't uniform. Better methods use group-wise scaling, mixed precision (keeping some sensitive layers at higher bit counts), or calibration on a small dataset to minimize error. A well-tuned 4-bit model is often nearly as good as the original; a poorly done one can be surprisingly bad even at 8-bit.

So in practice: 4-bit is the sweet spot for most people who want to run large models locally — you get roughly a quarter of the memory with minimal quality loss. 2-bit is mostly for research or for pushing a model onto extremely constrained hardware where you accept real quality hits.
