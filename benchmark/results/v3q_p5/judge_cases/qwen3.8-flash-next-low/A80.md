## Beyond GGUF: MLX, MXFP4 and NVFP4
### NVFP4
NVFP4 is NVIDIA’s 4-bit format for Blackwell GPUs. Like MXFP4 it stores 4-bit floating point values, but it shares an FP8 scale across every 16 values instead of 32 and adds a per-tensor FP32 scale, for about 4.5 bits per weight. NVIDIA’s NVFP4 write-up describes it as about 3.5 times smaller than FP16 and reports an accuracy loss of 1% or less against FP8 on DeepSeek-R1-0528. ## How Floating-Point Precision Works: FP32, FP16, BF16, INT8, INT4
FP32 uses 32 bits per weight: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 mantissa bits. This is full precision — the training default — but at 4 bytes per parameter, a 7B model consumes 28 GB.
INT8 (Q8_0) uses 8 bits (~1 byte per parameter). A 7B model fits in ~7 GB. The key innovation here, as Tim Dettmers showed with LLM.int8(), is that naive INT8 quantization causes catastrophic quality loss because ~0.1% of weights — outlier feature channels in attention layers — have magnitudes that blow up the quantization error. LLM.int8() solves this with mixed-precision decomposition: those outlier channels stay in FP16 while the rest goes to INT8. This is why Q8_0 in GGUF is effectively lossless while naive INT8 is not.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means re-storing the same weights with fewer bits. Nothing is deleted and the architecture is unchanged — each of the seventy billion parameters is still there, it's just described less precisely. In practice you take a small block of weights, find its range, and store a shared scale factor (plus a zero-point offset in asymmetric schemes) for that block, with each weight kept as a low-precision integer or a 4-bit float. At inference the integer is multiplied back out to a float on the fly, which is why you get the memory savings without the math silently going wrong.

For the memory, the rule of thumb is parameters times bits per weight, divided by eight. So a 70-billion model in 16-bit, which is how open models are usually released, needs about 140 gigabytes just for weights. At a nominal four bits that's 35 gigabytes, and at a nominal two bits it's 17.5 gigabytes — exactly a quarter and an eighth of the original. The catch is that real quantized files are a bit heavier than the name suggests, because those block scales take space and most good formats keep sensitive layers at higher precision. A GGUF Q4_K_M of a 70B model works out to roughly 4.5 bits per weight, call it 39 gigabytes. Q2_K lands nearer 2.5 to 2.6 bits, so around 22 to 23 gigabytes rather than 17.5. You also need room on top for the key-value cache and activations, which typically adds half a gigabyte to a couple, and grows with context length.

The trade-off is accuracy, and it is not linear. Published benchmarks on a 70-billion model show 8-bit being essentially indistinguishable from 16-bit, four-bit costing about one to two percent worse perplexity, and two-bit costing around eleven percent. What's more telling is that reasoning tasks fall apart faster than perplexity does: on grade-school math problems, four-bit loses roughly one and a half accuracy points while two-bit loses around fourteen. So a two-bit 70B model will chat acceptably but is a poor choice for coding, tool use, or agent work, and the advice in most of the local-model literature is not to go below four-bit for anything you rely on.

There's a second, subtler trade-off. A tiny fraction of weights — outlier channels, on the order of a tenth of a percent, mostly in attention layers — have much larger magnitudes than the rest, and naive low-bit quantization wrecks them. That's why plain 2-bit GPTQ can degrade to the point of not producing coherent text, while methods like SpQR stay usable at 2 bits by keeping those few outliers at 16-bit precision. Good low-bit formats spend part of their budget on that bookkeeping, which is exactly why the effective bits-per-weight exceeds the label.

The upside that makes all this worthwhile is speed. Inference on consumer hardware is usually bottlenecked by how fast weights stream across memory, so a model that's four times smaller runs several times faster — one benchmark measured a 70B model at 4-bit running nearly four times faster than 16-bit on the same machine. And it turns a model that needs a datacenter card into one that fits on a workstation or a high-end consumer GPU. One practical caveat: a few newer 4-bit floating-point formats, like NVIDIA's NVFP4, need recent Blackwell hardware for native FP4 math, so it's worth checking your runtime before picking one of those.
