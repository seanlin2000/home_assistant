ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future, and the interest rate is what determines how heavily you discount that future.

A dollar you receive years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar can be invested and grow. So to value a business, you take its expected future cash flows and divide each one by a discount rate, roughly the risk-free rate plus a premium for the risk of owning stocks. When the Fed raises rates, Treasury yields rise, the risk-free part of that discount rate rises, and every future dollar gets worth less in today's terms. The higher the rate, the steeper the haircut.

What matters most is *when* those dollars arrive. A company earning most of its cash next year is barely affected. A company whose earnings are expected to arrive mostly a decade from now is hit hard. That is why tech and other growth stocks are sometimes called long-duration assets, by analogy to a long-dated bond: their value is concentrated in far-off cash flows, exactly the ones discounting punishes most.

A simple illustration. Say you expect one hundred dollars ten years out. Discounted at three percent, it's worth about seventy-four dollars today. At five percent, about sixty-one. A two-percentage-point move in the rate cuts that value by roughly seventeen and a half percent, even though nothing changed about the business or its cash flow. For a distant, uncertain dollar, a bigger rate cut in the discount rate can wipe out most of the present value.

Growth stocks get hit twice. First, the discount-rate effect I just described shrinks the value of everything they will earn later. Second, higher rates raise the yields on safe alternatives, so investors demand a bigger reward for holding risky equities, which widens the equity risk premium and pushes the discount rate up further. In a valuation formula that divides a next year's cash flow by the discount rate minus the growth rate, the effect is brutal because the denominator is small. If the rate is nine percent and growth six percent, you divide by three points, and a hundred dollars of cash flow is worth about thirty-three hundred. If rates push the discount rate to eleven percent, you're dividing by five, and the same cash flow is worth two thousand. A two-point rate move cut the value by forty percent.

There are second-order effects too. Higher rates mean higher borrowing costs, and a lot of tech growth has historically been financed with cheap debt, including buybacks funded by borrowing. Cheaper consumer spending and tighter venture funding slow revenue growth, which feeds back into the cash flows you were discounting. Some tech firms with big cash piles actually earn more interest income, and profitable, cash-generating companies with pricing power suffer far less than unprofitable ones promising profits someday.

That last point explains the asymmetry you'd expect in real markets: the damage isn't uniform. A company generating strong free cash flow right now is much less sensitive than one that's years from profitability, because the latter's entire valuation is the distant, heavily discounted promise. And if the Fed is hiking because the economy is running hot and revenues are strong, that can offset some of the discounting, which is why rate rises don't always produce a clean selloff. But the mechanical pull, all else equal, is downward, and it pulls hardest on the longest-duration stories.
