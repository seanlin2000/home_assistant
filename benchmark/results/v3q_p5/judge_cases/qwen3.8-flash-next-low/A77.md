Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to one simple idea: the air in our atmosphere acts like a giant net that catches some colors of sunlight and lets others slip right through.

Start with the sun. Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mixture of every color — the same rainbow you see in a soap bubble or a patch of spray on a lawn. Each color is a wave of light, and the colors differ in how tightly packed those waves are. Red light has long, lazy, stretched-out waves. Blue and violet light have short, choppy, tightly bunched waves.

Now picture that sunlight hitting the air. The atmosphere is packed with tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, billions of them, far smaller than a speck of dust. When light smacks into them, it bounces off in all directions — scattered. Here's the catch: short, choppy waves like blue hit those molecules easily and get scattered in every which direction, over and over, across the whole sky. Long, lazy waves like red tend to ignore them and keep plowing straight ahead.

So when you look at the sky, away from the sun, you're seeing all the blue light that's been bounced around and sprayed toward your eyes from every direction. The whole dome above you is glowing with scattered blue. You might wonder why it isn't violet, since violet scatters even more than blue. The sun gives off less violet to begin with, our eyes are much less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets soaked up higher in the atmosphere. Together, those things leave blue as the color our brains settle on.

Sunset is the same physics, just with the geometry changed. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a huge, slanted slice of atmosphere to reach you — many times more air than when the sun is overhead. On that long trip, almost all the blue and violet light gets scattered out of the beam before it ever gets to you, thrown off to the sides and away. What's left surviving that long journey is the red, orange, and yellow light, the colors that slip through the air stubbornly in a straight line. That's what you see pouring out of the sun.

The dramatic colors spreading across the sky are what that remaining red and orange light does on its way past the clouds, dust, smoke, and pollution floating in the lower air. Those bigger particles reflect and glow with the long-wavelength light, which is why a sunset after a dusty or smoky day tends to be the most vivid one you'll see.

So the short version: blue is what the air throws around, red is what the air lets through. During the day you see the scattered stuff; at sunset, with the light forced to cross so much more air, all the scattered stuff has been filtered out and you're left looking at what survived the trip.
