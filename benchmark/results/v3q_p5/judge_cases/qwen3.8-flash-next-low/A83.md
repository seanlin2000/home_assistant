ASSISTANT: Here's how it works out.

At a sixty million dollar post-money valuation, your twenty thousand dollars buys about a thirty-three hundredths of one percent of the company — roughly zero point zero three three percent of the fully diluted equity.

Fifty percent cumulative dilution means your stake is cut in half, so you end up holding about zero point zero one six seven percent when the company sells. Applied to a one billion dollar exit, that comes to about one hundred sixty-six thousand six hundred seventy dollars before taxes.

Your multiple is about eight point three times your money, and your profit is roughly one hundred forty-six thousand six hundred seventy dollars.

Two caveats worth naming. The eight-point-three-times figure is really just the one-billion exit divided by the original sixty million valuation — eight-point-three-three — and the dilution happens to cancel out against the size of the increase only in the sense that your ownership halves while the value multiplies. If the "fifty percent dilution" instead means the founders and later investors took fifty percent of the company overall rather than halving your specific stake, the answer changes, so it depends on how that dilution is defined. Also, this ignores preferred liquidation preferences from later rounds, which in a one billion dollar sale typically get paid out first and can reduce what's left for a small common or non-preferred holder like you.
