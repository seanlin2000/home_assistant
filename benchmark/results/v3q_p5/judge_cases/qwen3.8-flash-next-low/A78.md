Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The stock market reflects expected profits of large, often multinational companies, and stock ownership is concentrated (in the US the richest tenth of households own the large majority of stocks), while people feel the economy through wages, prices, rent and jobs. Three concrete examples from any of: prices rising faster than wages while companies keep or raise margins; a handful of giant tech firms driving index gains while most businesses and regions lag; companies earning much of their profit abroad; cost cutting and layoffs that raise profits and share prices; low rates or stimulus inflating asset prices, which helps people who own assets (the K-shaped recovery of 2020 and 2021); housing costs climbing while the index climbs. Must give three distinct, concrete mechanisms, not three restatements of "the market is not the economy".

U.S. consumer sentiment rose in early November as households became somewhat less pessimistic about the economy and their finances, while inflation expectations eased. The University of Michigan’s preliminary sentiment index increased to 51.0 from October’s final reading of 48.0, a three-point gain that offered a measure of relief after several months of weak confidence.
## Improvement was uneven across households
Both main components of the survey moved higher. The Current Economic Conditions index rose to 51.0 from 46.7 in October, a 4.3-point increase, while the Index of Consumer Expectations advanced to 50.9 from 48.8, a gain of 2.1 points. The two measures capture different questions: how consumers judge conditions they face now and how they assess the outlook for their own finances and the broader economy.
Survey director Joanne Hsu said the improvement reflected modest gains in several areas, including expectations for personal finances and business conditions. Much of the change was concentrated among respondents identifying as political independents, rather than being spread uniformly across political groups.
## Inflation expectations eased, but the price burden remained
The October survey had already shown how affordability concerns were affecting major purchases. Buying conditions for durable goods fell sharply that month as respondents pointed to high prices and borrowing costs. November’s stronger overall reading does not by itself show that those pressures on large household purchases have disappeared, particularly when so many consumers still report an erosion of their living standards.
## Early interviews leave room for the final November reading to change
Hsu also reported growing concern among respondents about a possible artificial intelligence bubble. That worry was present alongside volatility in financial markets, particularly for households with large investment portfolios. Still, the university said the discussion of AI was much less prominent than concern about the everyday cost of living. The survey does not establish that worries about AI were the cause of the drop in sentiment among the biggest stockholders.

## How do you read the S&P 500?
Because the FRED series is price-only, reading it for total wealth requires a mental adjustment: add back roughly a dividend yield each year. That is also why the index level can sit below its inflation-adjusted prior peak for long stretches even when a dividend-reinvesting investor has fully recovered. For valuation context, the level is best paired with an earnings-based gauge such as the Shiller PE Ratio rather than read in isolation.
## How is the S&P 500 measured and reported?
MacroRadar sources the index level from FRED under the series SP500, which publishes the daily closing value. A quirk worth knowing is that FRED's SP500 series carries only a rolling ten-year window of daily closes rather than the full multi-decade history, a licensing constraint rather than a data gap. For longer-run equity context, MacroRadar's regime and base-rate work relies on total-return series that extend much further back.
The methodology caveat that matters most is price versus total return. The reported level is the price index: it reflects share prices weighted by float-adjusted market cap, with no dividends added. A total-return version reinvests dividends and grows meaningfully faster over time. The index is also reconstituted as companies are added and removed, so it is a measure of the large-cap segment rather than a fixed basket, and it is not adjusted for inflation in nominal form.
## How does the S&P 500 relate to MacroRadar's other charts?
Together these four — the index level, the Nasdaq, the CAPE, and the dividend yield — form the equity and valuation complex on MacroRadar. The dividend yield in particular explains the gap between the price-only level shown here and the total return an investor actually earns, which is the single most important adjustment when interpreting this chart over long horizons.

## DFA: Distributional Financial Accounts
### Distributional Financial Accounts Overview
The Distributional Financial Accounts (DFAs) provide quarterly estimates of the distribution of a comprehensive measure of U.S. household wealth, beginning with the third quarter of 1989 and through the most recent quarter.
These interactive charts and tables allow users to explore the level, composition, and share of U.S. household wealth held by five percentile groups of wealth: the top 0.1 percent, the remaining 0.9 percent of the top 1, the next 9 percent (i.e., 90th to 99th percentile), the next 40 percent (50th to 90th percentile), and the bottom half (below the 50th percentile). Additionally, the data set contains the level and share of aggregate household wealth by income, age, generation, education, and race.
#### Interactive Data Visualizations
Explore the Distribution of Wealth Over Time
These charts and tables explore the level and the share of aggregate U.S. wealth (as well as selected assets and liabilities) held by various subgroups of the U.S. population.
#### Documentation
The DFAs integrate two data products produced by the Federal Reserve Board: the Financial Accounts of the United States, which provide quarterly data on aggregate balance sheets of major sectors of the U.S. economy, and the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), which provides comprehensive triennial microdata on the assets and liabilities of a representative sample of U.S. households.
