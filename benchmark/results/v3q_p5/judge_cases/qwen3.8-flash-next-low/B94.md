# Swiss Travel Pass vs. Half Fare Card: Which Saves More Money?
## Quick summary
- Visiting for under a week with 3–5 big travel days? The Swiss Travel Pass usually wins.
- Staying longer, with a home base and day trips? Swiss Half Fare Card (CHF 150 / month) plus Saver Day Passes almost always wins.
- Living here? You can't buy the visitor products. Choose between the Half Fare Travelcard (CHF 190, then CHF 170) and the GA Travelcard (CHF 3,995).
- Rough break-even: about CHF 300 of full-fare travel per pass day.
- Children 6–15 travel free with an accompanying parent thanks to the Swiss Family Card — get this right and the maths changes completely.
For the reference version — ticket types, apps and how the Swiss fare system is built — see our train tickets and passes guide.
## 1. First, which products are you even allowed to buy?
This trips people up constantly. Swiss public transport has two parallel product ranges, and your residence decides which one applies to you.

- Unlimited travel on trains, Postbuses and lake boats across the national network.
- Local trams, buses and S-Bahn in more than 90 towns and cities.
- Free admission to over 500 museums.
- Free travel on the Rigi, Stanserhorn and Stoos mountain railways.
- Up to 50% off most other cableways, funiculars and RailAway excursion packages.
- Panoramic routes — Glacier Express, Bernina Express, GoldenPass, Gotthard Panorama Express — included on the rail portion, but seat reservations and premium-class surcharges are extra and mandatory on some of them .
- Up to 50% off luggage registration.

## 3. The Half Fare Card: what you actually get
The Swiss Half Fare Card is not a travel pass — it's a discount licence. For CHF 150 you get one month in which every ticket you buy costs roughly half. That includes point-to-point tickets, day passes, Saver Day Passes, most mountain railways and boats, and public transport in 90+ cities.
Two things make it stronger than the price suggests:
- It stacks with Saver Day Passes. A Saver Day Pass costs from CHF 52 without a half-fare card and from CHF 29 with one. Two Saver Day Passes at CHF 29 gets you two unlimited travel days for CHF 58.
- It stacks with Supersaver tickets. Advance-purchase discounts of 20–70% on specific trains apply*on top* of the half-fare reduction.
The catch is admin: you have to plan, book ahead, and check quotas. Saver prices are limited in number and the CHF 29 tier disappears first on weekends and holidays. If your idea of a holiday is deciding at breakfast where to go, that friction has a real cost.
Residents get the equivalent Half Fare Travelcard — CHF 190 for a first year, CHF 170 from the second, or CHF 120 / CHF 100 for 16–24 year-olds. SBB's own break-even example: four Bern–Zurich returns in 2nd class and it has paid for itself.

## What is the Gornergrat Railway?
The Gornergrat Railway (Gornergrat Bahn, GGB) is a cogwheel railway that climbs from Zermatt to the Gornergrat ridge in about 33 minutes, and it has run since 1898. It was built purely for sightseers, to carry visitors up to a ridge facing the Matterhorn and the Monte Rosa massif, and that is still what it does more than 125 years later.
### When did the Gornergrat Railway open, and how old is it?
The first train ran on 20 August 1898, after two years of construction. At first it ran only in summer. Year-round operation came in stages during the first half of the 20th century: winter service reached Riffelberg in 1940/41 and the summit around 1941, though some sources date the first year-round running to 1929. The summit station was moved about 71 metres higher in 1909, to its present position.
### Is it a cogwheel or a rack railway, and how does it work?
They are the same thing. “Cogwheel railway”, “rack railway” and “cog railway” all describe a train that drives a toothed wheel into a toothed rail between the tracks, which lets it climb gradients ordinary wheels could never grip. The Gornergrat uses the Abt rack system along its whole length, on metre-gauge track. It is also one of a handful of railways in the world that still run on three-phase electric power: that is why each train has two pantographs and there are two overhead wires. Our guide to how cogwheel railways work covers the rack systems, the snow clearing and the other Swiss rack lines.
### Is the Gornergrat the highest railway in Europe?
No, and the claim is repeated wrongly in many places. The Jungfrau Railway to Jungfraujoch (3,454 m) is higher, but most of it runs through a tunnel. The accurate claim, and the one the operator uses, is that the Gornergrat is Europe’s highest open-air rack railway. Its summit station at 3,089 m is the highest open-air railway station in Europe, and Rotenboden, at 2,815 m, is the second highest.
