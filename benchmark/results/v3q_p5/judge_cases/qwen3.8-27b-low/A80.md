ASSISTANT: To quantize a model means to reduce the number of bits used to store each weight in place of the higher precision it was trained in, typically sixteen or eight bits. A weight that once needed sixteen bits to represent a value like 0.003417 is instead stored as a small integer plus a scale factor, so it only takes four or two bits. The model behaves a little differently because the weights are no longer exact, but the change is often small enough that quality holds up well, at least down to four bits.

For a seventy-billion parameter model, the arithmetic is straightforward. Each parameter at four bits is half a byte, so the weights need roughly twenty-eight gigabytes of memory. At two bits, a quarter of a byte each, that drops to about seventeen point five gigabytes. For comparison, the same weights at full sixteen-bit precision would sit around one hundred forty gigabytes, which is why quantization matters so much.

The trade-offs are worth being honest about. Going from sixteen or eight bits down to four is usually a gentle loss, and the model still runs on a single high-end machine. But two bits is a much sharper cut. At that level the quantization error piles up across tens of billions of parameters, and you typically need special schemes like mixed precision, where a few sensitive layers stay at higher resolution, plus fine-tuning or calibration to keep the model coherent. Without that care, a two-bit model degrades noticeably in reasoning and instruction-following, even if it still produces fluent-sounding text. There are also overhead costs that the raw numbers hide: the scale factors, codebooks, and any calibration data stored alongside the weights add a bit on top, and inference at low bit depths can be slower in practice because the hardware has to unpack the compressed weights. So you are trading memory footprint and speed against a real, and at two bits a substantial, loss in quality.
