Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: So here's the short version: sunlight actually looks white, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, just blended together. When that light hits the air around us, the tiny molecules in the atmosphere — mostly nitrogen and oxygen — bump into the light and scatter it in every direction. The trick is that shorter wavelengths, like blue and violet, get scattered way more than the longer ones, like red and orange. That's why when you look up at the sky during the day, you're mostly seeing blue light bouncing around from all the air in front of you.

Now at sunset, the sun is low on the horizon, so its light has to punch through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach your eyes. Most of that blue light gets scattered away before it ever gets to you. What's left to make it through is the red and orange stuff, which scatters less and can travel in a straighter line. So the sky near the horizon glows warm, and the clouds catch that same red light and turn pink or gold.

The violet end of the spectrum actually scatters even more than blue, but two things keep the sky from looking purple: the sun emits less violet than blue, and your eyes are much more sensitive to blue. So blue wins.

One small bonus: if there's a lot of dust or smoke in the air — after a volcano, after a forest fire, or just on a hazy day — it scatters the longer wavelengths too, which is why sunsets on smoky days can look especially deep red.
