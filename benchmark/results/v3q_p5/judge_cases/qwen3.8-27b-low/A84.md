Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a great question, and the core of it comes down to how we value anything that pays off in the future.

Think of a stock's value as the present value of all the cash it will ever generate for you. To get from a dollar ten years from now to what it's worth today, you discount it by some rate, and that rate is anchored to interest rates, particularly the risk-free rate on Treasury bonds. The formula is straightforward: present value equals the future cash flow divided by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of how many years away that cash flow falls.

Now here's the key insight. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and the further out in time a cash flow sits, the more brutally it gets shrunk. A dollar due in one year barely changes. A dollar due in twenty years changes a lot. If you double the discount rate from four percent to eight percent, a dollar in twenty years is worth roughly half of what it was before. The farther out, the steeper the cliff.

This is where the "long-duration" concept comes in, and it's borrowed directly from bond math. A bond that pays its principal in thirty years is called long-duration because its price is hypersensitive to interest rate moves. A bond maturing next year is short-duration and barely budges. Stocks have the same property. A mature company like Procter and Gamble, which pays a meaningful dividend today and grows earnings at a modest rate, is relatively short-duration. A lot of its value comes from cash it's generating right now. But a growth-stage tech company that's reinvesting everything, paying no dividend, and whose earnings are supposed to explode a decade from now is extremely long-duration. Maybe seventy or eighty percent of its market value represents cash flows that won't exist for fifteen or twenty years.

So when rates rise, those distant future cash flows get hit hardest, and the multiple the market is willing to pay for each dollar of future earnings compresses. You see this in practice: high-flying tech stocks with price-to-earnings ratios of forty or fifty can get sold off much more violently in a rising-rate environment than a steady utility or consumer staples stock.

There are a few secondary channels that reinforce this. Higher rates make bonds and high-yield savings accounts more attractive, so investors rotate out of risky growth stocks into fixed income. Higher borrowing costs squeeze the free cash flow of capital-intensive tech companies that rely on debt. And rising rates can slow economic activity, which dampens the revenue growth assumptions that are baked into those valuations in the first place.

The short version: tech stocks are expensive because they promise big payouts far in the future. Higher interest rates make "far in the future" worth less in today's money, so the whole valuation pyramid gets compressed from the top down.
