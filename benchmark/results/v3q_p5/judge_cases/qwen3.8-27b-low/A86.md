Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

ASSISTANT: Here's the shape I'd set it up, assuming a typical small apartment and the big three voice options.

Put the Hue Bridge on a wired Ethernet connection to your router and treat it as the single hub. Every bulb talks to the bridge over the local Zigbee mesh, and the bridge is what everyone else talks to. That local link is the reason most of this still works even without the internet, so keep the bridge on a power strip that won't get unplugged and on the same network as your phones and speakers.

For multiple people, don't have everyone log into your one account. Each person makes their own Hue account, and you invite them into your Home under Settings, Home access. You then give each of them a permission level — just the lights, or lights plus security, and so on. They each see the same rooms and scenes on their own phone, you can revoke anyone individually, and it works over your local Wi‑Fi. That app path is your most reliable control for everyone.

For voice, pick one ecosystem — Apple Home, Google Home, or Alexa — and connect the bridge to it once, rather than mixing several. Each person then uses their own account on their own HomePod, Echo, or Nest, and everyone can say "turn off the kitchen light." For the strongest voice experience across different people and devices, pair through Matter where you can, because Matter is the path most likely to give you local, cloud‑independent control. Also worth adding a couple of physical Hue dimmer or tap switches on the wall for dead‑simple on, off, and dim that never depend on a phone, an app, or the internet at all.

One practical limit: a single bridge tops out around fifty lights and accessories. If you're over that you'd want a second bridge, which you can now put under the same Hue account.

Now the part you asked about — the internet going down. The good news is the lights themselves don't go dumb. The bridge runs on your local network, so any app that's on the same Wi‑Fi can still turn lights on and off and change scenes, and the automations stored on the bridge keep firing locally. What you lose is out‑of‑the‑home control — you can't reach the lights from your phone on cell data when you're away.

Voice is the trickier case, and it depends on the path you set up. If your assistant is talking to Hue through the cloud integration — which is the default when you just link the skill in the Hue app — then voice commands generally stop working the moment the internet drops, even if the app still does. To keep voice alive during an outage you want local control, which means pairing over Matter or using HomeKit local control, with a hub speaker that's on your local Wi‑Fi. Even then I'd treat offline voice as "usually works, occasionally flaky" rather than guaranteed — Matter and HomeKit local control have had real‑world reliability hiccups in the past. The bulletproof fallback during an outage is the physical switches and the local app, which is exactly why I'd recommend both.

So the short version: bridge on Ethernet, one shared Home with per‑person invites, one voice ecosystem via Matter where possible, and physical switches as the safety net. That gives you multi‑user control that degrades gracefully instead of dying when the ISP blinks.
