USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended not with a single dramatic moment but through a cascade of events between 1989 and 1991 that dissolved both the Soviet bloc in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union itself. To understand it cleanly, it helps to separate the deep causes from the immediate triggers, and then to pick out three events that mattered most.

The long-term causes were structural. The Soviet command economy had been stagnating since the mid-seventies, unable to keep up with Western productivity, while the arms race and the war in Afghanistan were draining resources the system could no longer spare. Underneath all of that ran a web of ethnic and national tensions across the fifteen republics that the central government had managed by force for decades. None of these causes, by itself, made dissolution inevitable, but together they made the system brittle.

The three events I'd point to are these. First, Gorbachev's reforms, launched in 1985 and known as glasnost and perestroika. Glasnost loosened censorship and allowed public criticism of the regime for the first time since Stalin; perestroika attempted to introduce market mechanisms into the economy. These reforms were meant to save the Soviet system, not destroy it, but they removed the two pillars it had stood on: the monopoly on information and the monopoly on political power. Without that context, nothing else could have happened the way it did.

Second, the fall of the Berlin Wall on the ninth of November 1989, and the wider revolutions across Eastern Europe that same year. Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany all saw their communist governments fall within a few months. The critical thing was that Moscow did not intervene. The tanks that had crushed the Hungarian revolt in 1956 or the Prague Spring in 1968 stayed in their barracks, a policy journalists called the Sinatra Doctrine. That single decision told every republic inside the Soviet Union that central authority was no longer willing to back itself up with force.

Third, the failed coup in August 1991. A group of hardline officials within the Communist Party tried to arrest Gorbachev and reverse the reforms. The coup lasted roughly three days and collapsed, partly because it was poorly organized and partly because Boris Yeltsin, then president of the Russian republic, stood on a tank in Moscow and urged the public to resist. Its failure was fatal to the center. The republics, which had been watching nervously, saw that the central government could not even protect its own leader, and they moved quickly toward formal independence. Within four months, in December 1991, the union was formally dissolved and Gorbachev resigned.

So to draw the line between causes and triggers: the economic stagnation, the ethnic tensions, and the ideological exhaustion were the causes. They set the stage. Gorbachev's reforms were the cause that made the triggers possible, because they removed the repression that had been holding everything together. The fall of the Berlin Wall and the 1989 revolutions were the first immediate triggers, because they demonstrated that the Soviet Union would no longer act as a police power. The August coup was the final trigger, because it destroyed whatever remained of the center's authority and made the republics' independence not just possible but, in their calculation, costless. The dissolution in December 1991 was less a decision than a formality that caught up with what had already happened on the ground.
