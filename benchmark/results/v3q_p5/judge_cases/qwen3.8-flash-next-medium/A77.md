Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to one idea: sunlight isn't really white, and the air is picky about which colors it lets through.

Sunlight looks white, but it's a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow. Each color is a different wavelength of light — red is the long, lazy kind, blue and violet are the short, twitchy kind. Now imagine the atmosphere as a huge room filled with nitrogen and oxygen molecules, which are tiny — far smaller than a wavelength of visible light. When sunlight runs into those molecules, the light bounces off them in every direction. That bouncing is called scattering.

Here's the picky part: the shorter, twitchy wavelengths bounce far more easily than the long ones. It isn't a little bit more — it's dramatically more. The amount of scattering goes up as you shorten the wavelength, and the effect is compounded four times over. So compare red light at about 650 nanometers to blue light at about 450: I worked that ratio out, and blue gets scattered roughly four times more strongly than red. Violet is scattered even more than blue, but we don't see a violet sky because the sun gives off less violet to begin with, and our eyes are much less sensitive to violet than to blue.

So during the day, sunlight hits the upper atmosphere and the blue component gets flung in every direction, including straight down toward you. No matter which way you look up, you're catching some of that sprayed-around blue. The reds and yellows mostly shoulder their way through the air untouched. The sky looks blue because you're seeing sunlight that has been knocked sideways.

At sunset the geometry changes, and that's the whole story. When the sun is overhead, sunlight crosses a relatively thin slab of air to reach your eyes — a few miles, roughly. When the sun is near the horizon, that same light has to travel along through the atmosphere at a shallow angle, and it passes through something like ten to forty times more air on its way to you. All that extra air does to the blue light is exhaust it: it gets scattered so many times that by the time the beam arrives, the blues and violets have been scraped out of the direct path and scattered off somewhere you're not looking. What survives the long haul is the red, orange and yellow that the air barely bothered at all. So the sun itself, and the clouds and haze near it, look like the leftover warm colors.

Two other things fill in the details. The orange and red you see *away* from the sun, painting the clouds and the far horizon, is that stripped-out light bouncing around the upper atmosphere and coming back down to you, tinted. And sunsets look more dramatic after a day with dust, smoke, pollution or volcanic haze in the air, because bigger particles knock light around even more effectively than the molecules alone, which strips out more blue and gives the warm colors more material to glow on.

One thing worth saying, because it's a common mix-up: the red at sunset is not the sky changing color, and it isn't reflection off the ocean or the ground. It's the same sunlight, but with the blue removed by distance. Same light, longer road, different leftovers.
