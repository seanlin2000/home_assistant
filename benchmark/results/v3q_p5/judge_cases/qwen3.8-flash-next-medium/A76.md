On 26 December 1991, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved and ceased to exist as a sovereign state. It also brought an end to the Soviet Union's federal government and to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's efforts to reform the Soviet political and economic system in an attempt to stop a period of political and economic stagnation.
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 resulted from several factors: chronic economic stagnation, the unsustainable financial burden of the arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, intense ethnic nationalism and separatism within its union republics, and the destabilizing effects of Gorbachev's reforms (particularly glasnost and perestroika).
The process began with growing unrest in the country's union republics developing into an incessant political and legislative conflict between them and the central government. Until its final years, the Soviet Union was made up of 15 union republics that served as the homelands of different ethnic groups. Estonia was the first union republic to declare state sovereignty on 16 November 1988. Lithuania was the first republic to declare independence, in March 1990.

## End of the Cold War
After the end of the Revolutions of 1989, Gorbachev and President Bush Sr. met on the neutral island of Malta to discuss the events of the year, the withdrawal of the Soviet military from Eastern Europe, and the future course of their relationship. After their discussions, the two leaders publicly announced they would work together for German reunification, the normalization of relations, the resolution of Third World conflicts, and the promotion of peace and democracy (referred to by President Bush as a "New World Order").
As a consequence of the Revolutions of 1989 and the adoption of a foreign policy based on non-interference by the Soviet Union, the Warsaw Pact rapidly lost its political and military rationale and began to dissolve. Meanwhile, troops stationed across eastern Europe began returning to the Soviet Union, completing their withdrawal by the mid-1990s. This marked the end of the Soviet military presence that had defined the political order in Europe since 1945.

While the U.S. military buildup increased the costs of confrontation, Reagan’s turn toward cooperation helped reduce superpower tensions. European leaders consistently pressed for arms reduction, but the decisive change came with Gorbachev's post-Brezhnev leadership. His reforms, withdrawal from Afghanistan, and vision of a "common European home" signaled a new global commitment to disarmament. ### Causes
Scholars have pointed to materialist and ideational reasons for the end of the Cold War. Materialists emphasize Soviet economic difficulties (such as economic stagnation and sovereign debt), whereas ideationalists argue that the worldviews and personas of Gorbachev and Reagan mattered. Ideationalists point to a Gorbachev and Reagan's mutual desire to abolish nuclear weapons, as well as Gorbachev's perceptions of foreign policy. To this end, Gorbachev's re-conceptualization of security—emphasizing mutual restraint, political choice, and non-coercion—proved central to ending the Cold War.

Between 18 and 22 August 1991, amidst the dissolution of the Soviet Union, a hardliner group of top military and civilian officials within the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) attempted to forcibly seize power from Mikhail Gorbachev, who had been serving as the Soviet Union's president since March 1990 and as the CPSU's General Secretary since March 1985. Among the leaders of what is also known as the August Coup was Gennady Yanayev, who had been serving as the Soviet Union's vice president since December 1990.
Yanayev and his allies, who together formed the State Committee on the State of Emergency (Russian: ГКЧП, romanized: GKChP), had been motivated by their opposition to Gorbachev's political reforms, the collapse of Soviet hegemony over Eastern Europe, and the proposed New Union Treaty, which was to distribute much of the heavily centralized power of the Soviet government among each of the fifteen Union Republics. The initiative for de-centralization and increased autonomy had been spurred by Boris Yeltsin while he served as the Chairman of the Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR until July 1991; Yeltsin's demand opened a window for the GKChP to plan the coup d'état attempt.

## Aftermath
### The Dissolution of the Soviet Union
On 8 December, Boris Yeltsin, Leonid Kravchuk and Stanislav Shushkevich – the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus (which had adopted the name in August 1991) – as well as the prime ministers of the three republics, met in Minsk, Belarus, to sign the Belovezha Accords. The accords declared that the Soviet Union had ceased to exist "as a subject of international law and geopolitical reality". It repudiated the 1922 union treaty that established the Soviet Union and established the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS) in the Union's place. On 12 December, the Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR ratified the accords and recalled the Russian deputies from the Supreme Soviet of the USSR. Although this has been interpreted as the moment that Russia seceded from the union, Russia took the position that it was not possible to secede from a state that no longer existed. The lower chamber of the Supreme Soviet, the Council of the Union, was forced to halt its operations, as the departure of the Russian deputies left it without a quorum.
Doubts remained about the legitimacy of the 8 December accords, since only three republics took part. Thus, on 21 December in Alma-Ata, the Alma-Ata Protocol expanded the CIS to include Armenia, Azerbaijan, Moldova and the five republics of Central Asia. They also preemptively accepted Gorbachev's resignation. With 11 of the 12 remaining republics (all except Georgia) having agreed that the Union no longer existed, Gorbachev bowed to the inevitable and said he would resign as soon as the CIS became a reality (Georgia joined the CIS in 1993, only to withdraw in 2008 after its war with Russia; the three Baltic states were never a part of the commonwealth, instead joining both the European Union and NATO in 2004.)
On 25 December, Gorbachev announced his resignation as President of the Soviet Union. The red hammer and sickle flag of the Soviet Union was lowered from the Senate building in the Kremlin and replaced with the tricolour flag of Russia. The next day, 26 December, the Soviet of Republics, the upper chamber of the Supreme Soviet, formally voted the Soviet Union out of existence (the lower chamber, the Council of the Union, had been left without a quorum after the Russian deputies withdrew), thus ending the life of the world's first and oldest socialist state. All former Soviet embassies became Russian embassies, and Russia received all nuclear weapons located in other former republics by 1996. A constitutional crisis in late 1993 escalated into violence, and the new Russian constitution that came into force at the end of the year abolished the last vestiges of the Soviet political system.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that the Cold War ended because the Soviet Union stopped being able to afford it, and then stopped being able to hold itself together. Two things that look like one event are actually separate: the end of the confrontation with the West, which was largely settled by 1989 to 1991, and the death of the Soviet state itself, which happened in December 1991.

Three events stand out.

The first is Gorbachev's reform program beginning in 1985, and especially his foreign policy turn. He took power believing the Brezhnev-era stagnation could be fixed through perestroika, meaning restructuring, and glasnost, meaning openness. Crucially, he also rethought security itself, emphasizing mutual restraint and non-coercion rather than threat, and he abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, which had committed the USSR to invading any satellite state whose Communist government was threatened. That single reversal is what made everything after it possible. Alongside Reagan, over four summits between 1985 and 1988 he negotiated the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, which eliminated an entire class of nuclear weapons for the first time in the conflict's history, and he pulled Soviet troops out of Afghanistan, completed in early 1989.

The second event is the year 1989 in Eastern Europe. Popular revolutions swept away Communist governments in Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and Romania, with Hungary's border opening at the Pan-European Picnic in August helping set the chain in motion, and the Berlin Wall falling on November 9. Gorbachev and Bush Sr. then met at Malta and announced a shared agenda for German reunification and a "new world order." The Warsaw Pact lost its entire political rationale and began dissolving, and Soviet troops started going home, a withdrawal that finished by the mid-1990s. That is really the moment the Cold War ended as a geopolitical condition, even though the Soviet Union still existed for another two years.

The third event is the failed August coup of 1991, and what followed. Hardliners in the party, military and security apparatus formed the State Committee on the State of Emergency and tried to seize power from Gorbachev between August 18 and 22, motivated by his reforms, the loss of Eastern Europe, and above all the proposed New Union Treaty that would have devolved real power to the fifteen republics. The coup collapsed within days, but it destroyed what little authority the center still had and made Boris Yeltsin, who stood on the steps of the Russian parliament building against it, the dominant figure. On December 8 the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus met near Minsk and signed the Belovezha Accords, declaring that the Soviet Union had ceased to exist as a subject of international law and repudiating the 1922 treaty that had created it. On December 21 the Alma-Ata Protocol brought in Armenia, Azerbaijan, Moldova and the five Central Asian republics, expanding the Commonwealth of Independent States and preemptively accepting Gorbachev's resignation. He resigned on December 25, the hammer and sickle came down from the Kremlin, and on December 26 the upper chamber of the Supreme Soviet formally voted the country out of existence.

Now the distinction you asked for. The causes were long-run and structural: decades of economic stagnation; an arms race the Soviet economy could not match, made worse by Reagan's military buildup and by SDI, which Soviet leaders realized they could neither match technologically nor fund; the drain of foreign commitments like Afghanistan; deep ethnic nationalism inside the union republics, which glasnost gave a voice and which started early, with Estonia declaring state sovereignty in November 1988 and Lithuania declaring independence in March 1990; the Communist Party's loss of legitimacy, made visible when its 28th congress in July 1990 formally gave up its monopoly on power; and the reforms themselves, which loosened controls the system needed to survive without actually reviving the economy. That last point is the paradox at the heart of it: the attempt to save the Soviet Union is what dismantled it.

The immediate triggers were the short-fuse things that determined the timing and the shape of the collapse: the collapse of Soviet power in Eastern Europe in 1989, which removed the empire that justified the center; the non-interference guarantee that meant no one would use force to hold the periphery in place; the New Union Treaty debate, which opened the window the hardliners tried to slam shut; the failed coup, which discredited the center and shifted real power to Yeltsin's Russia; and then the Belovezha Accords and the Alma-Ata Protocol, which were the actual legal instruments of dissolution. Ukraine's independence referendum in December 1991 is usually counted too, since it removed any plausible way the union could be reconstituted with Ukraine inside it.

A useful way to hold it in mind: the causes explain why the Soviet Union was brittle, while the triggers explain why it broke all at once in the space of four months rather than fading gradually. Worth adding, since it explains a lot about the present, is that the dissolution was widely mourned rather than celebrated at the time. Levada polling in Russia has consistently found a majority of Russians regretting the collapse, including sixty-six percent in 2018, and Gallup found the same pattern in Armenia, Kyrgyzstan and Ukraine as of 2013.
