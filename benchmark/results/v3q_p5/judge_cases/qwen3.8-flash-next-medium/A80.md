## What You Actually Lose
First, the curve isn’t linear. Going 16 → 8 → 4 bits costs almost nothing each step. Going 4 → 3 → 2 costs a great deal, and saves less memory than you’d hope, because mixed precision means Q2_K is really about 3 bits per weight, not 2.
## The Rule That Matters Most
At a fixed memory budget, a bigger model quantized harder beats a smaller model quantized lightly.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means replacing the numbers that make up its weights with lower-precision stand-ins. A trained model normally stores each weight as a 16-bit float, roughly 6 or 7 significant digits of value. Quantization rounds those values onto a much coarser grid — with 4 bits you only get 16 possible levels per weight, with 2 bits just 4 levels — and stores, alongside them, a small amount of bookkeeping that maps those coarse levels back onto the real number range. So the model keeps its shape and its 70 billion parameters; it just describes each parameter less precisely.

The memory arithmetic is simple. At 16 bits per weight, 70 billion parameters is 140 gigabytes. At 4 bits, a quarter of that, about 35 gigabytes. At 2 bits, an eighth, about 17.5 gigabytes. Real quantized files are a bit larger than that, because the scaling data and the practice of leaving some layers at higher precision add overhead — in the GGUF format that home users commonly use, a Q4 70B model lands around 40 gigabytes rather than 35, and a Q2 70B model is closer to 26 to 30 gigabytes. That last point is worth noting: formats labeled "2-bit" often end up averaging nearer 3 bits per weight, so 2-bit saves less memory than the label implies.

The trade-offs break down fairly cleanly. Going from 16 to 8 bits costs essentially nothing in quality, and 4-bit is now widely treated as the practical floor for serious use: published evaluations of quantization strategies find that 4-bit models hold performance close to the unquantized originals on most benchmarks, with perplexity increases typically under 2 percent. Below 4 bits the curve falls off sharply. At 3 bits, degradation becomes visible in reasoning and multi-step tasks even when perplexity still looks tolerable. At 2 bits, standard methods like GPTQ can damage a model badly enough that it stops following instructions and produces incoherent text, with perplexity blowing up by orders of magnitude. Newer outlier-aware methods such as SpQR can make 2-bit workable by keeping the handful of unusually large weights at full precision, but 2-bit is not something you would pick for production.

Beyond raw accuracy, there are practical costs. Quantizing well requires a representative calibration sample, so a 4-bit model tuned on the wrong kind of text can underperform in your actual use case. Some formats add dequantization overhead that can make inference slower rather than faster, though on the whole the win goes the other way, since language-model inference is usually limited by how fast weights can be read out of memory. And weight quantization does nothing about the KV cache, which grows with context length and can quietly dominate your memory budget unless you shorten the context or quantize the cache separately.

The general rule people converge on is this: for a fixed memory budget, a larger model quantized harder usually beats a smaller model quantized lightly — but only down to roughly 3 bits, below which the damage outweighs the benefit of having more parameters, and you are better off running a smaller model at 4-bit.
