The choppy audio is almost certainly a radio problem, not a speaker problem. Bluetooth lives in the 2.4 gigahertz band, and your laptop is the one thing in your setup that saturates that band. Three sources keep showing up in the troubleshooting guides. First, 2.4 gigahertz Wi‑Fi: many laptops share a single antenna between Wi‑Fi and Bluetooth, so heavy network activity eats the airtime Bluetooth needs. That's why stutter often appears during downloads, game updates, or streaming, and it's why people notice their headphones work fine on a phone but choppy on a laptop. Second, USB 3.0. Unshielded SuperSpeed USB ports, hubs and cables leak broadband noise right into the 2.4 to 2.5 gigahertz range, which Intel has documented. One lab test I read measured a Bluetooth packet error rate jumping from about 0.02 percent to nearly 13 percent with a USB 3.0 SSD sitting ten centimeters from the laptop's antenna. Third, power management: Windows will power down or throttle the Bluetooth adapter when it thinks the machine is idle, which produces dropouts during quiet passages. Codec choice matters too, since a high-bitrate codec like LDAC at 990 kilobits per second collapses in a noisy band, where AAC or SBC keeps limping along.

So the systematic version goes like this, roughly five minutes each. Turn the laptop's Wi‑Fi off completely and play something. If the stutter vanishes, interference is confirmed, and the fix is to join the laptop to a 5 or 6 gigahertz network, and to turn off 40 or 80 megahertz channel width on the 2.4 band if you have devices that must stay there. Next, unplug every USB 3.0 device, hub and external drive, then reintroduce them one at a time and note which one brings the stutter back. If it's a hub or drive, physically separate it from the laptop's antenna, which on a laptop is usually up by the hinge or the top bezel, and put any Bluetooth dongle in a black USB 2.0 port or on a short extension cable about a meter away. Third, go into Device Manager and uncheck "allow the computer to turn off this device" for the Bluetooth adapter, and turn off USB selective suspend in your power plan. Fourth, confirm the speaker isn't negotiating the hands‑free profile, which drops audio quality badly on Windows, and check whether your laptop and the JBL are using LDAC; if so, switch the codec to AAC or SBC and see if stability improves. Fifth, rule out the hardware: if everything above fails, a ten to twenty dollar USB Bluetooth 5.3 adapter with its own antenna is a cheap test, because budget laptops sometimes route Bluetooth through a controller that shares bandwidth with USB, and an external adapter sidesteps that entirely.
