Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is the present value of all the cash it will earn in the future, and the interest rate is the discount you apply to that future money. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate rises, and the same future dollars are worth less today.

That effect is tiny for a company whose profits arrive next year, but it is large for a company whose profits are mostly far out in the future. Here is the arithmetic. A hundred dollars promised ten years from now is worth about sixty-one dollars if you discount at five percent, but only about forty-six dollars at eight percent. That's a twenty-five percent hit to value from three points of discount rate, and nothing changed about the company's business.

That is why the comparison to bonds is so useful. A long-duration bond is all of its cash flow way out in time, so its price swings violently with rates, and it has a duration number that measures exactly how much. Growth stocks behave like unbounded-duration bonds. Tech companies in particular are often called long-duration equities precisely because so much of their value sits in the terminal, far-out years: the profits they expect in year ten, fifteen, twenty, not the profits they're making now. A software company with a great margin ten years out, a biotech with no revenue at all, an AI company that's still spending, are all mostly terminal value. Compress the discount rate and their valuations shrink the most. A boring consumer staples company earning steadily today barely moves, because its cash is close in time and hard to compress.

The discount rate itself moves for two reasons at once, which is why the effect is stronger than the Fed funds rate alone suggests. Treasury yields are the risk-free anchor underneath everything, and they follow the Fed fairly directly. But equity investors also demand a risk premium on top, and that premium tends to widen when rates rise, because the Fed usually tightens either because the economy is running hot or because it is trying to cool something that might break. So the discount rate applied to stocks often rises by more than the policy rate itself. Higher rates also mean Treasury bonds start offering a real competing return, and when a ten-year bond pays a decent yield, investors rebalance out of speculative equity and demand a bigger reward for holding risky stocks.

Then there are second-order effects that amplify it. Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, and a lot of tech growth was built on cheap debt and cheap private capital. When venture funding gets expensive and expensive to refinance, growth gets slower and some money-losing companies get squeezed toward profitability rather than growth, which forces the market to re-rate them on today's earnings instead of tomorrow's. Faster discounting also hurts the compounding story directly: if a company's growth rate expectation is cut even slightly while the discount rate goes up, the gap between growth and discount, which is the whole engine of a high multiple, narrows fast. And higher rates usually mean a stronger dollar and slower consumer and enterprise spending, which trims the revenue forecasts those valuations were built on.

There's a wrinkle worth saying out loud, because it isn't a law of nature. Rates rising because demand is genuinely strong can be good for tech earnings even as multiples compress, so the stock price depends on which effect wins. The clean case is what happened in 2022, when the Fed tightened hard and fast and the Nasdaq fell far more than the broader market, because rate-driven multiple compression hit long-duration growth names hardest and earnings weren't strong enough to offset it. Also, megacap tech with enormous cash flows buying back stock has behaved less like a long-duration growth story in recent cycles than it used to, so "tech" isn't one bucket anymore.
