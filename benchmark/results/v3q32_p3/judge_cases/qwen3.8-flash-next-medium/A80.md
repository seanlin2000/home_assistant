Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Quantizing stores each weight in fewer bits than the usual 16. Weights alone: 16-bit about 140 GB; 4-bit 70 billion times half a byte, about 35 GB (real 4-bit files run roughly 38 to 42 GB because of scales and mixed precision); 2-bit about 17.5 GB (about 20 to 25 GB in practice). The context cache and activations come on top. Trade-offs: some loss of quality, small at 4-bit and marked at 2-bit (reasoning and factual accuracy suffer most); faster generation, because inference is limited by memory bandwidth; careful methods (GPTQ, AWQ, importance-weighted quantization) reduce the loss. Wrong memory figures are an error.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing means replacing each weight, normally stored as 16-bit floating point, with a much lower-precision value, typically a small integer plus a shared scale factor per group of weights, so the model occupies far less memory and reads fewer bytes per forward pass.

For 70 billion weights: at 16 bits that's 140 gigabytes, at 4 bits about 35 gigabytes, and at 2 bits about 17.5 gigabytes, a fourfold and eightfold reduction. The 4-bit figure is a little higher in practice because group scales and the first and last layers are usually kept at higher precision.

The trade-offs are quality and stability. Four bits, as in GPTQ, AWQ or Q4 GGUF, is close to the original, with a small perplexity bump. Two bits is aggressive: reasoning, long context and code degrade noticeably, and some setups keep salient weights in higher precision to compensate. Speed improves mainly because memory bandwidth is the bottleneck, while quantization itself adds packing overhead and can slightly slow token generation.
