## What Quantization Actually Does
Quantization stores those same weights in fewer bits. Instead of 16 bits per weight, you use 8 bits (INT8), 4 bits (INT4), or even less. The math is straightforward: going from 16-bit to 4-bit cuts memory by 4x. That 70B model drops from 140GB to about 35GB, which suddenly fits on a single 48GB GPU.
## Bit Widths: INT8, INT4, and Below
The bit width is the most important number. It directly determines how much memory you save and roughly how much quality you risk.
Below 4-bit. INT3, INT2, and 1.58-bit schemes exist. They save more memory but the quality degradation becomes noticeable, and the methods needed to keep them usable get complicated. For production work, 4-bit is usually the practical floor. Going lower is research territory or a last resort when memory is extremely tight.
## The Methods: GPTQ, AWQ, and Friends
AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) starts from the observation that not all weights matter equally. A small fraction of weights, the ones multiplied by large activations, carry most of the model's behavior. AWQ identifies these salient weights using activation statistics and protects them by scaling, so the important channels keep their precision while the rest get quantized aggressively. AWQ is fast to apply and tends to preserve quality well, especially for instruction-tuned models.

## Group Size and Its Effect on Quality
GPTQ and AWQ both use per-group quantization scales, where a group is a contiguous block of weights that share the same quantization scale factor. Smaller group sizes mean more scale factors (more storage overhead but better quality); larger group sizes mean fewer scale factors (less overhead but coarser quantization). Group size 128 is the standard default — it adds roughly 3% memory overhead versus the weight storage and produces good quality for most models. Group size 32 produces noticeably better quality, especially for smaller models where each weight matters more, at roughly 10% memory overhead. ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means keeping the same architecture and the same learned knowledge, but storing each weight in a smaller number of bits. A 70‑billion‑parameter model released in its native 16‑bit precision, which is usually BF16 or FP16, needs two bytes per weight, so 70 billion times two bytes is about 140 gigabytes just for the weights — that's not a single card, that's a small rack.

Drop to 4 bits and each weight takes half a byte, so the weights alone come to 35 gigabytes. In practice you rarely get exactly 35, because 4‑bit schemes store a shared scaling factor for every group of weights — typically groups of 128 — plus some layers kept at higher precision. A common GGUF quantization called Q4_K_M works out to roughly 4.6 effective bits per weight, which is about 40 gigabytes for a 70B model. That's the number that lets it fit on two consumer 4090s or a single 48‑gigabyte professional card.

A nominal 2‑bit quantization would be 70 billion times a quarter byte, or 17.5 gigabytes. But here's the catch: the practical 2‑bit formats are mixed precision, keeping the most sensitive layers at 4 or 6 bits, so something like Q2_K actually lands closer to 3 bits per weight — around 26 gigabytes — not 17.5. That mismatch is worth knowing, because the memory saving you were promised isn't quite the saving you get.

The quality trade‑off is not linear, and that's the most important practical fact. Going from 16 bits to 8 is essentially free. Going from 8 to 4 costs very little — the commonly cited figure is around ninety‑two to ninety‑five percent of full‑precision quality on standard benchmarks, with perplexity barely moved. Going from 4 to 3 to 2 is where it starts to hurt, and the degradation is disproportionate to the memory you recover. Guides in 2026 consistently call Q4_K_M the sweet spot, treat Q6_K as the choice when you have memory to spare, and advise avoiding 2‑bit unless you are genuinely out of options.

What you actually lose is subtler than a single benchmark score. Quantization rounds weights to a small set of allowed values, so rounding error accumulates through the network. It tends to show up most in arithmetic, long‑context retrieval, exact instruction following, and structured output like tool calls — the tasks where precision matters. Perplexity can look fine while a model gets more prone to fumbling exact details or drifting in style. Methods like GPTQ and AWQ mitigate this by using a calibration dataset to choose the rounding that minimizes error layer by layer, and AWQ specifically protects the small fraction of weights that get multiplied by large activations. That introduces its own dependency: if your calibration data doesn't resemble your actual use case, the quantized model can underperform in ways a benchmark won't reveal.

There are operational costs too. Quantizing a 70B model takes on the order of an hour, and peaks at roughly 1.5 times the model size in memory during the process. Formats fragment — GGUF for CPU and llama.cpp, AWQ or GPTQ for GPU serving with vLLM, bitsandbytes for fine‑tuning with QLoRA — and picking the wrong one means your inference engine won't run it. bitsandbytes 4‑bit inference is typically 20 to 40 percent slower than AWQ or GPTQ because its kernels aren't throughput‑optimized.

One last thing that trips people up: quantizing weights says nothing about the context window. Activations and the key‑value cache are separate, and they grow with prompt length, so real memory use is weights plus context. A 40‑gigabyte model on a 48‑gigabyte card has only about 8 gigabytes left for everything else, which constrains how much context you can actually run.

If you want a single rule of thumb that the guides agree on: at a fixed memory budget, a bigger model quantized harder generally beats a smaller model quantized lightly.
