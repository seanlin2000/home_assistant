Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future, and the interest rate is the discount you apply to that future cash. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and every dollar you expect to receive later is worth less today. That mechanic applies to all stocks, but it hits technology companies especially hard for a few reasons.

First, tech valuations are back-loaded. A mature business like a utility or a consumer staples company earns a steady, sizable chunk of its total value in the next few years. A high-growth software or biotech company, by contrast, may be barely profitable now, with most of its eventual cash flows expected a decade or more out. In finance terms these are called long-duration assets, and the longer the wait for the cash, the more sensitive the present value is to the discount rate. Mathematically you divide the future cash flow by one plus the discount rate raised to the number of years, so the exponent magnifies the effect. A rate that is one percentage point higher barely dent a cash flow arriving in two years, but it can cut the present value of a cash flow arriving in fifteen years by a double-digit percentage.

Second, the discount rate investors use is built out of the risk-free rate plus a risk premium. The Fed's policy rate doesn't directly set the long-term Treasury yield, but rate hikes push the whole yield curve up and signal that the cheap-money era is over. Since the risk-free rate is the base of the whole valuation stack, when it rises, the required return on every risky asset rises with it, and prices fall until the expected returns catch up. Equity risk premiums can compress when growth expectations stay strong, which is why the relationship isn't perfectly tight, but the direction is consistent.

Third, higher rates raise the actual cost of capital for the companies themselves. Tech growth has historically been financed with cheap debt and cheap venture capital. When borrowing gets expensive, startups fundraise at lower valuations, companies pull back on buybacks and capital spending, and speculative money that was chasing yield into growth stocks gets drawn toward bonds and money market funds paying a real return. If you can get a decent yield in Treasuries, the case for paying fifty times earnings for a company that might not earn much for a decade weakens.

There's also a demand-side channel. Rate hikes slow borrowing, hiring, and business spending, which is exactly what enterprise software buyers, ad-driven platforms, and hardware customers cut when their own costs rise. So the higher discount rate often arrives alongside a genuine slowdown in the revenue growth that justified the premium multiples in the first place. A valuation is a function of both the size of future cash and the rate you discount it at, and rate hikes tend to squeeze both at once.

Two nuances worth knowing. One is that the real driver is the long-term real yield, not the Fed funds target itself. A hike accompanied by falling long rates can actually help growth stocks, which is part of why markets sometimes rally on hawkish-sounding Fed news if inflation expectations drop faster than the policy rate rises. The other is that some mega-cap tech firms now generate enormous current free cash flow and are cash-rich rather than cash-hungry, so they behave less like pure duration plays than they did in the past. Even then, the multiple they trade at is still anchored to a discount rate, which is why the sector as a whole tends to de-rate when rates rise and re-rate when they fall, as the 2022 rate cycle demonstrated very starkly.
