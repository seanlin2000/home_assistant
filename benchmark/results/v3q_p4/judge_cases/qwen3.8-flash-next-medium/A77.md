Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to sunlight, tiny air molecules, and how far the light has to travel.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of every color — the same rainbow you see when light passes through a prism. Each color is a wave of a different size. Red light has long, lazy waves. Blue and violet light have short, choppy ones.

Now the atmosphere is full of nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and they are much smaller than a wavelength of visible light. When sunlight hits them, the light bounces off in all directions — but not equally. Short, choppy waves like blue get knocked around far more easily than long, easygoing waves like red. Physicists call this Rayleigh scattering, and the rough rule is that blue light scatters roughly ten times more strongly than red.

Here's the part that explains the daytime sky. As sunlight crosses the sky overhead, it's passing through a relatively shallow layer of air. A modest amount of blue gets scattered out of the beam and sent off in every direction, including down toward your eyes. But it isn't scattered just once — it bounces around again and again, so the blue light ends up spread across the entire sky dome. You're not looking at the sun; you're looking at air that's glowing with redirected blue light. Violet scatters even more strongly than blue, which is why the upper atmosphere actually leans violet — but our eyes are much less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up, so we perceive blue.

Sunset is the same physics with one big change: distance. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to skim through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you — dozens of times more air than when the sun is overhead. Every color gets a chance to scatter, and over that long journey the blues and violets get scattered away completely, scrubbed out of the direct beam before it ever reaches your eye. What survives the trip is the red and orange end of the spectrum, the light that was too stubborn to be knocked out of the way.

That's also why the colors concentrate near the horizon rather than covering the whole sky: the long path is only in the direction you're looking. And why a sunset is more dramatic when the air is loaded with dust, smoke, wildfire ash, or volcanic particles. Those larger particles scatter and block even more of the remaining light, deepening the reds. The famous line that sunsets are red because the light passes through more air is the short version; the deeper version is that the longer path removes the blue first, and whatever's left is what you see.

One last thing worth knowing: red sunsets are about scattering, not about the sun changing color. The sun emits the same light all day. The sky is just doing a different amount of filtering depending on the angle you're looking through it.
