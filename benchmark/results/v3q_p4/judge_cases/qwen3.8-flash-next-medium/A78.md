The wealthiest 10% of US households held 88.1% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares in Q2 2026, according to Axis Intelligence Research calculations from Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts. The top 1% alone held 50.9%, and the 90th to 99th percentile held 37.2%.
### How much of the stock market does the bottom 50% own?
The bottom half of US households by wealth held 0.6% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares in Q2 2026, according to the Federal Reserve. That share was 1.1% in 1989. Many bottom-half families do participate, but their holdings are small relative to the aggregate.
### What is the Shareholder Concentration Multiple?
The Shareholder Concentration Multiple (SCM) is an Axis Intelligence Research metric dividing a wealth group’s share of US equities by its share of households. In Q2 2026 the top 1% read 50.9 and the bottom 50% read 0.012, computed from Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts.

## Who owns the value
Participation is broad, but ownership of the value is extraordinarily narrow. In Q2 2026 the top 1% of households by wealth owned 50.9% of all corporate equities and mutual fund shares, the top 10% owned 88.1%, and the bottom 50% owned just 0.6% (see the chart and table below).

## The concentration is growing
The top's grip is tightening. In the Fed's latest data the top 1%'s share of equities rose from 49.6% in early 2023 to 50.9% in Q2 2026, worth about $32.9 trillion, approaching the 52.3% high it reached in 2019.
Because stock ownership is so skewed, every market rally widens the wealth gap: the gains flow overwhelmingly to households that already own the most. The bottom half, holding 0.6% of equities, is largely a spectator to the market's wealth creation.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that a stock index and a household budget measure different things. An index prices the future profits of a few hundred large companies, mostly ones that compete globally. A household budget is wages minus the price of rent, gas and groceries, and those two lines barely move together. Here's three concrete ways they come apart, using this year's data.

First, the people who own the stocks are a small slice of the population, and the concentration has been getting worse. According to the Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts, as of the second quarter of this year the wealthiest one percent of households held 50.9 percent of all corporate equities and mutual fund shares, the top ten percent held about 88 percent, and the bottom half of the country held 0.6 percent. Gallup finds that around 58 percent of American adults have some money in the market, but participation is broad while the dollars are extremely narrow. So a rally genuinely improves millions of balance sheets, just not most of them, and the median holding in the top decile is tens of times larger than the median in the bottom half. If your retirement account is modest and your rent is not, a record close changes your life very little.

Second, inflation is a revenue line for companies and a tax on households at the same time. This year an oil shock tied to the conflict with Iran pushed headline consumer prices back up to 3.7 percent year over year in September, while core inflation stayed around 2.6 percent, meaning the increase was concentrated in fuel, groceries and utilities, the categories nobody can skip. Meanwhile real hourly wages slipped below zero for the first time in three years, the personal savings rate is heading toward roughly 3.3 percent, and credit card and auto loan delinquencies remain elevated at the New York Fed. Companies can hold or raise prices and protect margins, which is exactly what supports earnings and the index. Households absorb the same price increase out of a shrinking buffer. The University of Michigan's sentiment index hit 44.8 this May, the lowest in its 74-year history, and was back near 48 in October, even as the S&P 500 set records and rose roughly 11 to 13 percent on the year. The survey director, Joanne Hsu, has pointed out that the pessimism is concentrated among lower-income households with small stock portfolios, the ones least represented in whatever is setting records.

Third, an index measures a portfolio, not a country. Germany is the clearest case: the DAX hit an all-time high of about 26,644 points this month, while the German economy grew 0.1 percent in 2025 and its manufacturing purchasing managers' index has been sitting in the mid-40s, which is contraction. The index is weighted toward global exporters like SAP and Siemens, whose profits come from worldwide demand and are actually helped by a weaker euro. Japan is the same phenomenon in a different key. Real household spending fell 6.8 percent in September, the tenth straight monthly decline and the steepest since 2020, even though nominal wages rose at their fastest pace since 1997. The explanation there is that a yen around 157 to 158 to the dollar flatters exporters' earnings while making the food and energy households buy more expensive, and after several years of prices outrunning pay, a few good months feel like a promise, not a windfall, so people save instead of spending.

There's one more wrinkle worth knowing. Stock prices are forward-looking, so a rally can be anticipating earnings that haven't shown up in paychecks yet, often from capital spending on AI infrastructure, which raises corporate revenue and GDP without adding many jobs. Hiring this year has slowed to a crawl, around 50,000 jobs a month on a three-month average, in what economists are calling a low hire, low fire labor market. That combination, steady employment but falling real pay, is precisely the case where the market and the mood diverge most sharply. Neither reading is wrong; they're just instruments pointed at different things.
