The stock market reflects expected profits of large, often multinational companies, and stock ownership is concentrated (in the US the richest tenth of households own the large majority of stocks), while people feel the economy through wages, prices, rent and jobs. Three concrete examples from any of: prices rising faster than wages while companies keep or raise margins; a handful of giant tech firms driving index gains while most businesses and regions lag; companies earning much of their profit abroad; cost cutting and layoffs that raise profits and share prices; low rates or stimulus inflating asset prices, which helps people who own assets (the K-shaped recovery of 2020 and 2021); housing costs climbing while the index climbs. # Exchange 1

USER: Why can a country have a strong stock market while its average citizens feel like the economy is getting worse? Give three concrete examples of how this happens.

ASSISTANT: That's a real and well-documented gap, sometimes called the "vibecession." The short version is that a stock index measures the value of a few hundred large companies, not the finances of a few hundred million households, and those two things can move in opposite directions for long stretches. Three concrete ways it happens:

First, market gains are concentrated in a handful of stocks and in the hands of a few people. In the United States, the top ten companies make up roughly a third to a half of the S&P 500's total value, and the wealthiest households own the overwhelming majority of equities. So when an index rises because a few technology giants get more valuable, most households see almost none of that gain in their own net worth. Meanwhile, wages for typical workers grow slowly, so paychecks don't keep up with what the market is doing.

Second, corporate profits can rise faster than wages, which boosts earnings and therefore share prices while squeezing household budgets. Companies protect margins by holding headcount flat, automating, cutting hours, or pushing costs onto consumers as higher prices. A business that grows profit per employee while hiring fewer people looks great on an earnings call and feels worse to the person whose hours got cut or whose rent went up. Stock buybacks have the same effect in a purer form: a company borrows cheap money to purchase its own shares, so earnings per share and the stock price rise without any new product, plant, or job.

Third, asset prices can go up while the cost of living goes up faster than pay. A strong market often sits alongside expensive housing, because low borrowing costs and limited supply lift home prices; a young worker watching their rent and potential mortgage climb can reasonably feel poorer even as index records fall. Inflation is the same mechanism more broadly: if inflation runs at three percent and typical wages grow at two, households are losing purchasing power every year even while equity markets post gains. And stock market strength tends to show up in headline wealth figures long before it shows up in anything a renter or an hourly worker actually feels.

There's also a timing effect worth mentioning: markets price expectations about the next year or two, while people experience last month's grocery bill and paycheck. A market can rally on the expectation that inflation is cooling and rate cuts are coming, while prices are still high in absolute terms. People judge the economy by levels they pay today; markets judge it by the direction of change they expect tomorrow.
