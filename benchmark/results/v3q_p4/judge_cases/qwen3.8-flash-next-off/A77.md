Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that sunlight looks white, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and the air scatters the shorter blue wavelengths around much more strongly than the longer red ones. So during the day you're seeing scattered blue light coming at you from every direction in the sky, while at sunset the sunlight has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere, which strips away almost all the blue before it reaches your eyes, leaving the oranges and reds behind.

Here's the mechanism more slowly. Sunlight is a spread of wavelengths — short ones read as violet and blue, long ones read as orange and red. The molecules in our atmosphere are much smaller than those wavelengths, so when light hits them it gets bounced, and the amount of bounce depends steeply on wavelength: shorter light scatters far more strongly than longer light. Doubling the wavelength cuts the scattering down by a factor of sixteen. Blue is scattered maybe ten times more readily than red.

So at midday, sunlight enters the top of the atmosphere and the blue component immediately starts ricocheting off air molecules in every direction, including downward toward you. No matter which patch of sky you look at, you catch some of that redirected blue. The red and yellow mostly travel straight through, so the sun itself looks whitish or slightly yellow rather than blue. The reason the sky isn't violet, even though violet is scattered even more strongly than blue, is a combination of the sun giving off less violet to begin with, our eyes being much less sensitive to violet, and the upper atmosphere absorbing some of it. Blue wins out as what we perceive.

At sunset the geometry flips. When the sun is low, its light reaches you at a shallow angle, so instead of passing through maybe a few miles of air it skims through a path tens of times longer. That long journey scatters the blue and violet out of the beam almost completely — which is why the sun looks yellow or orange when it's near the horizon, and can look deep red when there's dust, smoke, haze, or pollution along the path, since larger particles remove the remaining shorter wavelengths too. What arrives at your eyes is the leftover long-wavelength light: oranges, reds, pinks.

The colors you actually see painted across the sky at sunset come from that same stripped-down beam hitting something else on its way in — high clouds, aerosols, haze — and scattering what's left over toward you.
