USER: What does it mean to quantize a 70-billion-parameter language model to 4-bit or 2-bit precision? How much memory would the weights require in each case, and what trade-offs does quantization introduce?

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means storing its weights with fewer bits than the original full-precision numbers. A 70-billion-parameter model trained and shipped at 16-bit floating point — the usual format for inference — needs two bytes per weight, so about 140 GB just for the weights. You can compute that as 70 billion times two divided by a thousand cubed, which comes out to roughly 130 GiB or 140 GB depending on which unit you use.

At 4-bit precision you store each weight in half a byte, so the same 70 billion parameters take about 70 GB, give or take. In practice formats like GGUF Q4 add a small amount of overhead for per-group scaling factors, so real files land somewhere around 40 GB for the common 4-bit-quantized versions of 70-billion models — those are typically counted in binary gigabytes, and they also keep some layers at higher precision. At 2 bits you're down to a quarter byte per weight, roughly 17.5 GB as a raw floor, with real files usually a bit larger for the same scaling metadata.

The trade-offs come from squeezing many continuous values into a handful of discrete levels. A 4-bit integer can represent only sixteen distinct values, so every weight is snapped to the nearest one, and you rely on group-wise scales and zero-points to keep the ranges sensible. The consequences are predictable: perplexity rises, meaning the model is less certain about the next token; long-form coherence, precise arithmetic, exact instruction following and tool-calling reliability tend to degrade before general conversational quality does; and very low bit rates can quietly damage specific capabilities like code generation or multilingual handling while leaving benchmarks that mostly measure common prose looking nearly untouched. Quantization also usually slows each token slightly compared to a native 16-bit implementation, because the weights must be unpacked during the matmul, though it speeds things up relative to being forced out of memory entirely.

Two bits is where the damage becomes visible. Techniques like Q2_K or AQLM can keep a 70-billion model at roughly four bits' worth of quality loss with clever codebook and residual methods, but naive 2-bit rounding usually produces a noticeably worse model. The rule of thumb from published quantization work is that below about 3 bits per parameter you start losing real information, not just rounding noise, so the practical sweet spot for a 70-billion model is 4-bit: roughly a quarter of the 16-bit footprint at a quality cost most people find acceptable, versus 2-bit, which fits on much smaller hardware but asks you to accept a measurably weaker model.
