A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). # Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This one is mostly a matter of financial mechanics rather than current events, so let me lay out the reasoning directly.

**The core idea: a stock's value is the present value of its future cash flows.**

Every share is worth what the money it will produce in the future is worth *today*. To get from future dollars to today's dollars, you discount them — divide by a rate that reflects both the time value of money and the risk of those cash flows. That discount rate is essentially the risk-free rate (anchored by long-term Treasury yields, which rise when the Fed tightens) plus a risk premium.

**Duration matters — and tech stocks are the equity version of long bonds.**

In bond math, a 30-year zero-coupon bond is far more rate-sensitive than a two-year note, because nearly all of its value sits far in the future. The same logic applies to equities. A mature utility or consumer staples company earns most of its cash flow in the next couple of years; those near-term dollars barely move when the discount rate ticks up. A high-growth technology company is the opposite: a large share of its total value comes from profits expected many years out, sometimes not yet achieved at all. Its "cash-flow duration" is long, so a small change in the discount rate moves a big chunk of its value. That's why the phrase "long-duration growth stocks" gets used — and why the tech-heavy Nasdaq tends to fall more than the Dow when rates spike.

**A rough illustration.**

Say a company's earnings stream has an effective duration of about eight years. If the discount rate rises by one percentage point, present value falls roughly by that eight-to-one ratio — call it somewhere in the neighborhood of eight percent, all else equal. A staples company with a duration of three or four might lose only three to four percent. Same rate move, very different damage. (That's an approximation using a duration heuristic, not a precise formula — real valuations also react to expectations about growth.)

**Why tech is especially exposed, beyond the discounting math.**

First, growth expectations get revised down. Tighter policy slows borrowing, hiring, and corporate IT budgets, which is where much software and cloud demand comes from. If the *numerator* — expected future cash flows — shrinks at the same time the *denominator* — the discount rate — rises, both push valuation the same direction.

Second, tech has historically been cheap-money businesses. Venture capital funding, leveraged buyouts, share buybacks funded with debt, and consumer financing for hardware all get more expensive. Startup valuations collapse first because they are pure far-future promises with no current earnings to discount at all.

Third, the risk-free rate acts as a floor under required returns. When five-year Treasuries yield close to zero, investors accept tiny yields from growth stocks and pay high multiples for them
