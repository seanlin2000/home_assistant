Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value today is nothing more than the sum of all the cash it will produce in the future, pulled back to the present through a discount rate. That discount rate is essentially the risk-free rate — which is what the Fed directly pushes up — plus an equity risk premium for the uncertainty involved.

Here's the mechanics in a way you can feel. Suppose you're promised a hundred dollars ten years from now. Discount it at three percent and it's worth about seventy-four dollars today. Discount it at eight percent and it's worth about forty-six. That's a 38 percent drop in value from a five-point move in the discount rate, purely from the math of time. Now run the same exercise on money arriving next year: at three percent it's worth about ninety-seven, at eight percent about ninety-three — only a four-and-a-half percent difference. Same rate change, wildly different impact, entirely because of how far away the cash is.

That is why tech gets hit hardest. Software and platform companies are what investors call long-duration assets. Their cash flows aren't arranged like a utility's, with steady earnings each year. They're back-loaded: thin or negative margins early, with most of the profit assumed to arrive five, ten, fifteen years out, and an even bigger share of the value sitting in terminal value, which by definition lives in the distant future. When the discount rate rises, that back-loaded cash flow gets squeezed far more severely than the near-term cash flow of a bank, an energy company, or a maker of toothpaste. The duration analogy to bonds is exact: a thirty-year bond is far more rate-sensitive than a two-year one, and a growth stock is the equity version of the thirty-year bond.

A second channel compounds the first. When Treasury yields were near zero, a bond genuinely paid almost nothing, so investors accepted low expected returns on stocks and were willing to pay high earnings multiples. Once a ten-year Treasury yields four or five percent, the competing safe asset is actually attractive, so the equity risk premium investors demand rises at the same time the risk-free rate does. Both halves of the discount rate move up together. There's also a smaller mechanical effect: higher rates mean higher bond interest expense for companies that carry debt, and growth-stage companies often fund years of losses with debt or expensive private capital.

The 2022 cycle is the clean natural experiment. The Fed went from near zero to about four and a half percent in a single year, seven hikes. The S&P 500 fell about 19 percent for the year, roughly 25 percent peak to trough, while the Nasdaq fell about 33 percent. The asymmetry was exactly what the discounting logic predicts, since the Nasdaq is concentrated in those back-loaded businesses while the S&P holds more companies earning cash right now. The extreme case was ARK Innovation, which sells unprofitable, maximum-duration stories and lost about 75 percent that year. Some analysts frame the sensitivity as roughly 15 to 25 percent of fair value per percentage point of discount rate for a long-duration growth company; I'd treat that as a rough rule of thumb rather than a precise law, but it's the right order of magnitude for something whose value is mostly terminal value.

Two important qualifications. First, the discount rate is only half the equation. A higher discount rate can be offset if earnings themselves grow faster, or if the rate rise reflects an economy that's genuinely stronger. The 2004 to 2006 tightening cycle barely dented equities for that reason, and many large tech companies have enormous cash piles and low debt, so their interest expense hardly moves. What makes a rate cycle damaging is the combination of fast hikes and rich starting valuations, which is what made 2022 unusual. Second, the Fed's rate doesn't move in isolation from expectations about future rates; the market's forecast of the path, plus the ten-year yield and the real yield rather than just the nominal one, is what actually drives valuations. The St. Louis Fed has noted that in 2022 the short nominal rate and forward real rates told meaningfully different stories, and it's the real rate that matters for discounting.

So the short version: rates set the price of time, and tech stocks are the ones whose value lives furthest in time.
