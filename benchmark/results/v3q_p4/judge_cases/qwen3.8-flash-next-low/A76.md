On 26 December 1991, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved and ceased to exist as a sovereign state. It also brought an end to the Soviet Union's federal government and to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's efforts to reform the Soviet political and economic system in an attempt to stop a period of political and economic stagnation.
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 resulted from several factors: chronic economic stagnation, the unsustainable financial burden of the arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, intense ethnic nationalism and separatism within its union republics, and the destabilizing effects of Gorbachev's reforms (particularly glasnost and perestroika).
The process began with growing unrest in the country's union republics developing into an incessant political and legislative conflict between them and the central government. Until its final years, the Soviet Union was made up of 15 union republics that served as the homelands of different ethnic groups. Estonia was the first union republic to declare state sovereignty on 16 November 1988. Lithuania was the first republic to declare independence, in March 1990.

Between 18 and 22 August 1991, amidst the dissolution of the Soviet Union, a hardliner group of top military and civilian officials within the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) attempted to forcibly seize power from Mikhail Gorbachev, who had been serving as the Soviet Union's president since March 1990 and as the CPSU's General Secretary since March 1985. Among the leaders of what is also known as the August Coup was Gennady Yanayev, who had been serving as the Soviet Union's vice president since December 1990.
Yanayev and his allies, who together formed the State Committee on the State of Emergency (Russian: ГКЧП, romanized: GKChP), had been motivated by their opposition to Gorbachev's political reforms, the collapse of Soviet hegemony over Eastern Europe, and the proposed New Union Treaty, which was to distribute much of the heavily centralized power of the Soviet government among each of the fifteen Union Republics. The initiative for de-centralization and increased autonomy had been spurred by Boris Yeltsin while he served as the Chairman of the Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR until July 1991; Yeltsin's demand opened a window for the GKChP to plan the coup d'état attempt.

In 1990, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Armenia had already declared the restoration of their independence from the Soviet Union. In January 1991, a violent attempt to return Lithuania to the Soviet Union by force took place. About a week later, a similar attempt was engineered by local pro-Soviet forces to overthrow Latvian authorities. These attempts failed, and Lithuania and Latvia retained their statehood.

## Legal basis and ratification
On 10 December, the agreement was ratified by the Verkhovna Rada of Ukraine and the Supreme Council of Belarus. On 12 December, the Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR formally ratified the Belovezh Accords, denounced (that is, withdrew from) the 1922 Treaty on the Creation of the Soviet Union, and recalled the Russian deputies from the Supreme Soviet of the USSR. The Fall of the Berlin Wall and the fortified inner German border occurred after a Politburo press conference on 9 November 1989 prematurely announced that East Germans could travel without restrictions after, in the decades before, hundreds had been killed by Border Troops of the German Democratic Republic when trying to sneak through the Iron Curtain.
Soon, people gathered at Berlin border crossings, citing the news that were broadcast in East and West, and puzzled border guards eventually let them pass in the late evening hours, with live television broadcasting worldwide. The next days, thousands travelled across the border, also in other places less covered by press. After this symbolic fall, obstacles were removed, former crossings were opened again, and following demolition, L-shaped concrete segments of the Berlin wall were actually falling and spread around the world.
## Background
### Opening of the Iron Curtain
The June 1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre had shown what could happen under communist rule. The opening of the Iron Curtain between Austria and Hungary at the Pan-European Picnic on 19 August 1989 set in motion a peaceful chain reaction, at the end of which there was no longer an East Germany and the Eastern Bloc had disintegrated.
## Events
### Misinformed public announcements
At 18:53, near the end of the press conference, ANSA's Riccardo Ehrman asked if the draft travel law of 6 November was a mistake. Schabowski gave a confusing answer that asserted it was necessary because West Germany had exhausted its capacity to accept fleeing East Germans, then remembered the note he had been given and added that a new regulation had been drafted to allow permanent emigration at any border crossing. This caused a stir in the room; amid several questions at once, Schabowski expressed surprise that the reporters had not yet seen this regulation, and started reading from the note. After this, a reporter, either Ehrman or Bild-Zeitung reporter Peter Brinkmann, both of whom were sitting in the front row at the press conference, asked when the regulations would take effect. After a few seconds' hesitation, Schabowski replied, "As far as I know, it takes effect immediately, without delay" (German: Das tritt nach meiner Kenntnis ... ist das sofort ... unverzüglich). This was an apparent assumption based on the note's opening paragraph; as Beil attempted to interject that it was up to the Council of Ministers to decide when it took effect, Schabowski proceeded to read this clause, which stated it was in effect until a law on the matter was passed by the Volkskammer. Crucially, a journalist then asked if the regulation also applied to the crossings to West Berlin. Schabowski shrugged and read item 3 of the note, which confirmed that it did.

The orchestra and choir were drawn from both East and West Germany, as well as the United Kingdom, France, the Soviet Union, and the United States. On New Year's Eve 1989, David Hasselhoff performed his song "Looking for Freedom" while standing atop the partly demolished Wall in front of 200,000 people. Roger Waters performed the Pink Floyd album The Wall just north of Potsdamer Platz on 21 July 1990, with guests including Scorpions, Bryan Adams, Sinéad O'Connor, Cyndi Lauper, Thomas Dolby, Joni Mitchell, Marianne Faithfull, Levon Helm, Rick Danko and Van Morrison.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War didn't end with a treaty or a battle; it ended when one of the two sides simply stopped existing. On December 26, 1991, the Supreme Soviet formally dissolved the USSR, which is why that date, rather than the fall of the Wall, is the real endpoint.

It helps to separate the slow causes from the quick triggers. The causes were structural and had been building for years. First, decades of economic stagnation under Brezhnev, made worse by an arms race the Soviet economy couldn't afford, by the war in Afghanistan, and by the cost of propping up satellite states. Second, ethnic nationalism inside the fifteen union republics, grievances that central authority had suppressed but never resolved. Third, and this is the paradox, Gorbachev's own reforms. Glasnost loosened censorship, which let people voice grievances openly; perestroika tried to inject market mechanisms without dismantling the command economy, which produced disruption rather than growth. Once criticism became legal, the forces that held the union together lost their coercive edge. Estonia declared sovereignty in November 1988, Lithuania declared independence in March 1990. The union was already coming apart before any single dramatic event.

Now the three events I'd call most important.

The first is Gorbachev's turn to reform and his "Sinatra doctrine" of non-intervention, roughly 1988 to 1989. By signaling that Moscow would no longer use force to keep its satellite governments in place, as it had in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, he removed the guarantee the Eastern Bloc regimes depended on. That's the hinge that turns an internal Soviet problem into the end of the Cold War itself.

The second is the fall of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989, which followed the chain reaction set off when Hungary opened its border with Austria in August. Worth noting the mechanics here: it wasn't a planned opening. A Politburo spokesman, Günter Schabowski, mishandled a draft travel regulation at a televised press conference and said it took effect "immediately, without delay." Confused border guards let crowds through. So the Wall's fall was itself an accidental trigger sitting on top of deep causes, and its consequence was German reunification and the disintegration of the Eastern Bloc, which is what actually ends the Cold War.

The third is the failed August coup of 1991, which is the clearest example of a trigger doing the decisive damage. Hardliners in the party, military and KGB, calling themselves the State Committee on the State of Emergency, placed Gorbachev under house arrest on August 18 to stop the proposed New Union Treaty, which would have devolved real power to the republics. They had aimed to preserve the union. Instead, three days of street resistance led by Boris Yeltsin broke the coup, and in its aftermath the Communist Party was suspended, Gorbachev's authority was gone, and the republics moved immediately. Ukraine voted for independence on December 1, and on December 8 the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus signed the Belovezha Accords declaring the Soviet Union had ceased to exist. The Alma-Ata Protocol on December 21, signed by eleven of the remaining twelve republics, removed any legal basis for it to continue. Gorbachev resigned a few days later.

So the pattern is this: economic exhaustion, ethnic nationalism and the destabilizing effects of glasnost and perestroika are the causes. The refusal to intervene in 1989, the botched press conference that opened the Wall, and the botched coup that destroyed the center's last authority are the triggers. And the striking thing about 1991 is that the hardliners' attempt to stop the collapse is what finished it. One caveat worth holding onto: the Belovezha Accords were arguably legally questionable, since only three of the fifteen republics signed them, which is exactly why Alma-Ata mattered a couple of weeks later.
