Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of every color, each of which is a different wavelength of light. Red is long and lazy; blue and violet are short and twitchy. The air around you isn't empty — it's packed with nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and those molecules are much smaller than the wavelengths of visible light. When sunlight runs into them, it bounces off in every direction, and here's the whole trick: the shorter the wavelength, the more violently it gets knocked around. It's not a little more, either — blue light at around four hundred nanometers gets scattered roughly nine times more strongly than deep red light at seven hundred nanometers.

So when the sun is high overhead, its beam passes through a relatively thin slab of atmosphere on its way down. Along that path, the blue and violet get scattered out of the beam almost immediately, flung sideways and up and every which way. Now imagine lying on your back and looking up. You're not looking at the sun; you're looking at air. And that air is full of blue light that was scattered into your eye from every direction. The blue you see is sunlight that already took a wrong turn. Space, by the way, is black for exactly the opposite reason — there's no air there to scatter anything.

Violet actually scatters even more strongly than blue, which is why you might expect a violet sky. Two things stop that: the sun gives off a fair amount less violet than blue to begin with, and your eye is much less sensitive to violet, with some of it absorbed up high in the atmosphere. The mix your eye settles on is the blue we're used to.

Sunset is the same physics with a much longer road. When the sun is on the horizon, its light has to cross the entire breadth of the atmosphere sideways to reach you — a path dozens of times thicker than the light traveling from an overhead sun. Along that enormous journey, the blue gets scattered away again and again, and scattered, and scattered, until almost none of it survives in the direct beam heading toward your eye. What's left arriving is the long, stubborn wavelengths: orange, then red. Meanwhile, all the blue that got knocked out of that beam was flung in directions away from you, so you don't get it back either. That's why the sun itself goes deep orange and why the air just beside it looks pale, milky white.

The extra colors you see in a good sunset — the pinks, magentas, and the way clouds catch fire — come from bigger particles in the air: dust, sea salt, pollution, wildfire smoke, or volcanic haze lingering high up. Those are large enough to scatter all wavelengths more evenly, which lengthens the range of colors and pushes them higher into the sky after the sun has already dropped below the horizon. The best sunsets happen after something has loaded the upper atmosphere with fine particles, and the reason a sunset fades so quickly is exactly the reason it exists at all: the path length through the air changes minute by minute, and once the sun dips far enough, the light goes below your horizon entirely.
