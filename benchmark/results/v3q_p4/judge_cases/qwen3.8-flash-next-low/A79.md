Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

ASSISTANT: GPUs are effective at inference for one structural reason: nearly every layer in a modern neural network reduces to the same few operations — multiply a big matrix by a vector, then a chain of pointwise math like activations, softmax, and layernorm. That is dense linear algebra with no branches and no dependencies between elements, which is exactly what a GPU's thousands of small cores and matrix units are built for. Modern NVIDIA chips add tensor cores that do the multiply-accumulate in mixed precision, so FP8 and now FP4 formats do more work per watt and per byte of memory traffic.

The deeper point is that autoregressive decoding — generating a transformer one token at a time — is limited less by how fast you can multiply than by how fast you can read the weights. Reading tens of gigabytes of model weights to produce a handful of new tokens is a low arithmetic-intensity workload, so the bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not FLOPS. That is why GPUs carry high-bandwidth memory stacked on the package, and why each generation has chased bandwidth harder than raw compute. Current flagship parts sit around 288 gigabytes of HBM4 per chip, according to NVIDIA's Vera Rubin disclosures from August 2026. GPUs also handle batching well: when you serve many requests at once, you turn that memory-bound problem back into a compute-bound one, and the GPU's raw throughput starts to pay off. And CUDA, cuDNN, TensorRT-LLM and vLLM mean almost any new model runs on a GPU the week it is published.

Now the three categories. A GPU is a general-purpose programmable machine: you write kernels, the hardware executes whatever you ask. An ASIC hard-codes the computation into the circuitry. Etched's Sohu is the cleanest example — transformer attention and MLP layers are fixed-function logic rather than instructions on a programmable unit, which removes instruction fetch, scheduling and cache management overhead at the cost of running only transformers. The extreme end is Taalas, which hard-wires the trained weights of one specific model into the silicon itself, reporting something like seventeen thousand tokens per second on Llama 3.1 8B. A specialized inference processor sits in between: programmable, but architecturally opinionated. Groq's LPU uses no external memory and no caches at all, with the compiler statically scheduling every instruction so timing is deterministic regardless of context length. Cerebras's wafer-scale engine puts the model in enormous on-chip SRAM with petabytes-per-second of bandwidth. Google's TPUs use systolic arrays; AWS Trainium and Inferentia, SambaNova's dataflow chips and Intel's Gaudi belong to this class too, though Intel has confirmed the Gaudi line is being discontinued in favor of its own GPUs.

A specialized chip wins when the workload is narrow, large, and stable. Four conditions matter. First, latency-critical serving at batch size one: interactive agents, voice, and reasoning chains where time-to-first-token and per-token latency compound. Cerebras has reported over two thousand tokens per second on Llama 3.3 70B, and Groq claims sub-millisecond per-token latency, versus roughly two hundred to three hundred for the best H100 and H200 hosted providers. Second, you are running a small set of popular models you can invest in optimizing. Third, the models are the shape the silicon expects — dense transformer decoders, not mixture-of-experts, multimodal, or diffusion; DeepSeek, Gemma and multimodal models are simply absent from Groq and Cerebras catalogs. Fourth, you have enough volume to amortize the tooling and the compiler risk.

Where the specialized chips lose: model capacity, since on-chip SRAM is hundreds of megabytes, not hundreds of gigabytes, so big models need many chips; ecosystem lock-in; model churn — a hard-wired transformer ASIC is a bet that attention remains dominant for several years; and often raw cost per token at high batch, where a B200 or B300 instance at a few dollars an hour comes out around eighteen to twenty-three cents per million output tokens.

One caveat on the numbers. The eye-catching claims — Etched's five hundred thousand tokens per second for an eight-chip server, Taalas's seventeen thousand — are vendor statements. MLPerf Inference v6.0, published in April 2026, had twenty-four submitting organizations, but Groq is not among them, and Etched's Sohu was pre-production with no public access. The credible pattern from independent runs is that specialized silicon wins by five to twenty times on latency at batch one for a handful of well-optimized open models, while NVIDIA's rack-scale systems win on capacity, throughput economics, and the fact that almost everything runs on them.
