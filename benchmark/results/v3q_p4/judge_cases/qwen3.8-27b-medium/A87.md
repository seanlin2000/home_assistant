USER: My projector is connected to a JBL Bluetooth speaker. Sometimes the audio becomes choppy when I connect my laptop, and playing video games on the projector causes more eye strain than playing on my monitor. What could cause these problems, and how would you systematically troubleshoot them?

ASSISTANT: Both of these have a few likely culprits, and the good news is that the same kind of methodical approach fixes both. Let me start with the audio.

For the choppy Bluetooth audio, the most likely causes are interference and source switching. Bluetooth operates on the 2.4 gigahertz band, which is the same band as most home Wi-Fi. When you connect your laptop, its Wi-Fi and Bluetooth radios wake up and compete for that same airtime, and that alone can make a weak Bluetooth link stutter. A second possibility is that the speaker is trying to handle two sources. Some JBL models let you pair multiple devices but only play audio from one at a time, so when your laptop connects, the speaker may be bouncing between the projector and the laptop, which sounds exactly like choppy audio. A third, less common cause is that plugging the laptop into the projector over HDMI triggers the projector to renegotiate its audio output, which can briefly reset the link to the speaker.

To work through it systematically, I would isolate variables one at a time. First, reproduce the problem with the speaker connected only to the projector, and see if it stutters before the laptop comes into play at all. If it does, the problem is the projector's Bluetooth itself, and you should update the projector's firmware. Then test the laptop pairing: deliberately pair your laptop to the speaker, and listen. If the audio drops out exactly when the laptop is connected as a second source, forget the laptop from the speaker's list of paired devices and keep the speaker dedicated to the projector. If the stutters happen when the laptop is just turned on nearby without pairing, suspect Wi-Fi interference, and switch the laptop to a five gigahertz Wi-Fi network, which leaves the 2.4 gigahertz band clear for the speaker. Finally, if you need the laptop's audio on that speaker anyway, the cleanest fix is often to skip Bluetooth entirely and use a wired connection, like a 3.5 millimeter cable to the speaker's auxiliary input, which eliminates the whole class of wireless problems.

Now for the eye strain with the projector. The root cause is usually that a projected image is fundamentally harder for your eyes than a monitor image, and the setup is probably amplifying it. Monitors have high native contrast, so blacks are truly black. Projectors reflect light, so blacks look gray, especially in any lit room, and your eyes work harder to resolve detail in low-contrast images. Other contributing factors are brightness mismatch, which happens when the room is much brighter than the screen or the screen is far too dim, plus focus and image geometry. If the image is even slightly soft, or distorted from keystone correction, your eyes strain to track it, which is brutal during a game where you're following fast motion. Refresh rate matters a lot too. If the projector is running below the refresh rate your monitor uses, or if its motion processing is on and doing weird interpolation, motion looks smeared or juddery and fatigue sets in fast.

I would troubleshoot in this order. First, set the projector to its native resolution and highest refresh rate, which on a modern model is usually 120 hertz, and turn off any motion enhancement or dynamic contrast features, which are common eyesores for gaming. Second, get the focus razor sharp and undo any keystone correction by physically centering the throw, because a distorted image is one of the most underrated causes of strain. Third, fix the lighting: dim the room lights to match the brightness of the screen, and if it's practical, put a soft bias light behind the screen rather than in front of it, because glare on a matte surface is the worst combination. Fourth, lower the contrast and brightness on the projector to comfortable levels and pick a neutral color temperature, since oversaturated or overly bright images are fatiguing.
