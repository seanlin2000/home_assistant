Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and the short version is that the color of the sky has to do with how light bounces off the air itself.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, each with its own wavelength. Blue light has short, choppy waves, and red light has long, smooth ones. When sunlight streams into our atmosphere, it bumps into all the tiny nitrogen and oxygen molecules floating in the air. Those molecules are much smaller than the light waves passing by, and here's the key part: they scatter the short, blue waves much more strongly than the long, red ones. So as the sun's light travels down through miles of air, the blue gets kicked around in every direction. That scattered blue light is what reaches your eyes from all over the sky, which is why it looks blue when the sun is high.

Now at sunset, the light has a much longer journey to reach you. Instead of coming straight down from above, it skims across the bottom of the atmosphere, passing through many times more air than it does at noon. All that extra distance means the blue light has been scattered away entirely, over and over, long before it reaches you. What's left to get through to your eyes is mostly the long red and orange waves that the air molecules don't bother scattering. So the sun itself looks deep orange or red, and the sky near the horizon glows the same way.

A couple of nice extras: clouds and dust in the air actually make sunsets even more dramatic, because the bigger particles scatter the remaining red and orange light beautifully, spreading it out. And it's why sunsets look especially vivid after volcanic eruptions, when fine ash hangs high in the air.

So really, the blue sky and the red sunset are the same effect seen from two different angles. Blue dominates when the light has a short trip through the air, and red takes over once the journey gets long enough to filter the blue out.
