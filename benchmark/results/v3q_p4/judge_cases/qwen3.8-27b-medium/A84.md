Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core of it is that stock valuations, at least in theory, are about the present value of all the money a company will ever earn, and interest rates are the yardstick you use to translate future money into today's money.

When you value a company using a discounted cash flow model, you take each year's expected profit or cash flow and divide it by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of how many years away that cash arrives. The discount rate is basically the return investors could get elsewhere for the same amount of risk, so when the Fed raises rates, that benchmark goes up, and the math is unforgiving: the further out a dollar lands, the more it gets shrunk. A dollar in two years loses a little, but a dollar in twenty years gets cut almost in half for the same rate hike. That's the whole mechanism.

Now here's why tech stocks feel the pinch more than most. Growth companies, especially young or fast-scaling ones, have a cash flow profile that's heavily weighted to the distant future. A lot of today's big tech names are valued on earnings that arrive five, ten, fifteen years from now. They're what investors call long-duration assets, in the same sense that a long-duration bond is very sensitive to rates. A utility or a consumer staples company, by contrast, pays you a steady stream now, so its value is less dependent on what happens to the far future. Same rate hike, but the tech multiple gets compressed much more violently because most of its value lives far out on the timeline.

There are a couple of secondary effects layered on top. Higher rates mean higher borrowing costs, so the cheap credit that fueled expansion and acquisitions gets more expensive, and that squeezes projected margins. And there's an opportunity cost story: when the Fed raises rates, safe bonds start paying meaningfully more, so the extra risk of owning volatile growth stocks has to be compensated with a bigger expected return, which pushes their prices down until the numbers make sense again. That's why you often see money rotate from high-growth tech into value stocks or bonds in a tightening cycle, and why a rate-cutting cycle tends to do the opposite, lifting tech the fastest.

One honest caveat: this is the textbook relationship, and it's a strong tendency rather than a law. Tech stocks can still rally in a rising-rate environment if earnings growth is strong enough to outrun the discounting, or if the market thinks the Fed is behind the curve. But all else being equal, higher rates mean a higher discount rate, which means lower present values, and the longer the duration of the cash flows, the bigger the hit.
