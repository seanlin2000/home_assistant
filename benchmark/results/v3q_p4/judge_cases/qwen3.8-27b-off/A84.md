Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a classic question about how monetary policy interacts with equity valuations, and it's really a story about time value of money. Let me walk through the mechanics.

When you value any stock, you're fundamentally estimating the present value of all the cash flows that company will generate in the future. That "present value" calculation discounts those future dollars back to today using an interest rate. The core insight is this: a dollar promised ten years from now is worth less than a dollar today, and the higher your discount rate, the more you shrink that future dollar when you bring it back to the present.

Here's where tech stocks get uniquely exposed. Growth companies—especially high-flying tech firms—derive a disproportionate share of their value from cash flows that are far out in the future, not the next few quarters. A mature utility or consumer staples company might earn most of its value from steady near-term dividends and earnings, but a tech company trading at a 40x P/E is pricing in years and even decades of rapid expansion. Financial analysts call this "duration" in an equity context: long-duration assets are ones whose cash flows are pushed far into the future, short-duration assets have their cash flows coming in sooner.

Now watch what happens when the Fed hikes. The risk-free rate—essentially the 10-year Treasury yield—is a key component of the discount rate investors use. When that goes up, every future dollar gets discounted more heavily. But notice it's not uniform: a cash flow arriving in three years is hit much less than one arriving in fifteen years, because you're compounding that higher discount rate over more periods. So long-duration growth stocks see their valuations compressed disproportionately compared to short-duration value stocks.

There are a few reinforcing channels on top of the pure math. First, when rates rise, safer assets like bonds and even money market funds become more attractive, so some capital rotates out of high-risk growth names into fixed income—this is sometimes called "TINA" (there's no alternative to stocks) reversing itself. Second, higher rates raise the cost of borrowing for companies that have been funding expansion with debt, which can slow growth and pressure earnings. Third, many tech firms carry a lot of cash on their balance sheets, and while that looks like a plus, it actually makes them more sensitive to rate moves in a subtle way: when rates rise, the opportunity cost of holding that cash instead of investing it goes up, and investors start demanding a higher return.

One nuance worth flagging: this relationship isn't absolute. If a rate hike is happening because the economy is booming and tech earnings are growing even faster than the discount rate, stocks can still rise.
