Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

# Exchange 1

USER: I have Philips Hue lights connected to a Hue Bridge. I want multiple people in my apartment to control the lights, including through voice commands. What is the best architecture for this, and what happens if the internet goes down?

ASSISTANT: Great question. Here's how it works, and where the real gotcha is.

The core idea is that everyone shares one Hue Bridge, and each person gets their own local access to it rather than a separate setup. The bridge is the single source of truth for your lights, so you want all control paths pointing at it instead of having multiple apps or bridges fighting over the same fixtures.

For people in the apartment, each person installs the Hue app on their phone and joins your account. Philips has a "share access" feature where the account owner adds other users to the bridge by entering a pairing code. Those people then get full control from their own phones, can create their own scenes and favorites, but they're all talking to the same bridge over your local network. That's the cleanest way to do multi-person manual control, and it works entirely on your home Wi-Fi without any cloud involvement.

For voice commands, this is where the architecture matters most, because there are two very different paths and only one of them survives an internet outage. The good news is that Hue has native local integrations with both Alexa and Google Assistant through their respective smart home skills. When you set those up, the bridge registers on your local network, and voice commands like "Alexa, turn off the living room light" can be handled locally by the Echo or Nest Hub talking straight to the bridge over Wi-Fi. That means for simple on/off and brightness commands, an internet outage should not stop your smart speakers from controlling the lights, as long as your home network is up.

The catch is that voice assistants need the cloud for anything more complex. If you say "Alexa, turn the lights to a warm sunset scene" or use a routine that's stored in the cloud, or if you're away from home and trying to send a command, that round-trips through Amazon or Google servers and will fail without internet. Also, the initial setup of the skill, adding new devices, and syncing across accounts all require the internet. So you want to lean on simple local commands for daily use and treat fancy routines as a nice-to-have that needs connectivity.

If you want something more robust and fully offline-capable, including complex scenes and automation without depending on Amazon or Google at all, Home Assistant is the stronger architecture. You run it on a small always-on device in your apartment, connect it to the Hue Bridge over the local network using the built-in Philips Hue integration, and then point both Alexa and Google (or even just Home Assistant's own voice features) at it. In that setup, scenes, automations, and even multi-user access live locally on your network, so an internet outage barely matters for in-home control. The tradeoff is that you're running and maintaining extra software, which is more setup than most people want.
