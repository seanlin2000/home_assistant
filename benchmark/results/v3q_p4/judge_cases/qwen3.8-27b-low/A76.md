USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended in 1991 with the formal dissolution of the Soviet Union, but it really unraveled over several years through a combination of structural causes and a final trigger that made collapse impossible to stop. I'd pick three events as the most important, and they fall neatly into the cause-versus-trigger distinction you asked about.

The first, and the deepest cause, is Gorbachev's program of Perestroika and Glasnost, which began in 1985. Perestroika tried to reform the economy without abandoning the one-party state, and it largely failed, leaving shortages worse than before. Glasnost, the policy of open political speech, was even more consequential: it let people openly criticize the system, debate history, and, crucially, voice the grievances of the non-Russian nationalities that had been suppressed for decades. So the reforms didn't just expose the system's weaknesses; they removed the very controls that had held the union together, without providing a workable alternative. That's the structural cause. Everything else flowed from it.

The second major cause is the collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe in 1989, which peaked with the fall of the Berlin Wall in November. Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria — the whole satellite system came apart in the space of about a year, and the Soviet Union did not send tanks. That was the decisive signal. For decades, the implicit threat of military intervention had been what kept Eastern Europe under Moscow's thumb and, by extension, what reassured the Soviet leadership that the union itself was unchallengeable. Once it became clear that the USSR would not, or could not, use force, the Baltic states, the Caucasus, and eventually Ukraine felt emboldened to demand their own sovereignty. So the Eastern European collapse was a cause in the sense that it removed the last deterrent and set domestic secessionist movements in motion.

The immediate trigger was the August coup of 1991. Hardline communists in the Soviet leadership — the State Committee on the State of Emergency — tried to seize power, arrest Gorbachev, and roll back the reforms. It failed within about seventy-two hours, largely because Boris Yeltsin, the president of the Russian Republic, refused to recognize it and rallied public support from atop the Russian White House in Moscow. But the failure was fatal in a different way than a success would have been. The coup destroyed whatever remained of Gorbachev's central authority, handed Yeltsin overwhelming legitimacy as the real leader, and convinced the republics that the center was gone for good. Within months, the Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian leaders signed the Belavezha Accords in December 1991, formally declaring the Soviet Union dissolved and replacing it with the Commonwealth of Independent States. Gorbachev resigned on December twenty-fifth, the Soviet flag came down from the Kremlin the next day, and the USSR was gone.

So to summarize the distinction: Gorbachev's reforms were the underlying cause that made the system fragile and its people willing to demand change. The collapse of Eastern Europe removed the military deterrent and set secessionist movements in motion within the USSR itself. And the failed August coup was the trigger that turned a slow unraveling into an irreversible break within a matter of weeks.
