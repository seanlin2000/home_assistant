Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: When the Fed raises rates, it pushes up the cost of borrowing across the economy, and that ripples through stock valuation in a pretty specific way. The core mechanism is the discounted cash flow model, which is how most investors think about what a company is worth. The idea is that a company's value is the sum of all the cash it will generate in the future, brought back to today in present-value terms. To do that "bringing back," you apply a discount rate. That discount rate is built on top of the risk-free rate, which is essentially the return on Treasury bonds, and Treasuries move directly with what the Fed does. So when the Fed hikes, the risk-free rate goes up, the discount rate goes up, and every future dollar is worth a little less in today's terms.

Here's where tech stocks come in, and why they get hit harder than, say, a utility company or a consumer staples brand. A growth stock like a software company often gets most of its valuation from cash flows that are ten, fifteen, twenty years out. Investors are paying today for a company that is still burning cash or growing revenue but is expected to become a cash machine much later. That makes it what's called a long-duration asset. The further out those cash flows sit, the more sensitive their present value is to changes in the discount rate. It's the same math that makes a bond with a thirty-year maturity more volatile to rate changes than a two-year note.

You can think of it this way. If the discount rate goes up by even a point or two, a dollar you expect to receive in three years is worth a modest amount less. But a dollar you expect in twenty years is worth a much larger percentage less, because you're compounding that higher discount rate over more periods. So a company whose value is concentrated in distant future cash flows sees its present value drop disproportionately. A mature company that's already generating most of its cash today is much less affected, because those near-term cash flows get discounted over only a short period.

There's a second layer that makes this particularly relevant for tech. High-growth companies often justify their valuations partly on the assumption that they can borrow cheaply to fund expansion, or that the broader economy will keep producing strong returns on their investments. When rates rise, the cost of that capital goes up, which can slow the growth trajectory that the valuation was built on. And because growth stocks have to deliver higher future returns just to justify their price, they also carry a wider spread above the risk-free rate. When the risk-free anchor moves, that whole valuation stack shifts.

So the short version is this. Rates up, discount rate up, distant cash flows hurt more than near-term ones, and tech and growth stocks are the ones whose value lives furthest in the future. That's why you see a pattern where a rate-hiking cycle tends to compress valuations in high-multiple tech names first and hardest, while more cash-rich, lower-growth names hold up better. It's not that the companies themselves are doing anything different on day one. It's the lens through which investors are valuing them that changes.
