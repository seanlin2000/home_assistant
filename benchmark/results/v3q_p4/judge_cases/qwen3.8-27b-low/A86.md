ASSISTANT: Here's the architecture I'd suggest, and then the offline story, which is the part people get wrong.

Start with what you already have. The Hue Bridge is the right hub for a multi-person setup, so keep it. It runs the Zigbee network locally, and it's what gives you the headroom you need: scenes shared across bulbs, schedules, remote access, and voice control through any of the major assistants. A no-bridge Bluetooth setup would be fine for one person in one room, but it caps out around ten lights, has no remote access, and gives you only limited voice support, so it's not the right base for what you're describing.

For multiple people, the clean move is to add each person as a user on the same bridge through the Hue app, rather than having everyone log into one phone or share an account. Everyone gets control from their own phone without you handing over credentials, and changes stay in sync. Then, for voice, I'd pick one assistant for the whole apartment and expose the lights there, either through the Hue integration or, on newer setups, Matter. You *can* hook the same lights into both Alexa and Google, but mixing them tends to create double-naming and weird conflicts, so it's simpler to standardize on whatever your housemates already prefer.

Now the offline part, and this is where it splits in two. The lights themselves are fine. The bridge keeps handling everything locally, so when the internet drops the Zigbee mesh keeps running, the Hue app on your home Wi-Fi can still turn lights on and off and change scenes, and any physical Hue switches or remotes you have will keep working. Schedules and scenes stored on the bridge continue. Hue is genuinely one of the better setups here because the bridge processes commands locally instead of bouncing them through the cloud.

The weak point is voice. Alexa and Google process your speech in their cloud, so if the internet is down, those voice commands will very likely stop working even though the lights and the bridge are perfectly healthy. The exception worth knowing about is Apple's HomeKit path, which does local processing for some commands, so if offline voice is genuinely important to you, that's the direction to lean. Otherwise, the practical fallback is a couple of physical Hue wall switches or remotes in the main spots, so nobody is helpless when the connection dies. And if your Wi-Fi router is still up but only the internet is down, the local app and switches keep working; if the power to the router drops, that's a different failure and the app won't reach the bridge either.

So in short: bridge as the hub, each person added as their own user, one voice assistant chosen for the whole place, and physical switches as your guaranteed-offline safety net, with the caveat that Alexa and Google voice won't survive an internet outage.
