Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Okay, here's the short version: sunlight actually contains all the colors of the rainbow mixed together. When that white light hits the tiny gas molecules floating in the atmosphere, the molecules knock the light around in every direction. That's called scattering.

Here's the key part: the molecules scatter the short, fast colors of the spectrum way more than the long, slow ones. Blue and violet are short and punchy, so they bounce around everywhere. Red and orange are long and lazy, so they mostly just plow straight through.

Now think about the difference between noon and sunset. At noon, the sun is high overhead. The light only has to cross a thin slice of atmosphere to get to your eyes, so most of the blue bounces around and fills the whole sky. You look up and everything is blue because the blue light has been scattered in all directions above you.

At sunset, the sun is right on the horizon. The light now has to squeeze through a much thicker wall of atmosphere to reach you. That thick wall scatters away almost all the blue before it gets there. What's left to punch through to your eyes is the red and orange stuff, which is exactly why the sky glows those colors.

One thing people often wonder: violet scatters even more than blue, so why isn't the sky violet? Two reasons. The sun doesn't actually emit as much violet as it does blue, and our eyes are just not very good at seeing violet. So blue wins.

That's the whole trick. Same light, same air, just a different distance the light has to travel.
