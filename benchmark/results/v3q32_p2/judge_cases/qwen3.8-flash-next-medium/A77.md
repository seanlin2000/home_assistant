Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow. As that light hits the air, it bumps into tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, and those molecules scatter the light in every direction. Blue light gets scattered far more easily than red light, because blue waves are shorter and rattle the molecules more. So during the day, light everywhere in the sky has its blue component knocked sideways toward your eyes, and the whole dome looks blue.

At sunset, though, the sun is low, and its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away completely, scattered so many times it never arrives. What survives the trip is the red and orange light with its longer, sturdier waves. So the sun itself, and the clouds it's illuminating, look red, orange and gold.

In short: blue skies are sunlight's blue light scattered short distances; red sunsets are what's left after the blue has been scattered away over a long distance. Clouds help sunsets because they give that remaining red light something to bounce off.
