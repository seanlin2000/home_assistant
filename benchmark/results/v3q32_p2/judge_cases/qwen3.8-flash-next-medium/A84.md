Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is the present value of all the cash it's expected to produce in the future, and the discount rate used to compute that value moves with interest rates. When the Fed raises rates, the risk-free benchmark rises, so investors demand a higher return from risky equities, and each future dollar is worth less today.

That hits technology hardest because tech stocks are "long-duration" assets — their earnings are weighted toward far-off years rather than near-term profits. A distant cash flow discounted at, say, ten percent shrinks much more sharply when rates rise than a cash flow arriving next year does. So the same growth story supports a smaller valuation.

There's a second channel: higher rates raise borrowing costs and typically slow the economy, which can cut the growth rates tech companies are priced on. Investors also rotate toward bonds and value stocks whose cash arrives sooner.

Two important caveats. The effect is about the discount rate, not the accounting value of a company's assets, and it applies to expected cash flows, so profitable tech firms with strong near-term free cash flow are less exposed than unprofitable, story-driven growth names. And a rate rise driven by strong demand can partly offset the discounting effect by boosting earnings — which is why real-world reactions vary.
