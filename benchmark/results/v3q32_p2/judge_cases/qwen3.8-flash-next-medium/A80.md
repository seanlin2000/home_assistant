Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Quantizing stores each weight in fewer bits than the usual 16. Weights alone: 16-bit about 140 GB; 4-bit 70 billion times half a byte, about 35 GB (real 4-bit files run roughly 38 to 42 GB because of scales and mixed precision); 2-bit about 17.5 GB (about 20 to 25 GB in practice). The context cache and activations come on top. Trade-offs: some loss of quality, small at 4-bit and marked at 2-bit (reasoning and factual accuracy suffer most); faster generation, because inference is limited by memory bandwidth; careful methods (GPTQ, AWQ, importance-weighted quantization) reduce the loss. Wrong memory figures are an error.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing means replacing each weight, stored as a 16-bit number, with a lower-precision one, say 4 or 2 bits. It cuts memory and bandwidth, and usually speeds up generation, at the cost of accuracy.

The arithmetic: 70 billion weights at 16 bits take about 140 GB. At 4 bits, one half-byte each, that's 35 GB. At 2 bits, a quarter-byte, about 17.5 GB. Add overhead for activation caches, KV cache and the quantization scales, so real-world figures run a bit higher.

The trade-offs: 4-bit quantization, in formats like GGUF or GPTQ, generally keeps most of the model's quality and is the practical sweet spot. Two bits is aggressive, often needing finer group-wise quantization, and typically degrades perplexity and reasoning noticeably, sometimes badly. Quantized models are also harder to fine-tune, and quality varies by task, with math and long-context work hit hardest.
