Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The stock market reflects expected profits of large, often multinational companies, and stock ownership is concentrated (in the US the richest tenth of households own the large majority of stocks), while people feel the economy through wages, prices, rent and jobs. Three concrete examples from any of: prices rising faster than wages while companies keep or raise margins; a handful of giant tech firms driving index gains while most businesses and regions lag; companies earning much of their profit abroad; cost cutting and layoffs that raise profits and share prices; low rates or stimulus inflating asset prices, which helps people who own assets (the K-shaped recovery of 2020 and 2021); housing costs climbing while the index climbs. Must give three distinct, concrete mechanisms, not three restatements of "the market is not the economy".

# Exchange 1

USER: Why can a country have a strong stock market while its average citizens feel like the economy is getting worse? Give three concrete examples of how this happens.

ASSISTANT: Stock markets measure the profits and expectations of large listed companies, not how ordinary households are doing, so the two can drift apart. Here are three concrete ways. First, ownership is concentrated. In the United States, the richest ten percent of households own the large majority of stocks, so a rally mostly lifts people who were already well off, while most families get their income from wages and have little in the market. Second, costs can outrun pay. If rents, groceries, insurance and childcare rise faster than wages, people feel poorer even while corporate profits and share prices climb. Companies can also grow profits by cutting costs, automating, or raising prices, which does not help workers. Third, markets look forward and are driven by things unrelated to the street economy: falling interest rates, a few giant technology firms, share buybacks, and global sales. A handful of companies can lift an index while many smaller businesses and their employees struggle. Add that people judge the economy by prices and job security, and the gap makes sense.
