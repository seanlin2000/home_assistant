Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of all the colors. When that light enters the atmosphere, it bumps into air molecules, which are much smaller than the wavelength of light. Those molecules bounce short wavelengths, blue and violet, around far more strongly than long wavelengths like red. So blue light gets scattered all over the sky, and wherever you look, some of that scattered blue reaches your eyes. Violet is scattered even more, but the sun gives off less of it, the upper atmosphere absorbs some, and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so the sky looks blue rather than violet. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light has to cross a much longer stretch of air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue has been scattered away from your line of sight, off to other people's daytime skies. What is left is the longer wavelengths, orange and red, so the sun and the clouds near it glow in those colors. Dust and haze can make sunsets even more vivid.
