Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of all the colors. When it hits the air, the tiny gas molecules bounce blue light around much more than red light, so blue gets scattered across the whole sky and reaches your eyes from every direction. That is why the daytime sky looks blue. At sunset the sun is low, so its light has to travel through far more air to reach you, a much longer path than at noon. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered out and sent off in other directions before it ever gets to you. What is left is the colors that scatter least, the reds and oranges, and those are what you see glowing near the horizon. Dust and haze can make the colors even stronger. So it is the same effect in both cases. Air scatters blue the most, and at sunset you are looking through so much air that the blue has already been stripped away.
