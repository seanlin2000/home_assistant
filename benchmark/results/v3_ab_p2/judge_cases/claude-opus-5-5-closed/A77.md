Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that sunlight is a mix of all colors, and air scatters blue light much more than red. Picture sunlight as a bundle of waves, with blue waves short and choppy and red waves long and lazy. The gas molecules in air are far smaller than those waves, and they bounce the short ones around much more strongly, roughly five to six times as much for blue as for red. So during the day, blue light gets scattered all across the sky and reaches your eyes from every direction, which makes the whole sky look blue. It isn't violet, even though violet is shorter still, because sunlight contains less violet and our eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light has to travel sideways through much more air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives, leaving the longer reds and oranges to come straight through. Dust, smoke and haze can deepen those colors even further.
