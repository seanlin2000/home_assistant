Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: Because a tech growth stock's value comes mostly from profits expected far in the future, and higher interest rates shrink what those future profits are worth today. Investors value a company by estimating its future cash flows and discounting each one back to the present at a rate tied to interest rates plus a risk premium. A dollar ten years away is discounted ten times over, so a higher rate cuts it far more than a dollar due next year. A mature company like a utility earns most of its value in the near term, so a rate hike barely dents it. A growth company may earn little now and most of its value a decade or more out, which makes it long duration, like a long-term bond, and very sensitive to the discount rate. As an illustration, a dollar fifteen years out is worth about forty-two cents today at a six percent discount rate, but only about thirty-two cents at eight percent. Higher rates also make safe bonds more competitive, raise borrowing costs for companies still burning cash, and cool investors' appetite for risk.
