Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

ASSISTANT: The setup you want is really three layers: the lights themselves, shared app access, and the voice assistant, and they each work a bit differently.

First, the lights and Bridge. Your Hue Bridge should stay plugged into your router with an Ethernet cable — it runs a local Zigbee network, so it does not need the internet to talk to the bulbs. That's the foundation for everything else and the reason your lights survive an outage.

Second, app access for everyone in the apartment. The best architecture is: one owner account owns the Bridge, and every other person gets their own Hue account and is invited into the home through the "Home access" or Device Sharing section of the Hue app. Each person signs in with their own email, accepts the invite, and then can control rooms, scenes, and switches from their own phone. The owner can give people lighting-only permission or full admin rights, and can remove someone's access at any time without touching the Bridge. This is better than everyone sharing one login, because you can revoke a roommate individually and see who has access.

Third, voice. The voice assistant — Alexa, Google, or Apple HomeKit — connects to the Hue system through one Hue account. In practice the cleanest thing is to link the assistant to the owner's account, and then everyone in the apartment just talks to the shared speaker(s). If people want their own personal speaker accounts, each of those assistant accounts would need its own Hue account that has been granted access to the home, which is doable but fiddly; one linked account serving the household is simpler and is how most homes do it.

Now the internet question, which is where people are often surprised. Because the Bridge and bulbs are purely local, when your internet drops: the Hue app keeps working on any phone connected to your Wi-Fi, automations and schedules that live on the Bridge keep running, and physical controls like the Hue tap switches and dimmers keep working against the lights. What stops is remote access from outside the apartment, and — the part that matters most for you — voice commands. Alexa and Google send your voice to their cloud to be understood, so without internet, voice control through them generally stops working. There are some narrow exceptions — certain Echo models can handle a small set of simple local voice commands, and they can control Zigbee lights paired directly to the Echo itself — but with your Bridge in the mix I'd treat voice as unavailable during an outage rather than counting on it.

So the practical recommendation: Bridge on Ethernet, one owner account with every housemate invited under their own Hue account, one voice assistant linked to the owner's account, and if you want lights you can absolutely rely on offline, add one or two Hue tap switches or wall remotes in the main rooms. That way the lights work no matter what happens to the internet or the speakers.
