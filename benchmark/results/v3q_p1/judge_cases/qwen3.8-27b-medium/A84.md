Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is, at its heart, the present value of all the cash it will ever pay you. To get that number, you take each future dollar and shrink it by the discount rate, which comes off the risk-free interest rate as its anchor. The discount rate is how much today's dollar beats tomorrow's, so when the Fed lifts rates, every future dollar is worth a bit less in today's terms, and the sum of them all falls.

The twist is that this effect is not equal across the market, because it depends on how far out in the time you get your cash. That's what "duration" means for a stock: not years of the company's life, but the weighted average time until its value actually arrives. A utility or a tobacco company that pays steady dividends and grows slowly is getting most of its value within the next few years, so it's a short-duration asset. A young technology company, by contrast, typically pays no dividends at all, and much of its promise is revenue and profit that won't show up for ten, fifteen, twenty years. All that value is concentrated far down the curve, which makes it a long-duration asset.

You can see the asymmetry in the math. If the discount rate jumps from three to five percent, a dollar arriving in five years is worth about ninety-one percent less in present-value terms. But a dollar arriving in twenty years, the same two-point hike wipes out roughly thirty-two percent of its value. The same absolute rate change does far more damage the further out the cash sits. So when the Fed raises, it's the distant, uncertain, still-future earnings of growth stocks that get hit hardest, while mature, cash-flow-heavy companies barely flinch. That's why a rate cycle tends to show up as tech compressing while value holds up.

There are two secondary channels that usually compound the first one. First, higher rates raise the cost of debt, which matters doubly for tech: companies with heavy investment needs and weak current cash flow get more expensive to fund, and the whole "buy growth on leverage" logic gets pricier. Second, and this is more behavioral than mechanical, higher rates make the safe alternative attractive. If a ten-year bond is paying more, a speculative software company with no earnings has to justify its multiple more urgently, so investors rotate from long-duration growth toward shorter-duration value. That rotation is what makes tech drawdowns in a tightening cycle so visible, even when the underlying business isn't suddenly worse.

A couple of honest caveats. The relationship is a strong tendency, not a law. In a fast economy, growth in tech earnings can outrun the discounting effect, and valuations keep rising even with rates climbing; the clearest break happens when the market starts doubting the future cash flows themselves. And "tech" spans a lot: a mature, dividend-paying infrastructure company behaves much more like a short-duration asset than a hypergrowth AI bet, even though both sit in the tech sector. But as a rule of thumb, every twenty-five basis points of Fed tightening lands disproportionately on the stocks whose value is furthest in the future, and that's the long-duration growth cohort.
