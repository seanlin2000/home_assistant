Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and honestly one of the best little bits of physics you can explain to someone at a dinner party.

The short version is that sunlight is actually a mix of all the colors, and the air in our sky acts like a sieve that scatters the shorter, bluer colors far more than the longer, redder ones. This is called Rayleigh scattering, named after the physicist who worked it out, but you can picture it more simply.

Think of a beam of white light as a crowd of people of different heights walking through a forest. The short, blue-wavelength "people" get bumped and scattered in every direction constantly by the molecules of gas floating in the air. So when you look up, you're seeing blue light that's been knocked around out of the direct path of the sun and bouncing toward your eyes from all over. The reds and oranges, the tall "people," mostly plow straight through without getting scattered.

Now here's the key part: during the day, the sun is high overhead, so its light takes a relatively short trip through the atmosphere to reach you. Just enough blue gets scattered around for the sky to glow blue, and you still get plenty of the full white light from the sun itself, which is why the daytime sun looks white or pale yellow.

But at sunset, the sun is right on the horizon, and its light has to travel a much, much longer path through the atmosphere to get to you. Over that long journey, nearly all the blue and green light gets scattered out of the beam entirely, before it can reach your eyes. What's left of the direct sunlight is the red and orange end of the spectrum, so the sun and the sky near it glow in those warm colors.

A couple of nice little details. The reason sunsets sometimes go especially deep red is that extra particles in the air—dust, smoke, pollution, even the aftermath of a volcano—scatter the light even more, filtering out even more of the remaining color and pushing it toward the deep red end. And you can see the same effect in reverse at the edges of a rainbow, where the short trip of light through raindrops separates the colors again.

So the whole show really comes down to one idea: blue light scatters easily, red light doesn't, and the length of the light's trip through the air decides which colors survive to reach you.
