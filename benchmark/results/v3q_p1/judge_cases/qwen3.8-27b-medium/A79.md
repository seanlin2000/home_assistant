Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why are GPUs so effective at AI inference? Explain the differences between GPUs, ASICs, and specialized inference processors. Under what circumstances could a specialized chip outperform an NVIDIA GPU?

ASSISTANT: GPUs are effective at AI inference for a few reasons that reinforce each other. First, the bulk of inference is matrix math. Every layer is a big multiplication of your input by the model's weights, plus a growing cache of past attention states called the KV cache. That kind of work splits cleanly across thousands of small arithmetic units, which is exactly what a GPU is: tens of thousands of cores working on different elements of the same operation at once. Second, inference is often memory-bound rather than compute-bound, meaning the chip spends more time moving the model's weights and the KV cache around than actually multiplying. GPUs pair their many cores with very high memory bandwidth through high-bandwidth memory, so they can stream large models at full speed. Third, modern GPUs have tensor cores, dedicated units that do 16-bit, 8-bit, and lower-precision matrix math at enormous throughput, and inference is increasingly run in low precision. Finally, there's an ecosystem advantage: CUDA has a decade-plus of head start, and every framework, quantization method, and serving stack is tuned for it.

Now the differences between the three categories.

GPUs are general-purpose parallel processors. They can do anything, which means they carry hardware and flexibility they don't always need. That flexibility is the point, but it costs power and transistors.

ASICs are application-specific integrated circuits, designed from scratch for a narrow workload. Google's TPUs and AWS's Trainium are the flagship examples. Because the designers know in advance exactly what the chip will compute, they can build a fixed data path, a memory hierarchy tuned to that workload, and skip all the generality hardware. The payoff is efficiency, usually several times more useful performance per watt and per dollar than a general GPU on the task it was built for. The cost is that if the task changes, the silicon is stranded.

Specialized inference processors are a newer, narrower breed: chips built only for running the inference step, often only for specific model families. Groq, Cerebras, SambaNova, and Etched are examples. They push tricks a general GPU won't, like putting most of the working set in on-chip SRAM for deterministic, low-latency token generation, or, in Etched's case, co-designing the chip around the exact model it will serve.

So when could a specialized chip beat an NVIDIA GPU? A few circumstances.

One, a stable and predictable workload. If a hyperscaler is going to serve one known model, at known shapes, for two or three years, a fixed-function design wins, because none of its transistors are wasted on generality it will never use. This is the classic TPU or Etched argument.

Two, the economics of scale. Even if a GPU has more peak floating-point performance, if the ASIC delivers equal tokens per second at half the power or a third of the cost, at datacenter scale the total cost of ownership dominates. Specialized chips tend to win on cost per token, not headline performance.

Three, when latency or bandwidth is the constraint and the model fits. A design like Groq's that keeps weights and activations in fast on-chip memory can generate tokens faster and more predictably than a GPU reaching out to external memory, especially on models small enough to fit in that fast memory.

Four, when precision is fixed. Inference increasingly runs at 8-bit or 4-bit. A chip designed around exactly that precision gets more useful throughput per square millimeter than one that has to handle full-precision floating point for other customers.

The flip side, and why GPUs still dominate, is that AI models change quickly. New architectures, mixture-of-experts designs, long context windows, multimodal fusion, new operators — every change erodes the advantage of a chip frozen to an older workload, and it hits the most specialized designs hardest. GPUs also win where deployments are small to medium, where flexibility and software maturity matter more than per-token cost, and during the rapid iteration phase of training or research. Roughly put: the more fixed, large-scale, and long-lived your inference workload is, the more a specialized chip can outperform a GPU on price and efficiency; the faster your workloads evolve, the more the GPU's generality is worth its premium.
