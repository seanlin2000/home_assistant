USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended not with a single dramatic moment but with a collapse from inside the Soviet system itself. The Soviet Union stopped existing on December 25, 1991, when Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as president and the red flag came down over the Kremlin. What led there can be traced to three pivotal events.

First, the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989. When Gorbachev stopped guaranteeing Soviet military intervention in Eastern Europe, the regimes there lost their shield. East Berliners began tearing down the Wall, and Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and other states followed with rapid democratic transitions. This was the moment the Soviet empire's satellite system ended, and it was enabled directly by Gorbachev's own reforms of glasnost and perestroika, which he had introduced to save a failing economy.

Second, the failed coup in August 1991. A group of hardline communists tried to overthrow Gorbachev and reverse the reforms, seizing his dacha and placing tanks in Moscow. It collapsed in roughly three days, largely because Boris Yeltsin stood on the tank in front of the Russian parliament and turned the coup into a spectacle that discredited the Communist establishment. The coup's failure removed the last real power base of the Communist Party and made Yeltsin, not Gorbachev, the dominant figure. Gorbachev's reforms had been meant to modernize the system; after August, the system simply had no one left to run it.

Third, the Belavezha Accords of December 1991, when the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus declared the Soviet Union dissolved and created the Commonwealth of Independent States. This was the legal act of death. Crucially, the trigger here was the December 1991 referendum, in which the Russian republic voted yes to keeping a reformed union, yet most other republics had already moved toward independence and Gorbachev's government could not hold any of them together.

Now for the distinction you asked about. The causes were long-term: an economy based on central planning that could not compete with the West, the huge burden of the arms race and of propping up Eastern Europe, a decade of war in Afghanistan, the oil price crashes of the 1980s that gutted Soviet export revenue, and the Chernobyl disaster in 1986, which exposed how brittle and secretive the system was. Those forces made the Soviet Union fragile. The triggers were the specific events that turned fragility into collapse: Gorbachev's decision to abandon the Brezhnev Doctrine, which let Eastern Europe walk away; the August coup, which destroyed the Communist Party's authority; and the 1991 referendum and accords, which converted the union's legal fiction into a formal dissolution. In short, the causes made the building about to fall, and the triggers were the blows that finally brought it down.
