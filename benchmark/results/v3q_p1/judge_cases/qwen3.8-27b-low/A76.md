ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended not with a single dramatic event but through a sequence of collapses that made the Soviet system unable to hold itself together. By the time the Soviet Union was formally dissolved on December twenty-sixth, nineteen ninety-one, the process had been in motion for over a year.

Here are the three most important events, and I'll separate the deeper causes from the immediate triggers as you asked.

**The first is the fall of the Berlin Wall in November of nineteen eighty-nine**, and more broadly the wave of revolutions across Eastern Europe that swept out every communist government from Poland to Romania in that single year. This is best understood as a consequence of Gorbachev's reforms. He had introduced glasnost, which meant openness, and perestroika, which meant restructuring the economy, and crucially he had abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, which had justified Soviet military intervention whenever a satellite state drifted away. Without the threat of tanks, the opposition movements in Eastern Europe moved faster than anyone expected. The loss of the Eastern Bloc was devastating for the Soviet Union because it removed the buffer zone that had been the strategic and economic foundation of the entire postwar arrangement. It also, psychologically, made clear to people inside the Soviet republics that the system was not eternal.

**The second is the failed coup of August 1991.** This is probably the single most decisive moment. A group of hardline communists in the government, including members of the security apparatus, tried to oust Gorbachev and reverse the reforms. They put him under house arrest and declared a state of emergency. But the coup collapsed in about three days, and it collapsed in large part because Boris Yeltsin, who was the head of the Russian republic, stood on the pedestal of the Russian parliament building in Moscow and defied the coup plotters. The coup's failure was catastrophic for the center. Gorbachev was left as a figurehead with no real authority, Yeltsin emerged as the dominant political force in the country, and the republics, which had been waiting for a signal, moved rapidly toward declaring independence. After August, there was simply no one left in the union who could say the other republics had to stay.

**The third is the Belovezhe Accords, signed on December eighth, nineteen ninety-one.** Yeltsin, along with the leaders of Ukraine and Belarus, met at a hunting lodge in the Belarusian forest and declared that the Soviet Union, as a subject of international law and geopolitical space, ceased to exist. In its place they formed the Commonwealth of Independent States, which was a loose association, not a union. This was the legal act that made the dissolution real. The remaining republics followed within a few weeks, and Gorbachev resigned as president on December twenty-fifth. The flag came down on the next day.

Now, the distinction between causes and triggers. The causes were structural and long-term. The Soviet command economy had been stagnating for well over a decade and was fundamentally unable to compete with market economies. The ideological legitimacy of Marxism-Leninism had eroded badly, and by the late eighties most educated citizens no longer believed the official narrative. Nationalism in the Baltic states, in the Caucasus, and in Ukraine had been suppressed for decades but was always there, waiting. Gorbachev's reforms were meant to save the system, but by loosening political control without being able to deliver economic improvement, they created a window in which all of those suppressed forces erupted at once. Those are the causes.

The immediate triggers were the specific events that made dissolution happen in nineteen ninety-one rather than in some modified or延缓ed form. The coup attempt destroyed the central government's ability to enforce the union. The Belovezhe Accords provided the legal mechanism. Without those two specific events, the Soviet Union might have lingered as a weakened federation for some time, as Yugoslavia did after its own breakup began, or it might have reformed into something different. But the coup and the accords made the end rapid and irreversible.

So in short: the system was rotten from the inside for years, but the wall fell in eighty-nine, the coup failed in eighty-one, and the accords were signed in December of ninety-one. Those three events turned a slow decay into a sudden death.
