On the architecture: keep the Hue Bridge as the single hub, wired by Ethernet into your router, with your bulbs talking to it over Zigbee. That local Zigbee layer is the part that never needs the internet, so it's the foundation everything else sits on. For multiple people, the important thing is that everyone's phone is on the same local Wi-Fi network as the bridge. Each person can just open the Hue app on their own phone and log in to the same free Hue account — the bridge serves all of them at once, and each person gets the full control, scenes and routines included. You don't need separate accounts or per-person bridges; one bridge, one account, everyone on it.

For voice, the simplest thing is to pick one assistant ecosystem and link it once to your Hue account through that assistant's app. After that, anyone in the apartment can just talk to the speaker — they don't each need their own account on it. The one genuinely important choice here is which assistant you standardize on, because it determines whether voice still works offline. I'd steer you toward Google: as of an update that rolled out in October 2026, Google Home now controls Hue lights through local Matter, and existing Hue bridges are being migrated to that in the background automatically with no re-pairing. The practical payoff is that voice commands and app control keep working during an internet outage, as long as the phone or speaker is on the same local network. Alexa and Apple/HomeKit are both fine day to day, but Google is the one where I can point to confirmed local, offline voice control for Hue specifically right now, so for an apartment where you care about resilience I'd anchor on that.

Two small habits that make it feel good for multiple people: name your rooms and groups clearly, because that's exactly what the voice commands and the app labels rely on, and keep the physical wall switches working on the lights that matter — a smart bulb is useless once someone kills power at the switch, so the wall switch should stay as a real fallback. If you can, a cheap UPS behind the router and the bridge is a nice bonus, since a brief power blip is otherwise what usually strands a bunch of bulbs in that messy "unreachable" state.

Now, what actually happens when the internet goes down. What still works: the bridge and the bulbs keep talking over Zigbee, the Hue app on any phone connected to your local Wi-Fi still controls everything, routines and scenes that are stored locally still fire, and — with the Google/Matter setup — voice commands keep working too. What breaks: remote access, so you can't turn the lights on from your phone while you're away, and any cloud-routed features like some assistant integrations that haven't moved to local control. One caveat worth knowing: your router has to keep its Wi-Fi up during the outage for any of this to work. Most home routers do, but some ISP gateways and cloud-managed mesh systems get flaky when the WAN link drops, and in that case your phone can lose the path to the bridge even though the bridge is perfectly alive. So if offline resilience matters a lot, it's worth testing once on purpose — cut the internet, leave the Wi-Fi on, and check that the app and a voice command still respond.

The one thing I'd double-check rather than guess: if you were leaning on Alexa or Apple specifically for voice, I'm less certain their Hue control is reliably offline the way Google's now is, so it's worth a quick test in your setup before you count on it during an outage.
