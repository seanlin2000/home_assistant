ASSISTANT: To quantize a model means to replace each of its weights with a lower-precision number, so you can store and compute with fewer bits per weight. A 70-billion-parameter model trained at full precision stores each of those weights as a 32-bit floating point value, or four bytes each, which comes out to about two hundred eighty gigabytes for the weights alone. Even the common 16-bit training format is still fourteen hundred... I mean one hundred forty gigabytes. When you quantize to four bits, each weight only needs a quarter of a byte, and at two bits it's a sixth of a byte. So for a model this size, the math works out to roughly thirty-five gigabytes at four bits and about seventeen and a half gigabytes at two bits. That's the whole point: it lets something that would not fit in consumer memory fit on a single large graphics card, a phone, or a laptop.

There is one important caveat to the numbers. Real quantized checkpoints carry overhead beyond the raw weights. The most common four-bit schemes, like the one from bitsandbytes or GGUF's Q4 variants, store small reference values per group of weights and sometimes keep the embedding and output layers in higher precision. So you should think of thirty-five gigabytes as a clean lower bound and expect the actual file to land a bit higher, often in the low-to-mid forties. Two-bit formats carry proportionally more overhead relative to their tiny core.

The trade-offs come in a few flavors. The first and biggest is accuracy loss. Going from full precision to four bits typically costs only a little, often a point or two on benchmark scores, and most people find the output still reads fine. Two bits is a much harsher cut, and you should expect noticeable degradation, more hallucination, worse reasoning, and trouble with anything that needs fine-grained knowledge. The second trade-off is speed versus flexibility. Four-bit inference is fast because the arithmetic is cheap and the memory footprint means weights stream from memory quickly, but you need hardware or software that actually supports those integer or block formats, and not every accelerator is good at them. The third trade-off is a loss of headroom for fine-tuning and prompt-based adaptation, because a compressed model has less room to shift its behavior. In practice four-bit is a sweet spot for running big models on limited hardware, while two-bit is mostly a research or extreme-edge case where you accept a meaningfully dumber model in exchange for it fitting somewhere that nothing else could.
