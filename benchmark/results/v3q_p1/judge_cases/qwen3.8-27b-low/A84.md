Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a great question, and the core of it is really just one mechanical fact about how we value anything that pays you over time.

When we value a stock, we estimate all the cash it will generate in the future and then discount those dollars back to today using some required rate of return. That discount rate is anchored to the risk-free rate, which is basically the yield on short-term government debt, plus a premium for the risk of the business. So when the Fed raises rates, the risk-free rate goes up, the discount rate goes up, and every future dollar you're expecting is worth a little less in today's terms.

Now here's where it gets interesting and where tech gets hit hardest. The *timing* of those future dollars matters enormously. A dollar you receive next year is barely affected by the discount rate. A dollar you receive twenty years from now is extremely sensitive to it. Let me give you a concrete example. If I discount a single dollar received in one year at five percent, it's worth about ninety-five cents today. At ten percent, it's about ninety-one cents. Not a huge difference. But a dollar received in twenty years, discounted at five percent, is worth about thirty-eight cents. At ten percent, it drops to about fifteen cents. That's a more than fifty percent collapse from the same five-point rate increase.

This is the same concept that bonds have, called duration. A long-duration bond, one that doesn't mature for thirty years, swings a lot more in price when rates move than a short-duration bond. And a growth stock whose value depends mostly on cash flows ten, fifteen, twenty years out behaves exactly like a long-duration bond. Its price is very sensitive to the discount rate.

Tech and other high-growth companies are typically the longest-duration assets in the market because their earnings profiles are back-loaded. They're often spending heavily right now, maybe even losing money, with the expectation that they'll scale into enormous profitability down the road. So a huge fraction of their market cap represents cash flows that are expected far in the future. That makes them disproportionately vulnerable when the discount rate rises.

A few reinforcing effects pile on top of the pure math. Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, which matters for companies that are funding growth with debt or equity at lower valuations. Higher rates make safe assets like Treasury bills more attractive, so investors are willing to accept a smaller premium for taking on the risk of a speculative growth story. And higher rates often slow the broader economy, which can push investors to lower their long-term revenue growth assumptions, which compounds the damage.

So in short: the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and because tech valuations are built mostly on distant future cash flows, those distant dollars get crushed by the higher discount rate in a way that near-term cash flows from a mature, dividend-paying company simply don't. It's the same reason a long bond drops more than a short bond when the Fed tightens.
