Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and it really comes down to one idea: sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of every color of the rainbow, and the air in our atmosphere treats each color differently.

Think of it this way. When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it hits the tiny molecules of gas all around you. The shorter, bluer colors of light bounce around and scatter in every direction much more easily than the longer, redder colors. So during the day, when the sun is high overhead, the light only has to punch through a relatively thin layer of air to get to your eyes. All that blue light gets scattered and splattered across the whole dome of sky above you, which is why the whole sky looks blue. You're not looking at any one patch of scattered light; you're seeing blue light bouncing back to you from every direction at once.

Now at sunset, things change. The sun is right down at the horizon, so its light has to travel through a much, much longer path of atmosphere to reach you. By the time it makes it all the way through, most of the blue and violet light has already been scattered away, bouncing off to the sides and never reaching your eyes at all. What's left is the longer-wavelength stuff: the reds, oranges, and yellows. That's the color that survives the long journey and gets through to you, so the sky near the sun looks fiery red and orange.

You can even see the effect in reverse: the clouds near the horizon glow red and gold at sunset, while the sky higher up, which is shorter of a path, might still have some of that blue in it, which is why you sometimes get that beautiful gradient from deep blue at the top to fiery orange at the bottom.

One small detail: violet light actually scatters even more than blue, but our eyes aren't very sensitive to violet, and the upper atmosphere absorbs a lot of it before it gets to us, so we mostly notice the blue.
