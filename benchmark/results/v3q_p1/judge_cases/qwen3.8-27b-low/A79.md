Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why are GPUs so effective at AI inference? Explain the differences between GPUs, ASICs, and specialized inference processors. Under what circumstances could a specialized chip outperform an NVIDIA GPU?

ASSISTANT: GPUs are effective at AI inference for a few interconnected reasons. First, inference is dominated by matrix multiplication and vector operations, and GPUs have thousands of small cores that can execute those operations in parallel. Second, modern GPU architectures pair those cores with very high-bandwidth memory, often HBM, so the compute units aren't starved waiting for data. Third, the arithmetic intensity of a forward pass — the ratio of floating-point operations to bytes moved — happens to sit right in the sweet spot where a GPU's compute-to-memory ratio shines. Finally, there's the software ecosystem: CUDA, TensorRT, cuDNN, and the model serving stacks built on top of them mean that even a less-than-perfectly-matched workload can still get decent utilization.

Now for the differences between the three categories.

A GPU is a general-purpose parallel processor. It was designed for graphics, repurposed for machine learning, and has become the default because it handles a wide range of workloads — training, inference, different model families, different precision levels — without redesigning the silicon. You pay for that flexibility in energy efficiency and, at the margin, in raw performance per watt for any single fixed workload. The NVIDIA H100 or B200 are the canonical examples.

An ASIC, or application-specific integrated circuit, is designed from the ground up for one task or a narrow family of tasks. It throws away everything it doesn't need — general-purpose registers, branch prediction, a flexible memory hierarchy, the ability to run arbitrary code — and replaces those with hard-wired data paths optimized for the target operation. Google's TPU, Amazon's Inferentia, and Cerebras' wafer-scale engine are ASICs in this sense. The tradeoff is stark: if your workload matches what the chip was designed for, you get dramatically better performance per watt and often better raw throughput. If your workload drifts, the chip is a brick.

Specialized inference processors sit between those two, though the line is blurry. They're ASICs or near-ASICs designed specifically for inference rather than training. Apple's Neural Engine, Qualcomm's NPU in their phone chips, Groq's LPU, and SambaNova's RDU are examples. They share the ASIC's efficiency advantage but often add some programmability or model-architecture flexibility to avoid being locked to a single model. Groq, for instance, uses a deterministic, software-scheduled execution model that eliminates the non-deterministic latency you get from a GPU's hardware scheduler, which matters a lot for real-time inference.

So when could a specialized chip beat an NVIDIA GPU?

The most obvious case is a fixed workload at massive scale. If you know you're serving one model, at one precision level, with one batch-size distribution, and you'll be running it for years at a scale where energy costs dominate, an ASIC tuned to that workload can be two to four times more energy-efficient per token generated. At datacenter scale, that translates directly into dollars.

Second, latency-sensitive applications. If you need deterministic, low-tail-latency inference — think real-time voice, autonomous driving, or interactive agents — the scheduling overhead and variability in a GPU pipeline can be a real problem. Chips like Groq's LPU are designed to eliminate that variability.

Third, if the model uses precision or quantization formats that a GPU handles inefficiently. GPUs support FP16 and BF16 well, and INT8 reasonably, but INT4 and mixed-precision schemes are often emulated or run at reduced throughput. An ASIC can have a native INT4 or FP8 data path built in.

Fourth, on-device and edge scenarios. You can't put an H100 in a phone or a car. A purpose-built NPU with a few tops of INT8 performance and a tiny power envelope will always beat a GPU in that context, because the GPU's general-purpose overhead is simply too large.

The counterpoint is that GPUs win when the workload is uncertain, changing, or mixed. If you're a research lab trying a dozen model architectures a month, or a cloud provider serving a heterogeneous portfolio of models, the flexibility of a GPU pays for its inefficiency. The specialized chip's advantage is real but conditional — it's a bet that your workload won't change, and that's a bet that's getting harder to make as model architectures evolve.
