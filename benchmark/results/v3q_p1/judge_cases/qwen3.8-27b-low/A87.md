USER: My projector is connected to a JBL Bluetooth speaker. Sometimes the audio becomes choppy when I connect my laptop, and playing video games on the projector causes more eye strain than playing on my monitor. What could cause these problems, and how would you systematically troubleshoot them?

ASSISTANT: These are two separate problems, so let's take them one at a time.

**The choppy audio**

The most likely cause is that your JBL speaker is handling two Bluetooth sources at once. Most JBL speakers support what they call multipoint, which lets them pair with two devices simultaneously, but the audio quality drops when both are active. The speaker is essentially splitting its limited Bluetooth bandwidth between the projector and your laptop, and that's where the choppiness creeps in.

There are a few other contributors to rule out. Bluetooth shares the 2.4 gigahertz band with Wi-Fi and USB 3.0 devices, so if your laptop's Wi-Fi adapter or a nearby USB hub is active, it can introduce interference. Your laptop's power management settings may also be putting the Bluetooth adapter to sleep briefly, which causes micro-dropouts. And firmware bugs on any of the three devices — speaker, projector, or laptop — can all manifest as audio stuttering.

Here's how I'd work through it systematically. First, isolate the variable. Play audio from the laptop to the speaker with the projector disconnected. If it's clean, then connect the projector and see if the choppiness returns. That confirms you're dealing with a multipoint or bandwidth issue rather than a problem with the laptop or the speaker on its own.

Next, if multipoint is the culprit, test whether the speaker's multipoint feature can be disabled in its settings or via the JBL app. If you can't disable it, try connecting only one device at a time and switching manually when you need to change sources.

Then check for interference. Move the speaker a bit farther from your router and any USB 3.0 hubs or hard drives on the laptop. If you have a dual-band router, make sure the laptop is on the 5 gigahertz band so it's not fighting for the same airtime.

After that, update firmware. Check the JBL app for a speaker firmware update, check the projector's system menu for a firmware option, and make sure your laptop's Bluetooth driver is current. These three devices shipping with slightly mismatched firmware is a common source of exactly this kind of intermittent stutter.

Finally, as a control, try a wired connection. If the projector has a 3.5mm audio out, run a cable from it to the speaker's AUX input. If the choppiness disappears, you've confirmed it's a Bluetooth-specific issue and you can decide whether a wired connection is acceptable or whether you want to invest in a Bluetooth transmitter with a longer, more stable range.

**The eye strain**

This one has several possible causes, and they stack on top of each other, so it's worth checking each one.

The biggest factor is almost certainly refresh rate. Gaming monitors commonly run at 144 or 240 hertz, while most projectors top out at 60 or 120. At 60 hertz, fast motion on screen is blurrier and your eyes work harder to track it. If your projector supports 120 and you're only getting 60, that's a quick fix. Check your input settings and make sure the signal from your console or PC is actually sending 120.

Second is resolution and sharpness. If your monitor is 1440p or 4K and the projector is 1080p, the image is noticeably softer. Your eyes strain more trying to resolve detail on a less sharp image, especially for text and fine game elements. This one is harder to fix without a better projector, but you can help by sitting at the right distance. If the image is too large relative to your viewing distance, your eyes are constantly saccading across the frame. The general rule is that your distance should be roughly 1.5 to 2 times the diagonal of the projected image. If you're closer than that, step back or use a larger throw lens.

Third is contrast and ambient light. A projector's image is only as good as the darkness in your room. Any ambient light washes out the blacks and forces your pupils to work harder to separate light from dark. If you're playing in a room with windows or overhead lights, try dimming them significantly. A high-gain projection screen also helps by reflecting more light back at you, which improves perceived contrast compared to a white wall.

Fourth, and this is specific to DLP projectors, is flicker. DLP projectors spin a color wheel to produce color, and even though the wheel spins fast, some people are sensitive to the residual flicker, especially at lower brightness settings. If your projector is DLP, try increasing the brightness or checking if there's a "flicker reduction" or "color wheel speed" setting. A quick test: hold your phone's camera up to the screen while a game is running. If you see rolling bands or a shimmer in the camera feed that you can't see with your naked eye, flicker is likely a contributor.
