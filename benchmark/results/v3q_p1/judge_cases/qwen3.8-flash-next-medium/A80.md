# AQLM vs QuIP: Extreme Compression for Consumer Hardware
## The 2-Bit Frontier: Why Extreme Compression Matters
QuIP (Quantization with Incoherence Processing) takes a different approach by applying a mathematical transformation to the model weights before quantization. By rotating the weight matrices to make them 'incoherent'—spreading outlier magnitudes uniformly—QuIP ensures that standard rounding-based quantization suffers minimal information loss. This results in a simpler, faster inference kernel compared to AQLM, as dequantization is just a lookup and a rotation. For Llama 2 7B at 2 bits, QuIP achieves a WikiText-2 perplexity of 6.12, trading a slight accuracy degradation for a more hardware-friendly compute pattern.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing means replacing the model's stored weight values, which normally sit in 16-bit floating point, with values from a much smaller set: 16 possible values if you use 4 bits per weight, or just 4 possible values if you use 2 bits. Each real-valued weight is snapped to the nearest available level, and you keep a small amount of extra bookkeeping, usually a scale factor and an offset for each group of weights (commonly groups of 128), so the decoder can reconstruct something close to the original number at run time. It is usually done after training, by running a calibration dataset through the model and choosing the rounding that hurts the outputs least, rather than by retraining.

For the memory question, a 70-billion-parameter model in 16-bit precision needs 140 gigabytes just for the weights. At 4 bits, that halves to 35 gigabytes, or about 32.6 of what an operating system calls gigabytes. At 2 bits it is 17.5 gigabytes, about 16.3. Those figures are weights only; the per-group scales and offsets typically add a couple of gigabytes on top, and separately you still need room for activations and the key-value cache, which grows with context length and batch size and is not shrunk by weight quantization at all. So 4-bit puts a 70B model in the range of a 48GB workstation card, while 2-bit can just about squeeze onto a 24GB consumer GPU with a modest context window.

The trade-offs are real but uneven. At 4 bits the damage is small: with modern methods like GPTQ or AWQ, which handle the handful of unusually large "outlier" weights carefully, the model keeps the great majority of its benchmark performance and perplexity rises only slightly. That is why 4-bit is the practical default, and why QLoRA-style fine-tuning uses 4-bit weights.

Below 4 bits, simple rounding-based methods fall apart. One source I looked at put it bluntly: pushing naive scalar quantization into 3-bit and 2-bit causes severe perplexity degradation and outright task failures, because the accumulated rounding error overwhelms the network's ability to compensate. Getting a usable 2-bit model requires cleverer schemes: QuIP and QuIP# rotate the weight matrices with random orthogonal transforms so the weights and the error-sensitive directions are "incoherent," meaning they are not aligned with the coordinate grid you are rounding onto, then round adaptively; AQLM instead represents each group of weights as a sum of vectors drawn from learned codebooks; QTIP uses trellis codes for better packing. Those make 2-bit genuinely viable. On Llama 2 70B, reported WikiText-2 perplexities for 2-bit models from QuIP#, AQLM and QTIP land in roughly the 5.6 to 6.3 range, versus a full-precision baseline in the low threes, so the degradation is noticeable rather than catastrophic. I'd treat those benchmark numbers as method-specific and somewhat promotional, since several came from vendors comparing their own stack.

Beyond raw accuracy, 2-bit costs you in other ways. Compression takes longer and often needs a calibration or fine-tuning pass. The dequantization step at inference is heavier, so 2-bit is not automatically faster; 4-bit integer formats usually win on throughput, and 2-bit vector-quantization methods can be limited by codebook size, since a large codebook does not fit in fast cache memory. Some capabilities degrade faster than perplexity suggests: arithmetic, code generation, long-context recall and tool or function calling tend to suffer more than fluent general prose. And quality is uneven across model sizes and families, so a 2-bit quant of one model is not interchangeable with another's.

The rule of thumb: 4-bit is a good deal, trading a few points of quality for a factor of four in memory; 2-bit is the frontier, trading meaningful quality and speed for a factor of eight, and worth it mainly when the memory ceiling is the hard constraint.
