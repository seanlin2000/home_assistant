A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core of it is a valuation idea, not a sentiment idea, and it comes down to one question: how much is a dollar you'll receive in the future worth today?

A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will eventually produce. To get that number, you discount each future dollar back to today at some rate — the discount rate — which is basically the risk-free Treasury yield plus a premium for the risk of owning a business instead of a bond. When the Fed raises its policy rate, short-term yields rise, the whole yield curve tends to shift up, and the discount rate investors use in that calculation goes up with it. A higher discount rate means every future dollar is worth less today.

That would hurt every stock, but it hurts technology companies disproportionately because of *when* their cash is expected to arrive. A utility or a consumer staples company earns a lot of cash soon — its profits are roughly here now. A high-growth tech company, by contrast, earns relatively little today and its value is mostly a bet on profits years or decades out. In finance we call those long-duration assets, by analogy to long-maturity bonds: the cash flows are far away, so they're far more sensitive to the discount rate.

The arithmetic makes it vivid. Suppose you're promised a hundred dollars at the end of some number of years, and the discount rate goes from three percent to six percent. If the money arrives in two years, its present value falls from about ninety-four to eighty-nine — a drop of roughly five and a half percent. Not much. But if that money arrives in ten years, it falls from about seventy-four to fifty-six, a drop of about twenty-five percent. Push it out to fifteen years and you're down nearly thirty-five percent. Same rate change, wildly different damage, entirely because the cash was further away.

There's a second reason tech is exposed, and it's the earnings themselves rather than the math of discounting. Many of the companies most sensitive to rate hikes are unprofitable or barely profitable, funding a growth push with cheap debt or with the expectation of profits that haven't arrived yet. Higher rates mean more expensive borrowing, higher interest expense, and a tougher path to profitability. Slow-growth, cash-rich companies have the same discount-rate problem in theory but a much smaller one in practice, because so much of their value is already realized cash rather than a promise.

And there's a portfolio channel on top of that. Higher rates make risk-free or low-risk assets genuinely competitive — if a Treasury pays five percent, a speculative growth story has to clear a much higher bar to be worth owning, and money rotates out of the highest-beta names. Higher rates also tend to slow the economy and business tech spending, which nibbles at the growth those stocks are priced on.

Two honest caveats. First, markets anticipate, so the pressure usually shows up when rates are *expected* to rise, not when the Fed actually moves; a hike that's already priced in can do very little. Second, the relationship isn't mechanical. The 2023–24 period is the standard counterexample: rates were high and rising, yet a handful of very large technology companies rallied hard on the AI buildout, because their expected cash flows were revised upward faster than the discount rate was. A higher discount rate compresses value; a bigger cash-flow forecast expands it. Which one wins is the whole contest. What's reliable is the sensitivity — long-duration growth stocks are the ones with the most to lose when the discount rate rises, and the ones the market sells first when it isn't sure.
