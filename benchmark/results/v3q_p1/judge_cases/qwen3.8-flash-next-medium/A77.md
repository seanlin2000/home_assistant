Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that the sky is blue because of the way sunlight bumps into the air around you, and sunsets are red because that same process gets pushed to its extreme when the light has to travel through a much longer stretch of atmosphere.

Here's the longer version. Sunlight looks white, but it's actually made of all the colors of the rainbow mixed together. Light travels in waves, and each color is a wave of a different size. Blue and violet waves are short and scrawny; red and orange waves are long and sturdy.

Now the atmosphere is full of nitrogen and oxygen molecules, which are tiny, much smaller than the wavelengths of visible light. When sunlight hits them, the light bounces off in new directions. This is called Rayleigh scattering, and the key thing about it is that it strongly favors the short, scrawny waves. Blue and violet light get flung around in all directions easily, while red light pretty much plows straight through without being bothered.

So during the day, look up away from the sun and what you're seeing is all that blue light that got knocked sideways by the air above you, scattered down into your eyes from every direction. The violet light gets scattered even more than blue, actually, but our eyes are much less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up in the atmosphere, so the mix we perceive comes out blue.

Sunset is where it gets interesting. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to cross a far thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you than it does when the sun is overhead. On that long journey, the blue light gets scattered away so many times that essentially none of it survives to arrive at your eye. What's left is the long-waved stuff that was never scattered much in the first place: the reds, oranges and yellows. The blue light didn't disappear; it was scattered out of the beam and sent somewhere else, which is part of why the sky near the horizon can glow with that band of color while the sky overhead fades to a deeper blue or purple.

Two things make sunsets especially dramatic. Dust, smoke, pollution and volcanic ash in the air add larger particles that scatter light in messier ways, deepening the reds. And when there's a thin layer of haze, the remaining light gets filtered further, which is why the most spectacular sunsets often happen after a big eruption or in dry, dusty seasons.

One common misconception worth clearing up: the sky isn't blue because it's reflecting the ocean. That's an old idea and it's wrong. The blue comes from the air itself, which is why the sky is blue over land too, and why astronauts see a black sky with a bluish halo glowing around the edge of the Earth.
