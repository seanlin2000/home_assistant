ASSISTANT: I have to push back on the premise, because it's the opposite of what happened. The Fed did not cut to zero last month. At its October 27–28 meeting it held the federal funds target range steady at 3.75 to 4 percent, and it did so on the heels of a quarter-point *increase* in September. Two members of the committee actually dissented because they wanted another hike, and rate futures are pricing a high chance of another increase in December. So the direction of travel right now is tightening, not easing.

Mortgage rates reflect that. As of this week the national average on a 30-year fixed is sitting somewhere in the neighborhood of 7 percent, give or take a few tenths depending on which survey you read, with 15-year loans in the mid-sixes. New York averages are a shade above the national figure. Not zero, and not close to it.

It's also worth knowing that the Fed funds rate isn't what sets your mortgage rate directly. Long-term mortgages track the 10-year Treasury and mortgage-backed security yields, which move on inflation expectations and term premium. Even a genuine cut to zero wouldn't automatically drag a 30-year note down to zero.

So the real question is whether *your* existing rate is high enough to beat today's roughly 7 percent market, once you pay closing costs. If you refinanced in 2020 or 2021, you're probably in the threes, and refinancing now would almost certainly cost you money over the life of the loan. If you bought in 2023 or 2024 at 7.5 or 8 percent, there's a real case for it, and the fact that rates may tick up further argues for moving sooner rather than waiting for a better number.

Tell me your current rate, your remaining balance, roughly how many years are left on the loan, and how long you plan to stay in the home, and I'll run the breakeven math for you.
