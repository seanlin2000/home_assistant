On 26 December 1991, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved and ceased to exist as a sovereign state. It also brought an end to the Soviet Union's federal government and to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's efforts to reform the Soviet political and economic system in an attempt to stop a period of political and economic stagnation.
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 resulted from several factors: chronic economic stagnation, the unsustainable financial burden of the arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, intense ethnic nationalism and separatism within its union republics, and the destabilizing effects of Gorbachev's reforms (particularly glasnost and perestroika).
The process began with growing unrest in the country's union republics developing into an incessant political and legislative conflict between them and the central government. Until its final years, the Soviet Union was made up of 15 union republics that served as the homelands of different ethnic groups. Estonia was the first union republic to declare state sovereignty on 16 November 1988. Lithuania was the first republic to declare independence, in March 1990.

## Consequences
### Economic decline and excess mortality
In the decades following the end of the Cold War, only five or six of the post-Soviet states are on a path to joining the wealthy capitalist states of the West, and most are falling behind, some to such an extent that over 50 years will be needed before they catch up to how they were before the dissolution of the Soviet Union. As of 2011, the experience of former Soviet republics is largely negative, with most states having suffered extreme Gross Domestic Product (GDP) loss, developmental stagnation, low happiness, and population decline.

In 1993 a series of economic reforms began to go into effect, initially enacted to offset the economic imbalances which was a result of the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. The main aspect of these reforms was to legalize the then illegal U.S. Dollar and regulate its usage in the island's economy.
### Sports and "Unified Team"
The breakup of the Soviet Union saw a massive impact in the sporting world. Before its dissolution, the Soviet football team had just qualified for Euro 1992, but its place was instead taken by the CIS national football team. After the tournament, the former Soviet Republics competed as separate independent nations, with FIFA allocating the Soviet team's record to Russia.
## Legacy
In 2013, the American Gallup analytics company found that a majority of citizens in four former Soviet countries regretted the dissolution of the Soviet Union: Armenia, Kyrgyzstan, Russia and Ukraine. In Armenia, 12% of respondents in 2013 said the Soviet collapse did good, while 66% said it did harm. In Kyrgyzstan, 16% of respondents in 2013 said the Soviet collapse did good, while 61% said it did harm. Ever since the Soviet collapse, annual polling by the Levada Center has shown that over 50 percent of Russia's population regretted its collapse. Consistently, 57% of citizens of Russia regretted the collapse of the Soviet Union in a poll in 2014 (while 30 percent said otherwise), and in 2018 a Levada Center poll showed that 66% of Russians lamented the fall of the Soviet Union. In 2005, Russian president Vladimir Putin called the dissolution of the USSR as "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the 20th century".
In a similar poll held in February 2005, 50% of respondents in Ukraine stated they regretted the disintegration of the Soviet Union. In 2013, according to Gallup, 56% of Ukrainians said that the dissolution of the Soviet Union did more harm than good, with only 23% saying it did more good than harm. However, a similar poll conducted in 2016 by a Ukrainian group showed only 35% Ukrainians regretting the Soviet collapse and 50% not regretting it.

The military budget of the Soviet Union was the portion of the military budget the portion of the overall budget of the USSR government that was allocated for the funding of the Soviet Armed Forces. Beyond the five armed services supervised by the Soviet Ministry of Defence (Strategic Rocket Forces; Air Defence Forces; Air Forces; Ground Forces; and Navy, plus the Soviet Airborne Forces), there were a wide variety of other armed organisations beyond the MOD, for example, the KGB Border Troops and Soviet Internal Troops.
Military budgets finance employee salaries and training costs, the maintenance of equipment and facilities, operations, and development and procurement of weapons, equipment, and vehicles (especially via the Military-Industrial Commission of the USSR). Especially since 1947, the level of military spending of the USSR was a matter of dispute, since the USSR did not publish reliable figures for its military expenditure. The burden of its military expenditure is believed to have been amongst factors contributing to the fall of the Soviet Union. "The Soviet economy was in.. serious trouble and the burden of military expenditure was much to blame" was the consensus among "most of the Soviet elite" as Gorbachev took over.
## History
### The Gorbachev era
#### Glasnost
In 1987, however, it was decided to declassify Soviet military expenditure within two or three years. In May 1989 a figure of 77.3bn rubles was finally published, or roughly 8.4% of Soviet GDP. This was, however, believed by many including Dmitri Steinberg, Soviet officials, and the CIA to still represent only a partial disclosure of the Soviet military budget.
## Accuracy of official statistics and alternative estimates
### Difficulties in estimation
However, attempts at re-estimation of the Soviet military budget, particularly attempts to produce dollar-figure estimates to compare it to the military expenditure of other countries, faced significant problems. This was partly because dollar-figure estimate of Soviet military spending represented what Soviet weapons, forces and military activities would cost if developed, purchased and operated in the United States. As such they did not represent the true burden of expenditure on the Soviet economy. Moreover estimation of the burden of Soviet military spending on the Soviet economy as a whole depended on estimations of the size of the Soviet economy, which was also a matter of debate. Additionally, the nature of the Soviet command economy made comparisons difficult, since, as Prof. Mikhail Burnstam of the Hoover Institute pointed out, the only measurable cost to the Soviet state as a whole of an individual weapon-system would be simply the labour used to create it, since the raw materials used to create it were bought from the state and any profit made from its sale would also be appropriated by the state. Moreover, since the cost of labour in the USSR was low due to the lack of free trade unions, this resulted in a very low cost compared to that of a comparable weapon-system produced in the west.
### "Building block" versus "residual" estimates
One method of estimation favoured by the US Central Intelligence Agency used a so-called "building block" (AKA "direct costing", known officially as Strategic Cost Analysis Model, or SCAM) method of estimation whereby the value of individual components/artefacts used by the Soviet military was either obtained from official sources or estimated, and the total cost of all these items summed. The CIA twice made substantial revisions to their estimates, the first time in May 1976 where the estimates nearly doubled due to what was believed to be a previous under-stating of the price of Soviet equipment and size of Soviet forces (and thus cost of maintaining them), and the second time in 1983 the CIA produced a downward revision of roughly 2-4% due to lower Soviet economic growth.
This method of calculations was criticised by analysts including Steven Rosefielde as understating the speed of growth of Soviet military expenditure due to false assumptions and failing to account for increases in price of Soviet military equipment due to their increasingly advanced nature. Rosenfielde accused the CIA of practising "false science" to minimise the degree of militarisation of the Soviet economy and even of using statistics manipulated by Soviet counterintelligence. Former CIA analyst William T. Lee, who believed the CIA was understating Soviet expenditure, also criticised the CIA's methodology as being too reliant on US defence companies to produce estimates for the cost of Soviet weaponry.
In contrast, Franklyn D. Holzman criticised the CIA's method of estimation as being, in his view, flawed due to their assumption of equivalence between US and Soviet military equipment when US military equipment and personnel was of a higher quality (and hence cost). Holzman focused particularly on the "index number problem", where, in his view, the CIA lacked prices in rubles for specific items and so instead valued them in dollars and then converted them based on the price of items whose price was known in rubles, which he believed tended to lead to an overstatement of Soviet military spending. Holzman charged the CIA with doing this to legitimise the increased military spending of Ronald Reagan's administration.
Writing in 1996, Robert M. Gates admitted that CIA estimates of Soviet expenditure had "understated the full cost of the Soviet military". As a result, Gates believed they had failed to properly estimate the burden of military spending on the Soviet Union.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War and the Soviet Union ended at different moments, and it helps to separate them. The Cold War is usually dated as ending in 1989, when Bush and Gorbachev met off Malta on December second and third and declared it over; the Soviet Union itself did not formally cease to exist until December twenty-sixth, 1991. The arms race had already been winding down before that, especially after Gorbachev and Reagan signed the INF Treaty on December eighth, 1987, which eliminated an entire class of intermediate-range nuclear missiles.

Now, the distinction you asked about. Causes are the deep conditions that made the system unable to keep going; triggers are the specific events that made collapse sudden and irreversible once those conditions were already in place.

The causes were mostly structural. First, long economic stagnation. Soviet growth had been sliding since the 1970s, and the country had become dependent on oil export revenue; when oil prices fell in the mid-1980s, that cushion disappeared, and the command economy could not correct itself. Second, the military burden. The Soviet elite themselves, as Gorbachev took over in 1985, largely agreed the economy was in serious trouble and that military spending was much to blame. The figure the Soviets finally published in May 1989 was 77.3 billion rubles, about 8.4 percent of GDP, and most analysts, including the CIA and Soviet officials, thought even that understated the real cost, because the USSR never kept honest military accounts. Third, ethnic nationalism inside the fifteen union republics, which had been suppressed rather than resolved. Fourth, and this is the irony, Gorbachev's own reforms. Glasnost and perestroika were meant to save the system by loosening censorship and injecting some market discipline, but freeing speech is what let grievances, memory of Stalin's crimes, and separatist movements organize openly. Wikipedia's own list of causes for the dissolution puts the Era of Stagnation, ethnic tensions, and glasnost and perestroika right at the top.

As for the three events, I would pick these.

The first is the wave of 1989 revolutions in Eastern Europe. Poland held partly free elections on June fourth, 1989, which Solidarity swept, Hungary opened its border, and on November ninth the Berlin Wall was thrown open after a bungled announcement by an East German Politburo official. Crucially, on October twenty-seventh the Warsaw Pact members formally renounced the Brezhnev Doctrine, meaning Moscow no longer claimed the right to intervene by force. That removed the outer empire without a shot fired, and it did something subtler too: it showed ordinary people and local politicians inside the Soviet Union itself that Moscow would not shoot, which emboldened them enormously. Estonia had already declared sovereignty in November 1988, and Lithuania declared independence in March 1990.

The second event is the failed coup of August nineteenth through twenty-first, 1991. Hardliners in the party, the military and the KGB formed the State Committee on the State of the Emergency, put Gorbachev under house arrest in Crimea, and tried to reverse his reforms just days before he was scheduled to sign a New Union Treaty that would have turned the USSR into a much looser confederation. The coup collapsed in three days, largely because the plotters could not command real support and because Yeltsin stood on tanks outside the Russian parliament. The result was a transfer of power that mattered more than the plot itself: Yeltsin ended up controlling the Russian economy and the security forces on Russian territory, with no constitutional basis for it, and Gorbachev was reduced to nearly nothing. As Shushkevich later put it, by December the union had already been broken up by the putschists. The coup also let the Baltic states finalize independence immediately.

The third event is the Belovezha Accords of December eighth, 1991. Yeltsin, Ukraine's Leonid Kravchuk and Belarus's Stanislav Shushkevich met secretly at a hunting lodge in the Belovezh forest near the Polish border and declared that the Soviet Union, as a subject of international law, was finished, replacing it with the Commonwealth of Independent States. That is why it is often called the death warrant: the three Slavic republics, the core of the union, simply walked away. Ukraine had already voted overwhelmingly for independence in a December first referendum, which is what made this possible. Gorbachev accepted the fait accompli, a few days later the remaining republics joined the CIS at Alma-Ata, and on December twenty-sixth the Supreme Soviet formally dissolved the Union.

So the clean way to hold it in your head: stagnation, the arms burden, nationalism and glasnost made the Soviet Union unable to hold itself together; the loss of Eastern Europe in 1989 removed the reason the center existed; the failed August coup destroyed the last functioning authority, Gorbachev's government, in three days; and Belovezha was the legal act that turned an already dissolved state into a formally dissolved one. One caveat worth naming: historians genuinely disagree about how much the arms race and Western pressure caused this versus how much was internal Soviet failure. There is a strong argument, made by scholars who worked on the CIA's estimates, that external pressure mattered less than the system's own inability to produce goods and keep its constituent nations willing to stay.
