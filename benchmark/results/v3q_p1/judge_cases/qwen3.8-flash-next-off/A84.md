A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

Even a 1% increase in the risk-free rate can sharply compress DCF valuations — particularly for growth portfolios where most value lies years in the future.

## The Cost of Money: Why Higher Rates Decimate Equity Valuations
The primary mechanism connecting central bank policy to public market prices lies in the Discount Rate. When the Federal Reserve raises its target rate, it sends a clear signal that the cost of money has increased.
This signal cascades directly to long-term Treasury yields, which analysts use as the risk-free rate (Rf)—the essential starting point for determining the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).
### The Mechanics of Valuation Compression: Duration Risk
The relationship between the discount rate and equity valuation is fundamentally inverse and, crucially, non-linear. The damage inflicted by rising rates is mathematically amplified by an asset's duration, which in equity terms, refers to how far into the future the majority of a company's cash flows are expected to materialize.
The present value (PV) formula dictates that a higher discount rate (r) reduces the value of a future cash flow (CFt):
PV=t=1∑N(1+r)tCFt
For high-growth companies (Tech, Biotech), the term "Terminal Value" (TV) can account for 70% to 80% of the total intrinsic valuation, placing the majority of the value 10 to 15 years in the future. A small increase in the WACC, which is the Discount Rate (r) in this context, imposes a much stronger penalty on these distant cash flows than on near-term cash flows. This effect is known as duration risk in equity analysis—and it is why rising rates disproportionately punish high-multiple, high-growth names.

ASSISTANT: Here's the core idea, and it's worth understanding as math rather than as sentiment.

A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future. The standard way to write that is: take each year's expected cash flow and divide it by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of how many years out that cash arrives. The discount rate starts with the risk-free rate — usually the ten-year Treasury yield — and adds a premium for the risk of owning a company instead of a bond. The Fed doesn't set the ten-year yield directly, but its policy rate is the anchor under it, so when the Fed hikes, the whole discount rate tends to move up with it.

Two things happen at once when rates rise. First, mechanically, a bigger denominator shrinks every future cash flow's present value. Second, the effect is not uniform across stocks — and that's the part that specifically explains tech.

The key concept is duration, borrowed from bond math. A bond that pays you everything in thirty years is far more rate-sensitive than one maturing next year, because the distant payment gets divided by a larger number compounded over many years. Stocks work the same way. A mature business like a utility or a cigarette company generates most of its value from cash flows arriving soon, so if you raise the discount rate by a point, those near-term cash flows barely change in present value. A high-growth technology company is the opposite: it may earn modestly today while analysts expect the bulk of its profits to arrive ten or fifteen years out. In fact, in most discounted cash flow models for growth names, terminal value — the value of everything beyond the explicit forecast window — accounts for something like seventy to eighty percent of the total intrinsic value. So the majority of what you're paying for sits at the far end of the compounding period, exactly where a higher discount rate does the most damage.

That's why the same one-point hike produces wildly different results across sectors. One analysis I read put it this way: hold the cash flow forecasts completely unchanged and move the discount rate from seven percent to eight percent, and a growth company's intrinsic value falls roughly ten percent, while a utility-type business sees something in the two-to-four percent range. Their typical estimate for technology and biotech is an eight to fifteen percent hit to intrinsic value per point of rate increase, versus two to four percent for utilities and energy. No earnings estimate changed in that exercise; only the cost of money did. That's the whole point — it's a re-rating, not a downgrade.
