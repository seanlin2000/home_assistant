Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The stock market reflects expected profits of large, often multinational companies, and stock ownership is concentrated (in the US the richest tenth of households own the large majority of stocks), while people feel the economy through wages, prices, rent and jobs. Three concrete examples from any of: prices rising faster than wages while companies keep or raise margins; a handful of giant tech firms driving index gains while most businesses and regions lag; companies earning much of their profit abroad; cost cutting and layoffs that raise profits and share prices; low rates or stimulus inflating asset prices, which helps people who own assets (the K-shaped recovery of 2020 and 2021); housing costs climbing while the index climbs. Must give three distinct, concrete mechanisms, not three restatements of "the market is not the economy".

# Exchange 1

USER: Why can a country have a strong stock market while its average citizens feel like the economy is getting worse? Give three concrete examples of how this happens.

ASSISTANT: That's a real and well-documented gap, often called the "vibecession." A stock index measures the value of companies, not the finances of households, so the two can move in opposite directions. Three concrete mechanisms:

**1. Ownership is concentrated, so gains don't reach most people.** In the United States, roughly half of stock holdings are held by the top ten percent of households by value, while a meaningful share of adults own no stock at all. If index gains are driven by a handful of mega-cap firms, a household with no equity and a wage that grew two percent feels nothing from a market up fifteen percent. The same pattern shows up in South Korea, where retail participation is high but concentrated in leveraged trading, and in much of Europe, where household wealth sits in housing and deposits rather than equities.

**2. Stock prices can rise because of buybacks, multiple expansion and cheap credit rather than rising living standards.** A company that borrows to repurchase shares lifts its price per share even while holding headcount flat or cutting costs through layoffs. Amazon's 2022–23 cost discipline is the standard example: a sharp drop in headcount alongside a violent rebound in the stock, with profits up but no wage or hiring benefit flowing outward. Buyback-heavy indices reward financial engineering more than paychecks.

**3. Market gains and household pain can share a common cause.** Two of the clearest recent cases:

- Artificial-intelligence capex. The S&P 500's recent returns have been disproportionately driven by a small number of AI-linked names, along with the chipmakers, data-center buildouts and the utilities supplying them. Those firms' earnings rise on capital spending, which needs relatively few workers, while many white-collar service sectors face weaker hiring and pay pressure. A strong index and a weak job market for recent graduates are not contradictory; they are two ends of the same investment boom.
- Tariffs and trade policy. US tariff hikes in 2025 lifted costs for importers, small retailers and manufacturers while selected protected sectors rallied, so an index can grind higher as household purchasing power erodes. Similar dynamics appear in Turkey, where high inflation and lira depreciation coexisted with a stock index that rose sharply in local terms because equities were a hedge against the currency, and in Argentina under its 2024–25 reform program, where the Merval soared in dollar terms while real wages and poverty moved the other way.

A fourth thing worth naming: indices are capitalization-weighted, so a broad decline across hundreds of average firms is invisible if a few giants rise. Add sticky housing costs, which consume a rising share of household income and don't appear in equity prices at all, and you get exactly the situation you described — record index levels alongside people who feel poorer every year.
