ASSISTANT: One correction before anything else: the Fed did not cut rates to zero last month. The federal funds target range is three and three quarters to four percent, where it has been since mid-September, and the FOMC held it there on October 28 with two members dissenting because they wanted a quarter-point *increase*, not a cut. So the premise behind your question isn't accurate, and that changes the advice quite a bit.

Mortgage rates have actually moved the other way recently. As of this week, the national average on a 30-year fixed is somewhere around seven to seven and a quarter percent depending on whose survey you read, with refinance offers averaging a touch higher than purchase loans. Rates ticked up after last week's CPI report.

So refinancing right now would only make sense if your existing rate is meaningfully above the roughly seven percent range — for example, if you locked in during 2023 or early 2024 at seven and a half to eight percent, or you're on an adjustable rate that's about to reset. If you bought in 2020 or 2021 at around three percent, refinancing today would almost certainly cost you money.

To give you a real answer rather than a general one, I'd need three numbers: your current rate, roughly how much is left on the loan, and your closing-cost estimate or whether you're rolling costs into the loan. Give me those and I'll calculate the new payment, the monthly savings, and how many months it would take to break even on the costs.
