Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short version is that sunlight looks white, but it's really a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and the air in our atmosphere sorts those colors out by scattering some of them around more than others.

Here's the mechanism. Light travels as a wave, and each color has a different wavelength — red light's waves are long and lazy, blue and violet light's waves are short and jittery. When sunlight hits the tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen in the air, those short waves get knocked off course much more easily than the long ones. This is called Rayleigh scattering, and it's why blue light gets bounced around in every direction instead of traveling straight through. So when you look up at any part of the sky that isn't the sun itself, you're seeing all that scattered blue light raining down from all directions at once. Violet actually scatters even more strongly than blue, but our eyes are far less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up in the atmosphere, so we perceive the sky as blue rather than purple.

Sunset is the same effect, just pushed to an extreme. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a much thicker slab of atmosphere to reach your eyes — dozens of times more air than when the sun is overhead. On that long journey, the blue and violet light gets scattered out of the beam almost completely, scattering off into the upper sky or back out to space. What's left arriving at your eyes is mostly the long-wavelength light that refused to be scattered: reds, oranges and yellows. That leftover beam, dimmed by all that distance, is what paints the sun and the clouds near it.

Two small details round it out. Clouds look dramatic at sunset because they're acting like a screen catching that remaining reddish light and spreading it across a big surface. And the reason dusk fades to a dull blue-gray rather than staying vivid is that once the sun drops below the horizon, the light you see is being scattered at very high altitude with no direct beam left to filter — which is also why the sky goes dark so gradually instead of switching off.

One caveat worth knowing: dust, smoke, pollution and volcanic haze make sunsets much more colorful than clean air does, because those bigger particles scatter all colors more evenly and add extra reddish glow. So a spectacular sunset usually means there's something in the air doing additional work beyond the molecules themselves.
