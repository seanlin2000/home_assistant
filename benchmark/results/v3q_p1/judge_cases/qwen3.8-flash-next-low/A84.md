Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock's value is never just next year's earnings — it's the present value of every dollar the business will earn from now until forever, and each of those future dollars gets divided by a discount rate that reflects what money costs. Raise that rate, and every future dollar is worth less today. Tech companies get hit harder than most because their value is unusually weighted toward the far future rather than the near present.

Think of it as two simple examples. Suppose you have a payment of one hundred dollars ten years out. At a three percent discount rate, that's worth about seventy-four dollars today. At five percent, it's worth about sixty-one — a 17.5 percent drop in value from a rate increase of just two percentage points. Now do the same thing with a payment one year out. At three percent you get about ninety-seven dollars; at five percent, about ninety-five. A two-point increase costs you only about 2.8 percent. Same company, same cash flows, same rate move — wildly different impact, purely because of when the cash arrives.

That timing effect is what bond investors call duration, and it's the whole reason technology stocks behave like ultra-long bonds. A utility or a packaged-food company earns most of its value in the next few years, so its cash flows are discounted only lightly. A high-growth software or biotech company often pays little or nothing today, and an analyst's model says the bulk of its value comes from earnings that dominate in year ten, fifteen or twenty — the terminal value. Those are the payments in the seventy-four-versus-sixty-one bucket, the most rate-sensitive line in the model. So when the discount rate moves, the stock's intrinsic value moves a lot, even if nobody changed a single forecast about revenue or margins.

A second channel amplifies it. The discount rate isn't set by the Fed directly; it's built out of the risk-free rate, usually the ten-year Treasury yield, plus an equity risk premium. When the Fed tightens, short rates rise, Treasury yields generally follow, and the safe, guaranteed alternative gets more attractive. A five percent yield on a Treasury is a five percent return with no risk, which raises the hurdle every risky asset must clear. Higher rates also usually strengthen the dollar, which hurts US multinationals' overseas earnings, and they raise the cost of the debt and cheap credit that growth-stage companies have historically relied on. Tighter financial conditions can also slow the ad spending, enterprise software budgets and venture funding that feed tech revenue — so the rate shock can dent the cash flows themselves, not just the discount rate. There's a knock-on valuation effect too: high-growth stocks are often priced on multiples of forward earnings, and when rates rise, investors are willing to pay a lower multiple for the same dollar of earnings, so a company can beat expectations and still see its stock fall.

Two important caveats, because the relationship is real but not a law. What matters to a valuation model is the real, expected path of rates, not the Fed's target in isolation — and a rate rise driven by strong demand and rising earnings forecasts can coincide with a tech rally, because the numerator rose faster than the denominator. Rates can also be high while a sector re-rates for reasons that have nothing to do with discounting, as happened during the AI capex boom, where investors were so confident in distant earnings that they discounted them less aggressively. And the 2022 episode is the clean counterexample to any claim that the Fed simply cuts tech: in that year of rapid hikes, the Nasdaq fell roughly a third while some of the most speculative, no-profit names fell far more, which is exactly the duration pattern you'd predict.

For context on where rates stand now: after raising by a quarter point in September 2026, the Federal Reserve held its target range at three and three-quarters to four percent at its October meeting, with two dissenters who wanted another increase. That's well below the 2023 peak but far above the near-zero era that made long-duration growth stocks so expensive, and analysts like the Conference Board read the October pause as a pause in tightening rather than the end of it.
