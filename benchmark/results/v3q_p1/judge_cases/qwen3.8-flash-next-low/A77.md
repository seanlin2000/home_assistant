Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to one idea: sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors, and the air is much better at bouncing the short, blue wavelengths around than the long, red ones.

Start with the sun. Light travels as a wave, and each color is a wave of a different size. Blue and violet light are short, choppy waves. Red and orange are long, lazy ones. Sunlight reaching the top of the atmosphere contains all of them, which is why it reads as white.

Now the air. It's full of nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and those molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. When a light wave runs into them, it makes the molecules flicker for an instant and re-emit the light, sending it off in a new direction. That's scattering. Here's the crucial part: the smaller the wave, the more violently it gets jostled. The effect goes up with the fourth power of the ratio of wavelengths, which means it explodes in strength as waves get shorter. Blue light, around 450 nanometers, bounces about four times more than deep red at 650 nanometers. Violet scatters even harder than blue.

So during the day, sunlight enters the air overhead and only has to pass through a fairly thin slice of atmosphere to reach you. As it travels, the blue gets knocked sideways over and over, and instead of coming straight at you from the sun, it arrives from every direction in the sky at once. Look anywhere away from the sun and you're seeing that blue light that's been flung around. The whole dome glows blue.

Two things keep the sky from looking violet, even though violet scatters more than blue. Some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are simply less sensitive to violet and mix the remaining blue and violet into a pale blue.

Sunset is the same physics with one change: geometry. When the sun is low, its light has to skim through a long, slanted path of air to reach your eyes — hundreds of times more air than when the sun is overhead. As light travels that long road, the blue and violet are stripped away almost completely, scattered out of the beam and off somewhere else. What survives the trip is the light that resists scattering: the long, stubborn red, orange and yellow waves. They come through relatively intact, and that's the color you see in the sun itself and in the clouds it's lighting up from behind.

There's one bonus effect at sunset. Dust, smoke and pollution particles in the lower air scatter light differently — big particles push all colors along more evenly, which deepens and warms the reds and gives you those dramatic banded sunsets. Cleaner air gives you a paler, softer sunset; air loaded with wildfire smoke or volcanic dust gives you the spectacular ones.

So the short version: the sky is blue because the air throws the short waves around, and sunsets are red because the long journey through the air removes the short waves and leaves only the long ones behind.
