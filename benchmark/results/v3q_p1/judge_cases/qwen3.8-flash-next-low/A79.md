Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
GPUs run thousands of simple cores in parallel with dedicated matrix units and very high memory bandwidth (HBM), and neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication; token-by-token generation is largely limited by memory bandwidth. NVIDIA's software stack (CUDA) is a large part of the advantage. An ASIC is a chip built for one kind of job (Google's TPU), more efficient but less flexible. Specialized inference processors go further for serving models, for example large on-chip SRAM instead of external memory (Groq), wafer-scale chips (Cerebras), or chips hard-wired for transformers (Etched). A specialized chip can win when the model architecture is stable and matches the chip, when the workload is high-volume and fixed, when latency per token matters most, or on cost or power per token; it loses when models change, when flexibility or training matters, or where software support is thin.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that inference is mostly a very specific kind of math — huge matrix multiplications and attention — and GPUs happen to be exceptionally good at exactly that kind of math, while also being programmable enough to handle whatever the model researchers invent next.

Break it into two halves, because modern large language model inference has two phases with completely different bottlenecks. The first is prefill, where all your prompt tokens are processed at once. That is compute-bound: lots of arithmetic per byte of weight read, which is where a GPU's thousands of parallel cores and its dedicated matrix-multiply units, the tensor cores, earn their keep. The second is decode, where the model generates one token at a time, and every step has to stream essentially the entire set of weights through the chip to do a handful of arithmetic on them. That phase is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, and its arithmetic intensity is close to one operation per byte. That single fact explains almost everything about why some specialized chips can beat a GPU and why others can't.

GPUs win the war overall for a few reasons beyond raw silicon. They have high-bandwidth memory stacked right next to the die, in the multi-terabyte-per-second range, which is the best you can do with off-chip memory. They can batch, and batching is the trick that turns a memory-bound problem into a more compute-bound one: serve a hundred users at once and you amortize each weight read across many requests, which is why GPU systems can sustain enormous aggregate throughput even when a single user sees a modest token rate. And critically, they are programmable general-purpose processors, so when an architecture changes — mixture of experts, a new attention variant, a new quantization format — the same hardware keeps working through a software update. CUDA, cuDNN, TensorRT, and the serving stacks around them are a decade of accumulated engineering that nobody has matched.

ASICs and specialized inference processors trade that flexibility for efficiency in different ways. A TPU or a Trainium uses systolic arrays, which are grids of multiply-accumulate cells where data flows through the array and weights stay parked in place. That removes a lot of the instruction fetching, cache hierarchy, and scheduling overhead a GPU pays, so you get more useful math per watt and per die area, but only for regular dense shapes, and only for the frameworks the chip is built to serve. A Groq LPU goes further: it keeps weights in on-chip SRAM and schedules everything statically at compile time, so there is no dynamic scheduler and latency is nearly deterministic. Sources I checked put its SRAM bandwidth at around one hundred fifty terabytes per second per chip, which is why it holds sub-ten-millisecond latency well. A Cerebras wafer-scale engine makes the same bet at absurd scale, a whole wafer as one chip with about forty-four gigabytes of on-chip SRAM and bandwidth they describe as petabytes per second, roughly thousands of times an H100's memory bandwidth, so the model rarely has to leave the silicon. Then there is the extreme end, like Etched's Sohu, which is built only for the transformer operation sequence, or Taalas, which hardwires a specific model into the transistor layout. That is the maximum possible efficiency and the minimum possible flexibility: the model is the hardware.

So when does a specialized chip actually beat a GPU? The circumstances are quite specific. You win when the model is known and stable — a handful of architectures, ideally fixed at silicon design time. You win when you're serving interactive, low-latency, small-batch requests, where per-user token speed and tail latency matter more than cost per million tokens. You win when the model fits comfortably in on-chip memory, which mostly means small to mid-sized dense models, or a very large system willing to spread a big model across many chips. You win when your traffic is steady and predictable enough to amortize the silicon and the compiler effort. In those conditions the margins are dramatic: reported per-user figures as of this year put Cerebras around one thousand eight hundred to two thousand one hundred tokens per second on an eight-billion-parameter dense model, Groq in the two seventy to two eighty range on a seventy-billion model, versus roughly two thirty on an NVIDIA H200 and around three fifty on a B200.

But the caveats matter as much as the numbers. Most real inference spend is batch or near-batch work — classification overnight, embeddings, offline generation — where dollars per token dominate and commodity GPU capacity wins outright, which is why specialized providers still hold a small share of total volume. Large models with hundreds of billions of parameters or mixture-of-experts routing favor GPUs' huge pools of HBM. Anything experimental, anything you also need to fine-tune, stays on GPUs. And the headline claims need skepticism: the often-quoted figure of an Etched server doing five hundred thousand tokens per second against a twenty-times-slower eight-GPU H100 system comes from the company's own simulations, with no third-party benchmark behind it as far as I could find, and Tenstorrent has reportedly been getting around half its theoretical peak on real LLM work. The field also shifted recently: NVIDIA acquired Groq in December of 2025 and is now shipping LPU-class parts alongside its own GPUs, and the emerging pattern for 2027 is hybrid pipelines that run prefill on a GPU or Trainium and decode on a specialized chip. That's really the honest conclusion — the fastest answer isn't a chip, it's splitting the two halves of inference onto the silicon each one fits.
