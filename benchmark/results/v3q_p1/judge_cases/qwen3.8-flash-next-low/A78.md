ASSISTANT: The short answer is that a stock index prices a company's future profits, while a household's wellbeing is priced in rent, groceries, gas and a paycheck. Those two things only move together if the profits actually reach wages and if the households that own the stocks are the same ones feeling squeezed. Right now in the US they mostly are not, and 2026 is a striking illustration: the S&P 500 has been hitting records while the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index has been sitting at its lowest levels in the survey's 74 year history, including an all time low of 44.8 in May and 46.3 in the preliminary October reading. Three concrete mechanisms explain that gap.

First, the stocks that rose are not evenly owned. The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, released this October covering 2025 data, found that the share of families with any direct or indirect stock holdings actually fell, from 58 percent in 2022 to 56 percent in 2025. But among the families who did hold stock, the median holding jumped 36 percent, from about 56,900 dollars to about 77,400. So the market made a lot of money for people who already had exposure, and the share of households with that exposure shrank. Fortune reports the top tenth of a percent of households together hold five times as much wealth as the bottom half. A market rally is, mechanically, a transfer of paper wealth to the people least likely to be counting the grocery bill.

Second, the rally has been extremely narrow, so an index record says very little about the average business. Through the end of September, the S&P 500 was up roughly 11.8 percent and the Nasdaq about 15.6 percent, driven by another big advance in the handful of large technology companies, while the Dow, which is weighted toward more ordinary businesses, gained only about 5.9 percent. Commentary through the year has kept describing the same split, corporate performance propelled by a narrow band of giants while Main Street deals with inflation and stagnant pay. If four or five companies are supplying most of the index's rise, the index is not a measure of anything most firms or most workers experienced.

Third, and this is the one people feel most directly, stock gains are being generated by the same forces that are suppressing household income. The AI investment boom adds to gross domestic product and to corporate margins at the same time that it thins out hiring. J.P. Morgan projects around 900 billion dollars of AI capital spending globally in 2026, and the IMF estimated that AI related technology investment added about half a percentage point to US growth in 2025. Meanwhile Stanford's Digital Economy Lab dashboards found employment in AI exposed occupations such as software development and customer service had fallen more than 4 percent this year, with entry level hiring squeezed in particular. A company that trims junior headcount and lifts margins often sees exactly what the stock market rewards, higher earnings per share, while the households that would have filled those jobs lose income. RBC's election year analysis put the numbers together: consumer prices rose about 5.4 percent since January 2025 while average hourly earnings rose about 5.5 percent, so pay has effectively just treaded water, and Fortune notes real wages have recently turned negative. In the third quarter, spending accelerated to 3.8 percent even as real disposable income grew only 1.3 percent, which means people are spending faster than they are actually getting ahead.

One more thing worth naming, because it explains why people feel worse now than they did during the worst of the inflation scare. Consumer prices in September were rising at 3.7 percent a year, far below the 9.1 percent peak of June 2022, yet sentiment is lower today than it was then. That is because surveys and households both respond to the price level, not just the rate of change. Gas and rent that rose 20 percent have not come back down just because the annual increase slowed. Add borrowing costs, with the 10 year Treasury yield above 5 percent for the first time in more than two decades, and you get the exact combination people describe: the market is at records, mortgage and credit payments are expensive, and pay is not catching up. In polls this fall, 84 percent of respondents called cost of living one of their most important issues, and of those 74 percent said it was getting worse.
