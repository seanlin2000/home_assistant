So here's the idea. A large language model like a 70-billion-parameter one stores all of its knowledge in those parameters, which are really just numbers — weights that get multiplied together during inference. By default these are stored at full precision, typically either 16-bit or 32-bit floating point. Quantization means you deliberately reduce the number of bits used to represent each weight, trading some numerical precision for a big drop in memory and faster computation.

The math is simple: 70 billion parameters times the number of bytes per parameter. At full 16-bit precision that's about 140 gigabytes just for the weights. Quantized down to 4 bits, you're using half a byte per weight, so roughly 35 gigabytes. At 2 bits it drops to about 17.5 gigabytes. To put that in perspective: a 70-billion model at full precision won't fit on most consumer hardware at all — you'd need something like an A100 or multiple GPUs. At 4-bit it fits comfortably on a single high-end GPU or even a powerful workstation, which is why tools like GGUF and bitsandbytes make 4-bit quantized versions of models like Llama so popular.

Now the trade-offs. The big one is quality degradation. Every time you round a weight to fewer bits, you introduce error, and those errors accumulate across billions of parameters and thousands of layers. In practice, going from 16-bit down to 8-bit usually costs almost nothing — maybe a fraction of a point on benchmark scores. Going to 4-bit is where it gets interesting: well-implemented 4-bit quantization with techniques like group-wise scaling (where you keep a small floating-point scale factor for every few weights) typically preserves most of the model's ability, and for many tasks the quality loss is barely noticeable. But on hard reasoning tasks, code generation, or niche knowledge, you will start to see it.

Going to 2-bit is where things get really rough. At that point you're losing a lot of information per weight, and standard quantization methods struggle. You need much more sophisticated techniques — sometimes mixed precision where some layers stay at higher bits, or calibration on a representative dataset — and even then the quality drop can be significant. 2-bit is mostly a research frontier right now; it's impressive to see people do it, but you'll notice the model gets dumber, more repetitive, and worse at following instructions.
