Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: This is a great finance question that I can answer from established economic theory without needing to search for current data, since you're asking about the general mechanism rather than today's specific rate level.

The core idea is that a stock's "fair" value is, in most valuation models, the present value of all the cash it will generate over its life. To turn future cash into today's dollars, you discount those cash flows by some interest rate — essentially the return an investor could get elsewhere for bearing the same risk. That discount rate moves with the broader level of interest rates, particularly short-term rates that the Fed sets.

Here's where tech stocks specifically feel it. Two things are going on:

First, the discount math itself. If you raise the discount rate, every future dollar is worth less today. But here's the kicker: the further out in time a cash flow sits, the more brutal the effect. A dollar in five years gets hit moderately; a dollar in twenty-five years gets crushed. Growth and tech companies — especially high-multiple ones — derive most of their valuation from cash flows far in the future, often years before they're even profitable today. Their earnings are "long-duration" assets, meaning their value is concentrated at the long end of the time horizon. So when rates rise, the present value of those distant cash flows shrinks dramatically, and the stock's fair multiple compresses. A company that looked worth 30x earnings at 2% interest might only look like 15x at 5%, even if its business hasn't changed at all.

Second, the "opportunity cost" or asset-allocation effect. When rates are near zero, a safe Treasury bond pays you almost nothing, so investors chase equities — especially high-growth tech — to get any meaningful return. That inflates valuations well beyond what fundamentals would support on their own. When the Fed raises rates, bonds start offering 4%, 5% without any risk. Some of that speculative money rotates out of risky growth stocks and into bonds or cash. The demand for high-multiple tech shares falls, pushing prices down.

There's also a third, more indirect channel: higher rates make borrowing more expensive for companies (including tech firms funding expansion) and for consumers (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards), which can slow the broader economy and dampen future revenue growth — the very thing these stocks are priced on.

So to tie it together: rate hikes raise the discount rate, which disproportionately crushes the present value of distant cash flows that make up most of a long-duration growth stock's worth; they also make safer assets more attractive, pulling speculative demand away from tech; and they tighten the macro environment those companies' future revenues depend on. That's why you see tech-heavy indices like the Nasdaq tend to fall when the Fed is in a hiking cycle, and often rebound sharply when rates come down or pause.
