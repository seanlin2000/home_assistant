Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will earn in the future. To get that value, analysts discount each future dollar back to today using a discount rate that is built on interest rates. When the Federal Reserve raises rates, the discount rate goes up, so every future dollar is worth less today. The effect is much stronger for growth stocks, like many tech companies, because most of their cash flow is expected far in the future. That makes them long-duration assets, similar to a long-term bond. A company earning little now but a lot in ten years loses more value from a higher discount rate than a mature company paying steady profits today. Higher rates also hurt in other ways. Borrowing costs rise for companies that depend on cheap financing, safer assets like Treasuries and savings accounts become more attractive compared with risky stocks, and a slowing economy can lower growth expectations. That is why tech shares often drop when rate hikes are expected. The relationship is a tendency, not a law, because earnings growth can outweigh the discount rate effect.
