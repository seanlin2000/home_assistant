Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The sky is blue because sunlight is a mix of every color, and the tiny molecules of air scatter blue light much more than red. Picture sunlight as a stream of balls rolling through a field of thin posts: the small blue ones keep bouncing off in every direction, while the red ones mostly roll straight through. So wherever you look in the sky, you see blue light that's been bounced toward you from all over. Violet scatters even more, but the sun sends less of it and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so the sky looks blue. At sunset the sun is low, so its light crosses far more air to reach you than at noon. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered off to the sides, and what still arrives straight at you is mostly red and orange. Dust, smoke and moisture near the horizon can deepen those colors, which is why some sunsets look fiery. Scientists call this Rayleigh scattering.
