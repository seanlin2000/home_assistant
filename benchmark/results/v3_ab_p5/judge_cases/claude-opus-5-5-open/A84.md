Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth, in theory, all the cash the company will ever produce, with each future dollar discounted back to today. That discount rate starts from the risk-free interest rate, roughly what Treasuries pay, plus a premium for risk. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate rises, so every future dollar is worth less today. And because the discount compounds year after year, the further away a dollar is, the bigger the hit. Growth tech companies are long-duration assets, a lot like long-term bonds: most of their expected profits sit many years out, while a utility or a bank earns more of its value sooner. So the same rate rise cuts a growth stock's present value by a much larger share. Higher rates also pile on in other ways. Safe bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing gets costlier for unprofitable firms that need fresh funding, and a slower economy can trim the growth investors were counting on. That's why high-valuation tech stocks tend to fall hardest when rates climb, as they did in twenty twenty-two.
