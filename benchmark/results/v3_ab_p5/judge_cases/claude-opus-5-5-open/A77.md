Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it's really a mix of every color, and each color travels as a wave of a different size: blue waves are short and choppy, red waves are long and lazy. When sunlight passes through the air, the tiny gas molecules bounce the short blue waves around far more than the long red ones, so blue light gets scattered all across the sky. Wherever you look during the day, scattered blue light is coming at you from that direction, so the whole sky looks blue. Violet scatters even more, but sunlight has less of it and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so we see blue. At sunset the sun is low, so its light has to travel sideways through much more air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered off in other directions before it arrives, leaving the oranges and reds to come straight through. Dust, smoke or haze adds extra scattering, which is why sunsets can look especially fiery on hazy evenings or after wildfires. Scientists call this Rayleigh scattering.
