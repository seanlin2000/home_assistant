Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white but is really a mix of all colors. When it hits the air, tiny gas molecules bounce it around, and they bounce blue light far more than red. So during the day, blue light gets scattered all over the sky, and that scattered blue is what your eyes see when you look up. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light has to travel through much more air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away to the sides, so by the time the light reaches your eyes, mostly the reds and oranges are left. Think of it like a crowd walking through a long hallway where the blue walkers keep getting pushed out the doors, and only the red walkers make it to the end. Dust and haze can make sunsets even more vivid, because they add more scattering along that path.
