Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of all the colors. When that light enters the atmosphere, it bumps into air molecules, and those molecules scatter blue light far more than red light. Blue gets bounced around in every direction, so when you look at any part of the sky, you see blue light arriving from all over. That is why the daytime sky looks blue. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light has to travel through much more air to reach your eyes. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away from your line of sight, off to other people's skies. What is left is the light that scatters least, which is the reds and oranges. So the sunset is red for the same reason the sky is blue, just seen through a much thicker slice of atmosphere. Dust and haze can make the colors even stronger.
