Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future. To get that value, analysts discount each future dollar back to today using a rate tied to interest rates. When the Fed raises rates, the discount rate goes up, and every future dollar is worth less today. The effect is not equal across companies. A mature company that earns most of its cash in the next few years is only mildly affected. A growth company, especially a tech firm whose big profits are expected many years away, has most of its value in the distant future, so a higher discount rate cuts its value much more. That is what long duration means, the same idea as long-term bonds falling more than short-term bonds when rates rise. Higher rates also make safe bonds more attractive, raise borrowing costs for companies that rely on funding, and can slow the economy, all of which pushes growth stock prices down.
