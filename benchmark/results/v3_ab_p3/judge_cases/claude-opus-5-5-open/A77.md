Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: It comes down to how air scatters sunlight. Sunlight looks white, but it's a mix of all colors, and each color travels as a wave of a different length: blue waves are short, red waves are long. The tiny gas molecules in air bounce short blue waves around far more than long red ones, roughly five times more for blue than red. So during the day, when you look anywhere in the sky away from the sun, you're seeing blue light that's been bounced toward you from all directions. That's why the whole sky glows blue. It isn't violet, even though violet scatters more, because sunlight has less violet and our eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset, the sun is low, so its light travels sideways through much more air to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives, and what's left is the oranges and reds. Dust, smoke and haze make that effect even stronger, which is why sunsets after wildfires can be so vivid.
