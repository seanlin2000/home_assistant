Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: It comes down to how investors value future money. A stock is worth the cash the company will produce in the future, translated back into today's dollars. That translation is the discounting step: each future dollar is shrunk by a discount rate, and that rate is built on top of the risk-free interest rate. When the Fed raises rates, Treasury yields rise, so the discount rate rises, and every future dollar is worth less today. Compounding makes this much harsher for dollars far in the future than for dollars next year. That's why growth tech stocks are called long-duration assets: much of their value comes from profits expected ten or twenty years out, so a higher rate shrinks their present value far more than it does for a utility or bank that earns most of its cash now. Higher rates also hurt in practical ways: borrowing to fund growth gets more expensive, safe bonds become a real alternative to risky stocks, and customers may slow spending. The reverse holds too, which is why tech stocks often rally when rate cuts are expected.
