Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future. To get that value, you take each future cash flow and discount it back to today using an interest rate, which reflects what you could earn elsewhere with little risk. When the Federal Reserve raises rates, the risk-free rate, like the Treasury yield, rises, so the discount rate rises, and every future dollar is worth less today. Technology and growth companies are hit hardest because they are long-duration assets. Much of their value comes from profits expected many years from now, while near-term earnings are small. A higher discount rate shrinks distant cash flows much more than near ones, because the discounting compounds over more years. A company paying big profits today is less affected. There are secondary effects too. Higher rates make bonds a more attractive alternative to stocks, so investors demand a larger return from stocks. Borrowing costs go up, which hurts firms that rely on cheap financing. And a slowing economy can reduce growth expectations. All of that pushes down the price people will pay for future growth.
