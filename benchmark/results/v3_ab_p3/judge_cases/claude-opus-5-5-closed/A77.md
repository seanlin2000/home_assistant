Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: It comes down to how sunlight bounces off the air itself. Sunlight looks white, but it's a mix of every color. The tiny nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air scatter short wavelengths, the blues and violets, much more strongly than long ones like red. Physicists call this Rayleigh scattering. So during the day, wherever you look away from the sun, you're seeing blue light that has been bounced around the sky and redirected toward your eyes. It isn't violet because sunlight has less violet to begin with and our eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the sun is low, so its light travels sideways through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. Along that long path, most of the blue gets knocked off in other directions, and what survives is red, orange and yellow. Dust, smoke and moisture can strengthen the effect, which is why some sunsets are so vivid. Picture a long, crowded hallway where blue keeps getting bumped aside while red walks straight through.
