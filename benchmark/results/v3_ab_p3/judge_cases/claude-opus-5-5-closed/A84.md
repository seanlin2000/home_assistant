Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: Because a stock's value is, in theory, all the cash it will ever produce, with each future dollar discounted back to today, and that discount rate rises when interest rates rise. When the Fed raises rates, safe assets like Treasury bonds pay more, so investors demand a higher return from risky stocks too. A higher discount rate shrinks the present value of every future dollar, but it hits distant dollars much harder, because the discount compounds every year. A dollar ten years away is worth about seventy-four cents today at a three percent rate, but only about fifty-six cents at six percent. Mature companies like utilities earn most of their cash soon, so they're less affected. Growth tech companies are the opposite: much of their expected value comes from profits a decade or more out, which is why they're called long-duration assets, like a long-term bond whose price swings most when rates move. Higher rates also make it costlier for unprofitable companies to raise money, and they slow the economy, which can shrink those future profits themselves.
