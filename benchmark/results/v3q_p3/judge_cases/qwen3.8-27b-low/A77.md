Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: It all comes down to one thing: sunlight is not actually white. It's a mix of all the colors of the rainbow, and each color behaves a little differently as it travels through the air around us.

Here's the key idea. Blue light travels in short, quick little ripples. Red light travels in long, lazy waves. The air in our atmosphere is full of tiny molecules, and when light bumps into them, the molecules scatter the light in all directions. But here's the crucial part: those molecules scatter the short, quick blue ripples much more effectively than the long, slow red ones. Think of it a bit like how a small pebble sends a short, choppy wave bouncing around a pond, but a long, slow ocean wave just rolls right over it almost unchanged.

So during the day, when the sun is high overhead, its light only has to punch through a relatively thin slice of atmosphere. That thin slice scatters a huge amount of blue light in every direction. When you look up, you're seeing all that scattered blue coming at you from every part of the sky. The blue doesn't just come from the sun; it comes from everywhere around you.

Now at sunset, the geometry changes. The sun is sitting right on the horizon, so its light has to travel through a much thicker slab of atmosphere to reach your eyes — maybe ten or twenty times thicker than when the sun is overhead. As that light squeezes through all that air, the blue and green get scattered away over and over again, in every direction, long before the light reaches you. By the time the remaining light finally arrives, it's been stripped of its blue and green, and what's left is mostly red, orange, and yellow. That's the color you see streaming in from the horizon.

So it's really the same physics both times — the air scatters blue more than red — it's just the distance the light has to travel that changes what you end up seeing. The shorter the path, the more blue survives. The longer the path, the more blue gets scattered out of the way, and the warmer colors win.
