Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
The Hue Bridge is the local hub: it talks to the bulbs over Zigbee and keeps scenes and automations. Share control by inviting the other people in the Hue app or letting them use it on the home network, add physical Hue switches for anyone without a phone, and connect a voice assistant (Apple Home through the bridge, Alexa, Google Home, or a local controller such as Home Assistant). If the internet goes down: the bridge, its automations, the Hue app on the local network and physical switches keep working; Apple Home with a home hub keeps working locally; cloud voice assistants such as Alexa and Google stop taking commands, and control from outside the home stops. A local voice setup avoids that dependency.

ASSISTANT: Here's how I'd set it up, and it's pretty clean because Hue was built around a local hub.

The core is your Hue Bridge. Every bulb talks to it over Zigbee, and the bridge sits on your home network over Ethernet. That's the part that stays local and alive regardless of what happens to the internet. For multiple people, don't share one login. Create a single Hue account, then in the app go to Settings and Home access and invite each person in the apartment. Everyone accepts on their own phone, gets their own account, and can control the same rooms and scenes. You can also give people different permission levels if you want, but for basic on, off, color, and brightness everyone just works. The one caveat is that the Hue app only does full-featured control on the same local network — the "away from home" remote control is what needs the internet.

For voice, pick one assistant ecosystem and link the bridge to it rather than juggling several. The important development here is recent: as of mid-October 2026, Google Home started automatically moving existing Hue bridges to local Matter control through your Google hubs, with no re-pairing needed. That means voice commands go from your speaker straight to the bridge over your home network instead of bouncing through the cloud. Apple Home works the same way in principle because HomeKit is local by design. Alexa also does local control, but it's more hit or miss depending on the device and command. My honest read is that Google and Apple give you the most reliable local voice story right now.

Now, what happens when the internet goes down. The good news is a lot keeps working. The Hue app still controls the lights locally, because the bridge and the Zigbee mesh don't need the internet — Philips explicitly says you can control lights even when the internet is down. Physical Hue dimmers and tap switches keep working too, over Zigbee alone. And voice should keep working as long as you're on a platform with local control like the new Google Home local Matter setup or Apple Home, with your speaker and the bridge on the same network.

The things that do stop: anything that needs to reach out. You can't control the lights from outside the apartment, and any routine or integration that depends on the cloud — like Sync tied to Spotify, or a smart-home routine that pulls from the web — will hang or fail until the connection comes back.

One thing to be straight with you about: Google has rolled this out as a background upgrade and describes it as faster and more reliable, but it hasn't fully guaranteed that every voice command works in a total outage, so I'd actually test it once — drop the Wi-Fi on your router, say "turn off the lights," and see. If Google turns out to be unreliable offline for you, Apple Home is the more established fallback for local voice. But the underlying lights themselves will never go dark on an internet outage.
