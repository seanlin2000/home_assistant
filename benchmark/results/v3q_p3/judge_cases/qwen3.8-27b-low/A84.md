Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The cleanest way to think about it is through the discounted cash flow model, which is how most investors roughly value a company. A stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will ever hand back to shareholders, and to get that present value you divide each future dollar by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of how many years away it is. That discount rate is anchored by the risk-free rate — essentially short-term interest rates — plus a premium for the company's risk.

When the Fed raises rates, two things happen at once. First, the risk-free rate goes up, so the whole discount rate moves up. Second, and this is the key, the effect is not even across time. A dollar next year is worth about ninety-five cents at a five percent discount rate and about ninety-three cents at seven percent — barely a dent. But a dollar thirty years from now is worth about twenty-three cents at five percent and only about thirteen cents at seven percent. That's a drop of roughly forty-three percent in present value for a single two-point rate increase, and the further out you go, the more brutal it gets.

Now connect that to what a tech growth stock actually is. A lot of the market value in companies like high-growth software or AI plays comes from cash flows that are projected years or decades into the future, not from what they earn this year. Financial people call that long duration: the "maturity" of the cash flows is far out. So when the discount rate climbs, the part of the stock price that depends on distant future cash flows gets marked down hard. A mature utility or a bank that earns most of its cash today is much less exposed to this, because its cash flows are near-term and barely move with the rate.

There are a few secondary channels worth mentioning. Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, which makes a company's own growth investments look less attractive, and that can slow the very growth the market was pricing in. And on the asset-allocation side, when bonds start paying more, the risk-free alternative becomes more competitive, so investors are willing to accept a lower multiple on risky growth stocks because the spread in favor of them has narrowed.

One honest caveat: this is the textbook channel and it explains why tech tends to fall in hiking cycles, especially when the hike is in response to a growth surge that raises rates faster than expected. But the real-world reaction always depends on *why* the Fed is hiking and how much was already priced in. A hike that is fully anticipated and seen as a sign of a strong economy can coexist with rising tech valuations, because the numerator — the expected future cash flows — is also rising. The rate channel is the discount-rate side of the equation, and it works in the opposite direction from the earnings side.
