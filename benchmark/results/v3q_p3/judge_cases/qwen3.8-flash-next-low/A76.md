On 26 December 1991, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved and ceased to exist as a sovereign state. It also brought an end to the Soviet Union's federal government and to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's efforts to reform the Soviet political and economic system in an attempt to stop a period of political and economic stagnation.
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 resulted from several factors: chronic economic stagnation, the unsustainable financial burden of the arms race with the United States and foreign conflicts, intense ethnic nationalism and separatism within its union republics, and the destabilizing effects of Gorbachev's reforms (particularly glasnost and perestroika).
The process began with growing unrest in the country's union republics developing into an incessant political and legislative conflict between them and the central government. Until its final years, the Soviet Union was made up of 15 union republics that served as the homelands of different ethnic groups. Estonia was the first union republic to declare state sovereignty on 16 November 1988. Lithuania was the first republic to declare independence, in March 1990.

The Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic, the second-most populous of the fifteen comprising the USSR, played a major – arguably, a decisive – role in the Soviet Union’s dissolution at the end of 1991. Several incidents had weakened the Ukrainian Communist Party’s authority in previous years. The explosion of the Chernobyl nuclear reactor in April 1986 had destroyed any sense of personal safety and dealt a serious blow to the republic’s economy. ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended in a way almost nobody predicted, including the men inside it. It didn't end with a war or a treaty between the superpowers; it ended when the Soviet Union simply ceased to exist, which removed one of the two poles of the whole system.

First, it helps to separate the causes from the triggers, because they are different kinds of things. The causes were structural and slow: decades of economic stagnation after the 1970s, an arms race with the United States and defense spending that crowded out everything else, the cost of propping up satellite states and the war in Afghanistan, and long-suppressed ethnic nationalism inside the fifteen union republics. On top of that sat Mikhail Gorbachev's own reforms. Glasnost, openness, and perestroika, restructuring, were meant to save the system. Instead, glasnost made it possible to openly say the system was failing, and perestroika loosened the central controls without replacing them with anything that worked. Gorbachev also refused, unlike Brezhnev, to use force to hold the empire together. Those conditions made collapse possible over a period of years. The triggers were specific events, months and days, that made collapse sudden and irreversible.

Three events stand out.

The first is the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe, symbolized by the fall of the Berlin Wall in November that year. Within a few months Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and Romania threw out their communist governments, and Germany unified. That was the outer empire dissolving, and it mattered for two reasons. It proved that Moscow would no longer intervene militarily, which settled the question that had underwritten Soviet power since 1956 and 1968. And it demonstrated inside the Soviet Union itself that these systems could be removed quickly and peacefully, which emboldened the republican nationalist movements. Estonia had already declared sovereignty in November 1988, and Lithuania declared independence in March 1990, so the sequence was already underway.

The second is the failed coup of August 19 to 21, 1991. Hardliners in the Communist Party, the KGB, and the military put Gorbachev under house arrest in Crimea to stop him from signing a new union treaty that would have transformed the USSR into a looser confederation of sovereign states. The coup collapsed in three days, largely because it was badly organized and because Boris Yeltsin, as Russia's president, stood on a tank in Moscow and mobilized public resistance, with parts of the army refusing to fire. This is the pivot point of the whole story. The coup's failure destroyed what remained of the central government's willingness and ability to coerce anyone, discredited the party and the security apparatus, and shifted real power from the Soviet presidency to Yeltsin and the republican leaders. Ukraine declared independence on August 24, days after the coup fell apart, and by the end of September eight more republics had followed. What is often described as a last-ditch effort to preserve the union was actually what ended any realistic chance of preserving it.

The third is the Ukrainian independence referendum of December 1, 1991, which is the single event most observers point to as decisive. Turnout was about 84 percent of eligible voters, and roughly 90 to 92 percent voted for independence, with majorities in every region, including the industrial east and Crimea, though with much thinner margins there. Ukraine was second only to Russia in population, economic weight, and military presence, and the armed forces stationed there were soon being placed under Ukrainian command. Almost everyone agreed at the time that no serious federal arrangement could exist without Ukraine in it. So the union had lost the one republic whose participation mattered as much as Russia's.

Then the triggers came fast. On December 8, 1991, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, Yeltsin, Leonid Kravchuk, and Stanislav Shushkevich, met secretly at a hunting lodge in Belarus and signed the Belovezh Accords, declaring that the Soviet Union had ceased to exist and creating the Commonwealth of Independent States instead. The sources are striking here: the three apparently arrived without a common plan, and by most accounts the decision to dissolve the country was made in the room. On December 21, eleven of the twelve remaining republics, all but Georgia, signed the Alma-Ata Protocol, which confirmed the dissolution and made Gorbachev's position untenable in practice, since the majority of republics needed for the federation to continue had agreed it was over. Gorbachev resigned on December 25, handing over control of the Kremlin and the nuclear codes to Yeltsin, the Soviet flag came down for the last time, and on December 26 the upper chamber of the Supreme Soviet formally voted the Soviet Union out of existence.

So the short version is this. Economic exhaustion, the arms race, nationalist resentment, and the unintended consequences of Gorbachev's reforms made the Soviet Union incapable of governing itself. The failure of the Eastern European empire in 1989 showed that Moscow would not fight to hold things together. The failed coup in August 1991 broke the center's power. And Ukraine's vote on December 1, 1991 made the breakup impossible to reverse. The Belovezh Accords were not the cause of anything; they were the paperwork acknowledging facts that had already been established.

One caveat worth stating: historians genuinely disagree about how much of this was inevitable. Some argue the union might have survived some looser form, and that Gorbachev's hesitations, not just the structural problems, decided the outcome. And the end of the Cold War and the end of the Soviet Union are related but not identical events; the Cold War is usually dated as ending in 1989 or 1990, with the Soviet collapse in December 1991 closing it out.
