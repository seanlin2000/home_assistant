ASSISTANT: The short answer is that a stock index is not a measure of how well households are doing. It's a weighted price of a few hundred large corporations, and it responds to earnings, interest rates, and what asset owners will pay for those shares. Household wellbeing responds to wages, prices, job security, and the ability to buy a home. Those two things genuinely come apart, and 2026 is a good illustration: the S&P 500 logged dozens of all-time highs this year and rose nearly 10 percent in the first half, while the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index hit a record low of about 45 in May and was still under 48 in September. Here are three concrete ways that gap opens up.

First, stock gains are concentrated in the hands of people who already own a lot of stocks, and they are concentrated in a handful of companies. Roughly 37 percent of the S&P 500's market value is technology, and the index has been carried by a few mega-cap names pouring money into AI infrastructure. Microsoft, Meta, Amazon and Alphabet alone spent about 303 billion dollars on data centers in the first half of 2026, and analysts expect that spending to account for around half of this year's earnings growth. Those companies mostly sell to other businesses and investors, not to your grocery budget, so their share prices can surge while ordinary spending feels squeezed. On the household side, Federal Reserve distributional accounts data shows the top 1 percent now holds about 32 percent of U.S. household wealth, while the bottom 50 percent holds about 2.5 percent, roughly the same share they had a decade ago, and their net worth is essentially flat once you adjust for inflation. Since 2019 the S&P 500 is up something like 113 percent. A household with almost no stocks gets none of that. That is the whole story of the "wealth effect," where the rich feel richer and spend more, and that spending itself props up the very companies driving the index.

Second, inflation pushes nominal asset prices up while it eats the purchasing power of a paycheck, so the same dollars mean different things on each side of the ledger. Prices overall are up roughly 25 percent since January 2020, more than double what the previous five years saw, and food-at-home prices were about 32 percent higher in October 2026 than in early 2020. Wages have risen too, around 3.3 percent in 2025, which sounds fine as a headline but does not catch up to what has already been lost on rent, groceries, insurance and health care. A stock index has no deflator on it. Higher price levels, tax cuts, fiscal spending and expectations of future rate cuts can all lift share prices, and none of that shows up as a lower grocery bill. So a person can look at the news, see records being set, then look at their own budget and see a smaller surplus at the end of the month. Sentiment follows the budget, not the index.

Third, and probably the least obvious one, is that a lot of the earnings growth behind today's prices comes from financial engineering and from companies hiring fewer people rather than more. S&P 500 companies repurchased about 1.1 trillion dollars of their own stock in the twelve months ending in June 2026, an all-time high, and buyback analysts estimate that repurchases add somewhere between 2 and 4 percent to reported earnings growth per year. Buying back shares raises earnings per share, which raises the stock price, without producing a single new product or wage. Meanwhile the actual job market feels awful to the people in it: hiring has been near its lowest level in more than a decade while layoffs are unusually low, so employers are barely firing but also barely hiring, which locks out job switchers, new graduates, and anyone hoping for a raise by moving. Labor force participation is close to its lowest level in about 50 years outside the pandemic, and real GDP growth has slowed from about 3.3 percent in 2023 to somewhere around 2 percent this year. Economists describe that as soft. Margins look great, headcount and paychecks do not.

Worth saying, though, is that economists do not mostly read this as a collapse signal. J.P. Morgan's Joe Seydl frames the index and sentiment as "apples and oranges," two different phenomena that normally move together but decouple when forward-looking pricing drifts from current activity, and the argument on the other side is that this kind of divergence lays groundwork for productivity growth that eventually broadens out. What it means for you practically is that a record high in the index tells you something about corporate profits and asset valuations, and very little about whether an average household feels financially secure. If you want the household picture, you want real wages after inflation, participation and openings in the labor market, and rent and home price data, not the level of the S&P.
