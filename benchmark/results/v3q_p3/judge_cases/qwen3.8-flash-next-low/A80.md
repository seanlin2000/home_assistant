## AWQ: Learned Scaling Factors for Superior Accuracy at Low Bit-Widths
AWQ (Lin et al., 2023) observes that not all weights are equally important—a small fraction of weights (0.1-1%) are 'salient' and disproportionately affect output quality. Instead of optimizing the quantization grid globally, AWQ learns per-channel scaling factors that protect salient weights during quantization. The key insight: scaling up important channels before quantization reduces their relative quantization error, and the scaling can be folded into subsequent layer norms or linear layers at inference time, incurring zero overhead.
The trick is that s is typically in [0.5, 2.0]. Channels with large activation magnitudes (outliers) get s > 1, reducing their quantization error at the cost of slightly increasing error for other channels. The scaling is then absorbed into the preceding layer's weights or the following layer's bias, making the inference kernel identical to standard INT4 matmul. AWQ achieves 4-bit accuracy comparable to GPTQ but with a simpler, faster calibration (no Hessian inversion).
AWQ excels at extreme low-bit widths (3-bit, 2-bit) where GPTQ degrades. For 4-bit, both methods are near-lossless, but AWQ's calibration is 10x faster (minutes vs hours). The trade-off: AWQ's scaling factors are channel-wise, not per-group, so it cannot correct fine-grained errors within a channel. However, for LLMs with heavy-tailed activation distributions, channel-wise scaling captures the dominant outlier structure. AWQ is supported in vLLM and TGI for production GPU inference.

ASSISTANT: Quantizing means replacing the model's weights, normally stored as 16-bit floating point numbers, with numbers represented in far fewer bits, usually integers, plus a small amount of metadata that lets the decoder reconstruct something close to the original values. The weights are what quantization shrinks; activations and the attention key-value cache typically stay at 16-bit or 8-bit floating point. In practice you don't store one global scale, you store a scale (and often a zero point) for every small group of weights, say 64 or 128 of them, because that limits how much one big weight can distort a whole block.

For a 70-billion-parameter model, the pure arithmetic is straightforward. At 16-bit that's 140 billion bytes, roughly 140 gigabytes, or about 130 gibibytes. At a true 4 bits per weight it's 17.5 billion bytes, about 16 gibibytes. Add the group scales and, with a group size of 128, you're closer to 4.25 effective bits per weight, which is about 37 billion bytes, and that lines up with what people actually observe: published benchmarks for Llama-class 70B models on a single H100 report roughly 35 gigabytes of VRAM for AWQ or GPTQ 4-bit. At 2 bits per weight the raw figure is 8.75 billion bytes, and with per-group metadata you're nearer 2.25 bits per weight, call it 20 billion bytes or about 18 gibibytes. So 4-bit is roughly a four-times reduction and 2-bit an eight-times reduction, with the caveat that the savings are always slightly worse than the headline bit-width because of that bookkeeping.

The reason anyone bothers is bandwidth. Decoder inference at small batch sizes is memory-bound, so reading four times fewer bytes per weight usually means several times more tokens per second, and it moves a 70B model from "needs a multi-GPU server" to "fits on one high-end consumer card or a large unified-memory Mac."

The trade-offs are real but uneven. According to 2026 benchmark roundups, 4-bit weight quantization costs on the order of one to three percent perplexity versus FP16, and roughly one or two percent on broad benchmarks like MMLU, which most people can't feel in ordinary conversation. INT8 is essentially lossless. The cliff is below 4 bits: 3-bit quantization shows three to eight percent perplexity degradation, and reasoning-heavy benchmarks fall about three times faster than perplexity does, which is why the general advice for agents, tool use, and code is not to go below 4-bit. At 2 bits, degradation is noticeable with plain post-training quantization; making it usable takes specialized techniques that identify and keep a small set of outlier or "salient" weights at higher precision, or quantization-aware training, and it requires real engineering effort and hardware support.

Two more subtleties worth knowing. First, quality degradation scales inversely with model size, so a 70B model tolerates 4-bit far better than a 7B model, which can lose five to ten percent and often needs 5- or 6-bit. Second, generic perplexity hides task-specific regressions: code generation, function calling, and long-context recall can degrade even when perplexity barely moves, so quantized models need to be checked against the workload you actually care about. The method also matters: AWQ-style activation-aware scaling protects the weights that disproportionately affect output, generally doing better than GPTQ at 3-bit and 2-bit, and the inference kernel matters as much as the algorithm, since well-optimized kernels like Marlin can change throughput by an order of magnitude. On newer NVIDIA hardware, native FP8, and FP4 on Blackwell, gives the best quality per unit of throughput, so quantization is less of a forced compromise there than it was on older cards.
