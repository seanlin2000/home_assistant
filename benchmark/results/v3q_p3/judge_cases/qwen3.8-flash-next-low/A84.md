Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock is worth the present value of every dollar it will ever earn, and those dollars have to be discounted back to today at a rate that reflects what money costs. When the Fed pushes short-term rates up, that discount rate rises too, and a higher discount rate makes distant money worth less. Tech companies are especially exposed because so much of their value is distant money.

Start with the discounting itself. Investors value a stream of future cash flows by dividing each year's cash by one plus the discount rate, raised to the number of years out. That discount rate is anchored to the risk-free rate, which is what you can get from a Treasury, plus a premium for taking equity risk. The Fed doesn't set the ten-year yield directly, but it steers it, so a tightening cycle moves the anchor.

Here's the part that matters for tech. Suppose you have a dollar of cash flow arriving ten years from now. At a three percent discount rate, that future dollar is worth about 74 cents today. At eight percent, it's worth about 46 cents. That's a 38 percent haircut on the far-out cash, while cash arriving next year only drops from about 97 cents to 93 cents, a four percent haircut. So the higher rate barely touches near-term earnings and devastates distant ones.

That's why people call these long-duration assets, borrowing the language from bonds, where a thirty-year bond's price lurches far more when yields move than a two-year note's does. A utility or an oil company or a bank is earning a steady pile of cash right now, so most of its value sits close to today and is relatively insensitive to the discount rate. A high-growth software or biotech company may be barely profitable today, and the case for owning it rests on margins and earnings that show up in year seven, year twelve, year twenty. Its whole thesis is the far end of the cash flow curve, exactly the part that gets hit hardest. So the same rate move can knock twenty or thirty percent off a money-losing growth name and leave a mature industrial largely alone.

There's a second mechanism on top of the math, and it works on the cash flows themselves rather than the discount rate. Tighter money means more expensive debt, which matters for companies that fund losses with borrowing. It usually cools credit, hiring, ad budgets and enterprise software spending, which are revenue lines many tech firms depend on. It raises the cost of capital for the venture funding that feeds the growth pipeline. Higher rates also tend to strengthen the dollar and compress the risk appetite that funds speculative positions. And there's a straightforward competition effect: if a ten-year Treasury pays five percent, a stock promising volatile growth has to clear a much higher bar to look attractive, which drains demand for the highest-multiple names and forces their price down toward what the earnings can actually support.

It's worth adding two nuances, because the relationship is a strong tendency rather than a law. First, the earnings effect and the discount effect can offset each other. If the Fed is hiking because the economy is roaring, tech revenues are growing, and investors can revise those far-out cash flows upward, which is partly why the sector held up reasonably well in 2023 and 2024 even with rates high. What did real damage in 2022 was hiking combined with falling expectations, which is the worst combination: less distant cash and a higher rate discounting it. Second, the market now splits "tech" into two very different animals. The giant platform companies generate enormous current free cash flow and are valued closer to mature businesses, so they behave more like the insensitive end of the curve. The unprofitable, high-multiple growth names are the true long-duration assets, and they move most violently with rates.

One simplification to keep in mind: I've been treating the discount rate as moving one-for-one with the Fed. In reality equity discount rates also include an equity risk premium, which can rise or fall during a tightening cycle depending on whether the hikes are seen as keeping inflation tame or as breaking something.
