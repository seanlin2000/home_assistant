Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. ASSISTANT: The short answer is that the atmosphere acts like a sieve that lets some colors of sunlight through more easily than others, and which colors get trapped depends on how far the light has to travel through the air.

Start with the sunlight itself. It looks white, but it's really a mixture of every color, blended together. Each color is light of a particular wavelength, with violet and blue being the short, choppy ones and orange and red being the long, lazy ones.

When that light hits the top of the atmosphere, it runs into molecules of nitrogen and oxygen. Those molecules are far smaller than a wavelength of light, and they bounce the light around in every direction. Here's the key part: they don't bounce all colors equally. Short, choppy wavelengths get deflected much more strongly than long ones. The effect grows extremely fast as the wavelength shrinks. Blue light, at roughly four hundred and fifty nanometers, gets scattered about six times as strongly as red light at seven hundred nanometers. That's the whole mechanism in one sentence, and physicists call it Rayleigh scattering.

So during the day, sunlight that's heading straight down passes through a relatively thin layer of air. Most of the red and yellow keeps going in a straight line, but the blue gets knocked sideways and bounced all over the sky, and it gets bounced again and again. Turn your head in any direction away from the sun and you're looking at a fresh batch of that scattered blue arriving at your eye. That's why the blue isn't just near the sun, it's everywhere overhead, and it's also why the sun itself looks a little pale yellow rather than pure white: you've had some of the blue removed from the beam that reaches you directly.

Sunset is the same process, just taken to an extreme. The sun is near the horizon, so its light has to travel sideways through a much thicker slice of the atmosphere to reach you, something like ten times more air than when the sun is overhead. The blue is scattered out of that direct beam so thoroughly, over and over along the long path, that almost none of it survives to arrive. What's left in the beam is the stuff that was hardest to scatter: orange and red. So the sun's disc itself turns deep orange or even red.

The colors you see smeared across the sky and the clouds come from the leftovers. The orange and red light that did manage to cross all that air hits dust and haze near the ground, and gets scattered off those onto the clouds and the underside of the sky, painting them pink and crimson. Meanwhile the part of the sky farthest from the sun, where the light took a shorter route, often still looks blue or violet. That's why a sunset has a gradient from red near the horizon to blue up and away from it.

Two loose ends people often ask about. First, violet is scattered even more strongly than blue, so you might expect a violet sky. The sun emits less violet to begin with, our eyes are much less sensitive to it, and some of it is absorbed high up, so what we perceive reads as blue. Second, hazy, dusty or smoky evenings give the most spectacular sunsets. Bigger particles like dust, smoke and volcanic ash scatter all colors more evenly and also absorb some light, which removes more of the blues and yellows and leaves an intense red behind.

One last way to picture it: on the Moon there's no atmosphere, so there's nothing to scatter the light sideways. The sun is bright and the sky around it is pitch black, even at high noon. The blue sky is not something the sky is made of. It's sunlight that the air has knocked out of its straight path.
