ASSISTANT: Both of those symptoms have pretty well-understood explanations, and the fact that the choppy audio shows up specifically when the laptop joins the picture is the biggest clue you have.

On the audio side, the most likely culprit is radio congestion in the 2.4 gigahertz band. Bluetooth hops around that band, and so does 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, many wireless mouse and controller receivers, and, if you are casting your laptop's screen wirelessly to the projector, Miracast, which runs over Wi-Fi Direct. Bluetooth and Wi-Fi in the same laptop often share a single combo chip and sometimes a single antenna, so when the laptop starts pushing heavy traffic, Bluetooth packets get fewer slots, get retransmitted, and you hear crackle and dropout. Microsoft's own support forum has a long thread on exactly this under Windows 11, and the commonly reported fix is moving the laptop onto a 5 GHz Wi-Fi network. A second, less obvious source is USB 3.0: Intel and the USB Implementers Forum published a white paper showing that poorly shielded USB 3.0 ports, hubs, cables and external drives radiate broadband noise that sits right on top of the 2.4 GHz band, and one test showed nearly a 20 decibel rise in the noise floor. So a hard drive or hub plugged in next to your laptop can quietly wreck nearby Bluetooth. Third, Windows can silently switch a Bluetooth device from high-quality stereo, which is the A2DP profile, into the low-bandwidth hands-free headset profile the moment something opens a microphone, such as Discord or in-game voice chat. That switch alone can make audio sound thin and stuttery. Fourth, if the audio path is laptop to projector by HDMI or casting, and then the projector sends it out by Bluetooth to the JBL, you have two wireless hops and stacked latency, and the projector's own Bluetooth implementation is doing the buffering. Fifth, ordinary physical things: distance, the speaker sitting behind or under the projector, a metal shelf, and Windows' power saving feature turning the Bluetooth adapter off intermittently.

Here is a way to narrow that down without guessing. Start by removing the laptop from the equation entirely, since that reproduces the fault cleanly. Play something from the projector's own apps, or from a phone, with no laptop in the room and no Wi-Fi traffic. If the audio is clean, the problem is your laptop or your network, not the speaker or projector. Then reconnect the laptop and change one variable at a time. Move the laptop's Wi-Fi to the 5 GHz band, or set the projector and laptop up on 5 GHz if you are casting. Turn the router's 2.4 GHz radio off for a few minutes, or move your Wi-Fi to a channel far from the one you use, and see whether the stutter changes. Unplug every USB 3.0 device, hub and cable near the laptop and test again; if it improves, reintroduce them one at a time to find the offender. Check the sound output device name in Windows: if it says hands-free or headset instead of stereo, close anything using your microphone, or disable the Hands-Free Telephony service on that Bluetooth device in Device Manager. Turn off the "allow the computer to turn off this device to save power" option for the Bluetooth adapter, and update the Bluetooth driver from the laptop maker's site rather than letting Windows Update supply a generic one. Move the JBL into clear line of sight within a couple of meters, and if the projector offers an audio codec choice such as SBC, AAC or the newer LC3, try the more robust lower-rate option. A clean test is to route the laptop's audio straight to the JBL by Bluetooth while the video still goes to the projector by cable; if that is clean, the projector's Bluetooth output stage is the weak link, and a wired optical or 3.5 millimetre connection to the speaker is the permanent fix.

Now the eye strain. Projectors are not inherently harder on the eyes than monitors; in fact reflected light is generally gentler than a direct-lit panel. But projectors introduce a specific set of things that cause fatigue, and in gaming setups one of them usually dominates. The first is angular size. A 110 or 130 inch image watched from a normal desk distance means your eyes and head are doing constant scanning and repeated refocusing across the frame, which is nothing like looking at a 27 inch monitor an arm's length away. That is the most common cause in the configuration you describe, and it is mostly a furniture problem: move back, or shrink the image. The second is sharpness at the pixel level. Projectors are almost never tacky edge to edge, especially on a curved or tensioned screen, and keystone correction, lens shift, and scaling a 1080p game signal up to a 4K projector all smear fine detail. Your eyes then strain trying to resolve text and HUD elements that are genuinely not in focus. Get this right before blaming anything else: focus until all four corners and the centre are equally sharp, disable keystone correction and use physical alignment instead, and set the projector's picture size to its native resolution with your PC outputting the projector's native resolution rather than being upscaled.
