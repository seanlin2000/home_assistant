## Consecutive days or Flex?
The standard pass runs on consecutive days: an 8-day pass covers 8 days in a row. The Swiss Travel Pass Flex (from CHF 289) gives you the same number of travel days to use on any dates within one month — you activate each day before your first ride.
## How many days do you need?
- Trip of 3–5 days: a 3 or 4-day pass matching your travel days — or compare with the Half Fare Card, which can win on short trips with few train rides.
- One-week trip: the 8-day pass is the sweet spot — it costs only CHF 40 more than 6 days and covers arrival and departure days, including your airport trains.

## First or second class?
Second class is comfortable, clean and how most Swiss travel — it is the right default. First class buys you quieter cars, wider seats and better odds of a window seat on busy panoramic routes in July–August. If the budget question is “1st class or two extra mountain excursions?”, take the mountains.
## How you actually use the pass (the part nobody explains)
- Just board. Swiss stations have no fare gates. Walk to the platform, get on, sit in your class. A conductor checks tickets on board — show the QR code on your phone.

# Mountain Excursions in Switzerland
Which summit is worth the day — and what your pass actually pays for
Ten summits worth the day, what your pass actually does on each, and the ones to skip. The mountains are where a Swiss trip gets expensive, so this is the page to read before you book anything.
## The short answer
The Swiss Travel Pass covers the journey to the foot of the mountain in full.
The railway or cable car above that is usually discounted rather than included, and the discount differs from mountain to mountain.
On nine of the ten summits below, the pass cuts the mountain railway fare rather than covering it, so budget for a ticket on top of your pass. Stanserhorn is the exception: it is free.
Jungfraujoch is the most expensive single excursion most visitors make, and the pass reduces it rather than covering it — by a third from Interlaken Ost, a quarter from Grindelwald or Wengen.
On several mountain railways the Half Fare Card gives 50% off, while the Swiss Travel Pass gives 25% to 50% depending on the route.
Every summit sells the same ticket in cloud as in sunshine, so the forecast decides more about value than the price does.

## Free, discounted, or full price — the distinction that decides your pass
This is the single most common misunderstanding in Swiss travel, and it costs people real money. “Covered by the Swiss Travel Pass” is used loosely online to mean three different things.
- Included. You board with the pass and pay nothing. This is true for the trains, buses, boats and city transport that carry you to the valley station — and for a handful of lower mountain railways, though none of the ten headline summits below.
- Discounted. You buy a ticket at a reduced rate. This is what happens on most of the big summits, and the reduction is not the same everywhere.
- Not covered at all. Some activities at the top — the rides at Grindelwald First, for instance — are ticketed separately and no pass touches them.
Work out which of the three applies to the mountains you actually want before choosing a pass. A trip built around two expensive summits and little daily travel often favours the Half Fare Card; a trip with daily train days favours the Swiss Travel Pass.
## When to skip the mountain
Three situations where the money is better spent elsewhere.
- The summit is in cloud. The view is the entire product. On a grey day you are paying a premium to stand in fog, and every one of these mountains charges the same either way.
- You are chasing the famous one on a short trip. Jungfraujoch eats a full day and a serious share of the budget. On a three or four day visit, a nearer summit gives you the same alpine panorama and leaves you a day to use.
- You booked ahead for a fixed date. Buying a dated summit ticket weeks out removes your ability to move it to the clear morning. Keep the mountain day flexible and decide it the night before.
When it does work, it is the part of the trip people remember. Clear sky, an early train, and a summit before the crowds arrive is worth planning the rest of the week around.
## Mountain excursion questions
## Are mountain excursions included in the Swiss Travel Pass?
Some are and most are not. The pass covers the trains, buses and boats that get you to the valley station in full. The mountain railway or cable car above that is usually discounted rather than included, and the size of the discount varies by mountain. On this list only Stanserhorn is covered in full. On the other nine the pass cuts the fare, usually by half: Gornergrat falls from CHF 132 to CHF 66, Titlis from CHF 102 to CHF 51, Glacier 3000 from CHF 89 to CHF 45. Rigi, Stoos, Klewenalp and Brunni are fully included too.
## Which mountain is worth it if I can only do one?
If the weather is clear and budget is not the deciding factor, Jungfraujoch is the one most people plan a trip around. If you want the same panorama for less, Schilthorn and Gornergrat both put the big peaks in front of you for a fraction of the outlay.
## Do children travel free on mountain excursions?
Children under 16 travel free on the Swiss Travel System with the Swiss Family Card when accompanied by a parent holding a valid pass, but mountain railways set their own child pricing and it is not always the same. Check the individual excursion before assuming the family rule applies all the way to the summit.

# Mountain Excursions in Switzerland
Which summit is worth the day — and what your pass actually pays for
Ten summits worth the day, what your pass actually does on each, and the ones to skip. The mountains are where a Swiss trip gets expensive, so this is the page to read before you book anything.
## The short answer
The Swiss Travel Pass covers the journey to the foot of the mountain in full.
The railway or cable car above that is usually discounted rather than included, and the discount differs from mountain to mountain.
On nine of the ten summits below, the pass cuts the mountain railway fare rather than covering it, so budget for a ticket on top of your pass. Stanserhorn is the exception: it is free.
Jungfraujoch is the most expensive single excursion most visitors make, and the pass reduces it rather than covering it — by a third from Interlaken Ost, a quarter from Grindelwald or Wengen.
On several mountain railways the Half Fare Card gives 50% off, while the Swiss Travel Pass gives 25% to 50% depending on the route.
Every summit sells the same ticket in cloud as in sunshine, so the forecast decides more about value than the price does.

## Free, discounted, or full price — the distinction that decides your pass
This is the single most common misunderstanding in Swiss travel, and it costs people real money. “Covered by the Swiss Travel Pass” is used loosely online to mean three different things.
- Included. You board with the pass and pay nothing. This is true for the trains, buses, boats and city transport that carry you to the valley station — and for a handful of lower mountain railways, though none of the ten headline summits below.
- Discounted. You buy a ticket at a reduced rate. This is what happens on most of the big summits, and the reduction is not the same everywhere.
- Not covered at all. Some activities at the top — the rides at Grindelwald First, for instance — are ticketed separately and no pass touches them.
Work out which of the three applies to the mountains you actually want before choosing a pass. A trip built around two expensive summits and little daily travel often favours the Half Fare Card; a trip with daily train days favours the Swiss Travel Pass.
## When to skip the mountain
Three situations where the money is better spent elsewhere.
- The summit is in cloud. The view is the entire product. On a grey day you are paying a premium to stand in fog, and every one of these mountains charges the same either way.
- You are chasing the famous one on a short trip. Jungfraujoch eats a full day and a serious share of the budget. On a three or four day visit, a nearer summit gives you the same alpine panorama and leaves you a day to use.
- You booked ahead for a fixed date. Buying a dated summit ticket weeks out removes your ability to move it to the clear morning. Keep the mountain day flexible and decide it the night before.
When it does work, it is the part of the trip people remember. Clear sky, an early train, and a summit before the crowds arrive is worth planning the rest of the week around.
## Mountain excursion questions
## Are mountain excursions included in the Swiss Travel Pass?
Some are and most are not. The pass covers the trains, buses and boats that get you to the valley station in full. The mountain railway or cable car above that is usually discounted rather than included, and the size of the discount varies by mountain. On this list only Stanserhorn is covered in full. On the other nine the pass cuts the fare, usually by half: Gornergrat falls from CHF 132 to CHF 66, Titlis from CHF 102 to CHF 51, Glacier 3000 from CHF 89 to CHF 45. Rigi, Stoos, Klewenalp and Brunni are fully included too.
## Which mountain is worth it if I can only do one?
If the weather is clear and budget is not the deciding factor, Jungfraujoch is the one most people plan a trip around. If you want the same panorama for less, Schilthorn and Gornergrat both put the big peaks in front of you for a fraction of the outlay.
## Do children travel free on mountain excursions?
Children under 16 travel free on the Swiss Travel System with the Swiss Family Card when accompanied by a parent holding a valid pass, but mountain railways set their own child pricing and it is not always the same. Check the individual excursion before assuming the family rule applies all the way to the summit.
