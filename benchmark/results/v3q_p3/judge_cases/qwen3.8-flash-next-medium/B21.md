ASSISTANT: First, a correction worth hearing before you do anything: the Fed did not cut rates to zero last month. At its October 28 meeting it held the federal funds target range steady at 3.75 to 4 percent, on a 10-to-2 vote, and it has generally been in a wait-and-see posture because inflation is still running warm. Reuters reported that the hold followed a hike in September, so the recent direction has been up, not down.

Mortgage rates reflect that. The average 30-year fixed rate is sitting somewhere around 7 percent right now, with different trackers showing 6.96 to 7.23 percent depending on how they sample lenders, and 15-year rates in the mid-6 percent range. Reports out of the November 10 inflation day actually pushed rates a bit higher, not lower. So the reason that would normally motivate a refinance, a sharp drop in rates, isn't present.

That said, whether you should refinance doesn't depend on the Fed's current level. It depends entirely on your existing rate. The rule of thumb is that refinancing makes sense when your current rate is meaningfully above what you can get today, roughly 1.5 to 2 percentage points or more, because the closing costs, usually around 2 to 3 percent of the loan balance, have to be paid back out of the monthly savings. If you're on something like an 8.5 or 9 percent rate from the 2023 to 2024 stretch, a 7 percent refinance is likely worth it. If you're sitting on a 5 or 6 percent rate from 2020 or 2021, refinancing at today's rates would probably cost you money.

Tell me your current rate, your remaining balance, roughly how many years are left on the loan, and whether you know your estimated closing costs, and I'll work out the new payment, the total interest, and how many months it would take to break even.
