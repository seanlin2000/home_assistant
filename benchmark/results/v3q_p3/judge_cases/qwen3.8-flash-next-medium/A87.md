ASSISTANT: Two separate problems here, and they're worth separating because the troubleshooting methods are quite different. Let me take the audio first.

The single most likely culprit for choppy Bluetooth audio that starts when a laptop connects is radio interference, because Bluetooth and 2.4 gigahertz Wi‑Fi share the same band and, in a laptop, they usually live on one combo card with a shared antenna. Microsoft's own support forum has a thread describing exactly this symptom on Windows 11, stutter appearing while Wi‑Fi is active on 2.4 GHz and going away on 5 GHz. So the cleanest test is to change nothing but the radio: connect the laptop to your router's 5 or 6 GHz band, or briefly disable Wi‑Fi entirely, and play the same local video. If the choppy audio disappears, it's coexistence, and the fixes are staying off 2.4 GHz, moving the laptop's Wi‑Fi antenna or router channel away from the channel Bluetooth is hopping through, and keeping USB 3.0 drives and hubs away from the laptop, since those leak broadband noise right into that band. If it's unchanged, interference isn't the problem and you move on.

The second test is to check whether the profile is changing. Windows will drop a Bluetooth device from high‑quality stereo to the hands‑free call profile the moment anything requests a microphone, and hands‑free audio is narrowband mono and frequently sounds choppy or thin. Open Sound settings and look at whether the JBL is listed as a "headset" with two endpoints, and try disabling hands‑free telephony for it. If your games use voice chat, that alone may explain why gaming is worse.

Third, isolate whether the projector is involved at all. If the speaker is connected to the projector over Bluetooth and you also pair the laptop, you now have two sources competing for one link, and multipoint handling on speakers is often the weak link. Test by connecting the laptop directly to the JBL with the projector disconnected, and separately test the laptop's audio going through the projector's own connection or through a cable. If it only stutters when three devices are in play, that's a topology problem, not a driver problem. Then work the usual software items one at a time: update the Bluetooth driver from the laptop manufacturer rather than trusting Windows Update, turn off the "allow the computer to turn off this device to save power" setting for the Bluetooth adapter, move the speaker closer with a clear line of sight rather than behind the projector or a piece of furniture, and check whether the JBL's own firmware has an update. Also note the codec: if the projector negotiates AAC or aptX but the laptop falls back to SBC, the laptop path will simply be worse, and switching output devices will look like a stutter at the moment of handoff.

Now the eye strain. General comparisons of projectors to screens tend to conclude that projectors are gentler, because you're looking at reflected light rather than a backlit panel. So the fact that yours is *worse* is actually useful: it suggests something specific in your setup rather than an inherent problem with projectors, and those specific causes are testable.

The first thing I'd suspect is flicker from the lamp power settings. Many projectors dim by pulse‑width modulation, meaning the light source is strobing on and off, and that becomes much more pronounced in Eco or Dynamic Eco mode and in the lower brightness presets. Combined with a dynamic iris or auto brightness that hunts up and down with the game's scenes, you get brightness changes your pupils are constantly reacting to. Set the lamp to full or max brightness mode with the dynamic iris and auto‑brightness features off, then judge comfort again. Related to that is brightness matching: an image that is far too bright for the room, or conversely a dim image you're straining to read, both cause fatigue. ViewSonic's guidance is essentially this, that a bright image in a fully dark room is itself a source of fatigue, so a soft ambient bias light behind the projector helps a lot.

The second thing is focus and sharpness, which is easy to overlook and hard on the eyes when it's subtly wrong. Check the focus across the whole image, not just the center, since a slightly tilted or buckled screen produces a soft zone you'll never consciously notice but will keep trying to resolve. Turn off any digital keystone correction, use physical lens shift or moving the projector instead, because keystone resampling softens text and fine detail. If you're projecting onto a bare wall rather than a screen, texture and sparkle add their own noise, and if you sit close enough to see the pixel structure, that's tiring too.

Third, motion. A monitor at 120 hertz with fast pixel response looks completely different from a projector at 60 hertz with slower response and often an interpolation "sports" or "game" mode layered on top. Motion smoothing can help movies and can badly hurt games, since it adds latency and creates its own artifacts, while slow response creates smearing that your visual system keeps trying to track. Also worth measuring is input lag, because the delay between your hand and the screen changes how tense your posture and eye movements become during a game. Turn the projector's processing modes off entirely and use its explicit game mode, which typically exists precisely to cut lag and interpolation.
