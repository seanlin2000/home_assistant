ASSISTANT: Good question, and the answer is more asymmetric than people expect: the model side is private, but the search side is a fairly leaky pipe, and the leak comes from the queries your model *writes*, not from your conversation.

What stays local. The model's weights, your prompt, your chat history, any documents you've loaded into context, the model's intermediate reasoning, and everything it writes in your notes or files never leave your machine. If the answer to the question is already in your own files, nothing goes out at all. That's the real advantage of local inference.

What gets transmitted. When the model decides to search, it emits a query string, and that string is the whole story from the provider's point of view. The provider sees the query text exactly as the model wrote it, plus your API key, which identifies your account, your IP address, a timestamp, and the parameters you passed, such as result count, language, SafeSearch level, and a country or location hint if you included one. Some providers also see which URLs you pulled afterward, because features like Tavily's Extract or Crawl, Exa's Contents API, and Brave's LLM Context endpoint go fetch full page text on your behalf, so the provider ends up holding a record of the specific pages you wanted, not just the topic.

The subtlety is that a query string is a compression of your intent, and models are terrible at compressing safely. Ask a local model to help with a confidential acquisition and it will cheerfully search for "financials for [company name] acquisition 2026," complete with the name you never intended to publish. Ask it about a medical symptom in a personal journal and the symptom, your age, and a city can all end up in the query. So even though the provider never sees your conversation, a handful of queries can be enough to reconstruct it, and combined with an API key and IP address, they're attributable to you.

One more thing worth knowing: not all search APIs are one hop. Scraper-based providers relay your query on to Google or Bing in real time, which means you've now exposed the query to a second company whose retention policies you can't control at all. Providers that run their own index are simpler to reason about. Brave markets itself on exactly this point, saying its independent index of something like 35 billion pages lets it avoid that relay. Do read the fine print, though: Brave advertises Zero Data Retention, but summaries of its API privacy documents say query records tied to a Search API account are kept up to 90 days for billing and troubleshooting, with true ZDR being an enterprise option. Tavily advertises SOC 2 and zero data retention but describes zero query retention as something you switch on in account settings rather than a default, with an EU-hosted endpoint offered separately. Exa similarly lists zero data retention as an enterprise-tier feature. The pattern across the industry is that zero retention is usually a setting or a paid tier, not the baseline, so the useful habit is to go look for that toggle rather than assume it.

To minimize exposure, the highest-leverage step is rewriting queries before they leave. Have your agent generate a sanitized search string: generic phrasing, no proper nouns, no filenames, no email addresses, no numbers that are only meaningful to you. Searching for "typical closing timeline for a mid-size SaaS acquisition" is fine; searching for the target company's name is not. Second, keep search on a dedicated account, a key created just for this, used only for search, so nothing links back to your name or your other projects, and ideally paid in a way that doesn't tie it to your identity. Third, run the search calls from a separate identity on the network, a VPN or a container on a different connection, so the IP and the machine don't line up with your home address. Fourth, prefer independent-index providers over scraper relays, and explicitly enable zero retention or enterprise ZDR if you're handling anything sensitive. Fifth, skip the extract and crawl endpoints when a snippet is enough, since fetching full text tells the provider which specific pages you cared about. Sixth, don't send parameters that carry your location unless the query genuinely needs them. And if you want a genuinely local option, self-hosted metasearch like SearXNG gives you a search layer on your own machine, though it still has to talk to upstream engines, so it narrows exposure to one intermediary rather than eliminating it.

A rough rule of thumb: assume every query you send is stored, attributable, and searchable later, and design your agent so the queries would be harmless if a stranger read them. One caveat I can't resolve from what I found: these providers' retention terms change and sometimes differ between marketing pages and the actual data-processing agreements, so if this is for regulated data, read the current DPA for whichever provider you pick rather than trusting the headline claim.
