Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. # Exchange 1

USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The whole thing comes down to sunlight, air, and distance.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow. Light travels in waves, and each color has a different wavelength. Blue and violet light has short, quick waves. Red and orange light has long, lazy waves.

Now, the air around us isn't empty. It's full of tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, far too small to see. When sunlight hits them, the light bounces off in all directions, like a ball bouncing off a wall. The catch is that the short waves — blue and violet — get knocked around much more easily than the long red ones. This is called Rayleigh scattering, and it's the whole story.

So during the day, sunlight enters the top of the atmosphere and the blue part of it gets scattered in every direction, all across the sky. No matter which way you look, some of that scattered blue light is heading toward your eyes. The sky isn't blue because you're looking at the sun; it's blue because you're looking at air that's been sprinkled with scattered blue light. (Violet actually scatters even more than blue, but our eyes are much less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up, so we see blue instead.)

Sunset is the same physics with a different geometry. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a long, shallow slice of atmosphere to reach you — many times more air than when the sun is overhead. On that long journey, the blue light gets scattered so thoroughly that almost none of it survives to arrive at your eyes. What's left is the reds, oranges and yellows, the long waves that weren't scattered away. So the sun itself, and the clouds near it, look like embers.

That also explains a few related things. The sky near the horizon at midday looks paler and whiter, because you're looking through a thicker wedge of air that has mixed in other scattered light. On a day when the air is loaded with smoke, dust, or volcanic ash, the bigger particles scatter all colors fairly equally, which washes the sky toward gray or brown — and sunsets get dramatically red, because the extra filtering strips out even more of what little blue remained.

A good way to picture it: the atmosphere acts like a sieve for color. Short waves are caught easily, long waves slip through. Look straight up through a short path of air and you see what got caught, the blue. Look along a long path through thick air and you see what made it all the way through, the red.
