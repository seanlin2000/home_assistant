Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: The core of it is a valuation idea: a stock's price is supposed to reflect the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future, and the interest rate is the discount you apply to that future cash to translate it into today's dollars.

Here's the mechanics. If a company will earn money years from now, that money is worth less to you today than money you can hold right now, because you could otherwise put today's dollars into something safe that pays interest. The higher the safe rate, the bigger the haircut. Mathematically you divide future cash flows by something like one plus the discount rate, raised to the number of years. Raise the rate a little, and the value of cash far in the future falls a lot, because you're compounding the discount.

Technology companies are what analysts call long-duration assets, and the duration here isn't about bonds, it's about when the money arrives. Many tech firms plow nearly all their revenue back into growth, so their current earnings are thin and their cash flows are expected to be large a decade or two out, in the maturity phase after they've captured a market. Cash that distant gets hit hardest by a rate increase, so the present value of the same business can shrink substantially even if nothing about the business itself changed. Growth stocks are, in effect, a bet on the distant future, and higher rates make the distant future worth less today.

A rising Fed funds rate transmits through in a few ways at once. First, it pushes up the risk-free rate that anchors the discount rate, which is the whole denominator in a discounted cash flow model. If the safe ten-year yield rises from, say, two percent to four percent, a dollar expected in ten years is worth meaningfully less, and a dollar expected in twenty years is worth less still. Second, it raises the hurdle rate for the riskier equity discount rate, so the premium investors demand for holding stocks also tends to widen. Third, it raises the cost of the borrowed money that many tech companies use to fund expansion, which eats into margins and slows the growth the valuation was premised on. Fourth, it strengthens the case for alternatives: higher yields make bonds, money market funds, and cash genuinely competitive, so the marginal dollar bidding for growth stocks has a real option it didn't have at zero rates. Fifth, higher rates usually slow the economy, which trims the very spending on software, devices, and advertising that tech revenue rides on.

There's a valuation shortcut that captures this: when you value a company as a multiple of its earnings, the multiple behaves roughly like one over the discount rate minus the growth rate. Lower the growth rate, or raise the discount rate, and the multiple compresses. Tech stocks historically carried some of the richest multiples in the market precisely because their expected growth was high and rates were near zero, so they had more room to fall when both variables moved against them.

The 2022 cycle is the clean case study. The Fed went from essentially zero to over four percent within about a year, the ten-year Treasury yield roughly doubled, and the Nasdaq fell somewhere around a third at its worst. The most striking detail was that the losses weren't spread evenly across companies with similar business quality; the biggest draws came from the highest-multiple, least-profitable names, exactly what you'd expect if the pain was primarily mathematical rather than a collapse in business prospects. Amazon is a favorite example among strategists because it was often cited as a case where the market had assigned enormous value to cash flows far in the future, so its price was unusually rate-sensitive.

Two important caveats, though, because the relationship isn't a law. A rate hike that reflects an economy genuinely booming can lift earnings enough to offset the discounting effect, and sometimes it does; rate rises driven by strong demand aren't uniformly bad for stocks. The transmission is also partly about sentiment and positioning, not just arithmetic, since a lot of tech valuation was built on assumptions about where rates would eventually settle. And some tech firms are now quite profitable with large cash piles and little debt, which makes them less rate-sensitive than the money-losing growth names of the previous decade. So the general rule holds: higher rates compress the value of far-off cash flows, and tech is where the far-off cash flows are, but the size of the hit depends on how profitable the company is today and where it sits on the maturity curve.
