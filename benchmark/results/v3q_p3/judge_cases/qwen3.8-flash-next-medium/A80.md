ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means keeping every one of its parameters but storing each one with fewer bits. A 70-billion-parameter model is typically released at 16-bit precision, so its weights take up 70 billion times two bytes, or about 140 gigabytes. Drop to 4 bits per weight and the arithmetic is 70 billion times half a byte, which is 35 gigabytes — a quarter of the original. Drop to 2 bits and it's 70 billion times a quarter byte, 17.5 gigabytes.

Those clean numbers are the theory, though. In practice, quantized weights are stored in groups, and each group carries a small scale factor and sometimes extra metadata, so real files are bigger than the naive math. A common 4-bit format, llama.cpp's Q4_K_M, comes out around 40 gigabytes for a 70-billion model, and the 2-bit options land higher than 17.5 too: the Q2_K level works out closer to 19 to 26 gigabytes, because mixed precision pushes the true average up to roughly 2.6 to 3 bits per weight rather than a flat 2. So the honest framing is: 140 at full precision, about 35 to 40 at 4-bit, roughly 20 to 26 at 2-bit.

The trade-offs are worth understanding in order. Going from 16 bits to 8 costs essentially nothing, and 16 down to 4 is usually a mild hit — the sources I read put a 4-bit 70-billion model at around 96 to 98 percent of full precision on benchmarks, a few percentage points of loss on perplexity and hard tasks, often invisible to a human in ordinary conversation. The curve gets steep below 4 bits. At 3 and 2 bits the loss is substantial and the model starts to visibly change character: worse at math, tool use, structured reasoning and long-context recall. A 2-bit 70-billion model can still be genuinely useful, whereas a 2-bit 4-billion model usually is not, because smaller models have less redundancy to absorb the rounding error.

Speed is a real upside, not just a downside trade. Inference on a large model is mostly bottlenecked by memory bandwidth, so moving a quarter of the bytes per token usually speeds generation up, commonly around double for 4-bit versus 16-bit. On a Mac or a PC you can also split the model between GPU and system RAM, which lets an oversized quant run at all, just more slowly.

Two caveats people miss. Weights are only half the memory story: the KV cache, the model's working memory of the conversation, grows with context length and defaults to 16-bit, and at long contexts it can rival the model itself, so quantizing the cache matters too, with 8-bit generally considered negligible loss and 4-bit a small but real risk on tasks that lean on long documents. And quantization is lossy in a way that's not uniform: some outlier weights matter far more than others, which is why formats like AWQ and the K-quant families protect those weights and get better quality per bit than a flat, uniform 4-bit encoding.

The one rule that actually helps in practice: at a fixed memory budget, a larger model quantized harder usually beats a smaller model quantized lightly. So 40 gigabytes spent on a 4-bit 70-billion model tends to be a better buy than the same 40 gigabytes spent on a full-precision smaller one.
