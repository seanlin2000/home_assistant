Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: The short answer is that sunlight looks white, but it's really made of all the colors mixed together, and Earth's atmosphere sorts them out. The color you see depends on how much air the light had to travel through before it reached your eyes.

Here's the mechanism. Sunlight hits the tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen in the air, and each color in the light makes those molecules flicker briefly and re-emit the light in a new direction. This is called Rayleigh scattering, named after a nineteenth-century physicist who worked out the math. The key detail is that it strongly favors short wavelengths. Blue and violet light has short, choppy waves, so it gets knocked around in all directions many times more easily than red light, which has long, smooth waves that mostly ignore the molecules. Estimates of that difference usually put blue at roughly ten times as likely to scatter as red.

So during the day, look up away from the sun and what you're seeing is sunlight that got bounced off the air itself, coming at you from every direction. The blue part of the spectrum has been flung out of the direct beam and spread across the whole sky like a dim glow. That's why the sky isn't dark in the daytime even when you're not looking at the sun, and why it looks bluer straight up, where the light has scattered toward you rather than reaching you directly.

You might reasonably ask: violet waves are even shorter than blue, so shouldn't the sky be violet? It does scatter more violet. Two things keep it blue instead. Sunlight contains less violet to begin with, and our eyes are much less sensitive to violet and tend to blend it with the surrounding blue. So we perceive a blue sky with a slight violet lean near the horizon.

At sunset the geometry changes completely. The sun is low, so its light has to skim through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you, tens of times more air than when the sun is overhead. Every color gets scattered along that long path, but the blues and violets are scattered out of the beam so thoroughly that almost none of them survive the trip toward your eyes. What's left arriving directly from the sun is the reds and oranges that resisted scattering all afternoon. That's why the sun itself, and the clouds near it, turn deep orange or red: you're seeing the light that made it through, stripped of its blue.

The same effect explains a few related things. The sky just above the horizon often looks paler, almost whitish, because there the light is scattered multiple times and colors get mixed back together rather than separated cleanly. And sunsets after a big wildfire or volcanic eruption are more spectacular, because larger particles in the air scatter all wavelengths, not just the short ones, which dims the whole sun and pushes its remaining light further toward red.
