ASSISTANT: Quantizing a model means storing its weights in fewer bits. A model trained at normal precision keeps each of its parameters as a 32-bit floating-point number, or four bytes. Quantization maps those values onto a much coarser grid — say 4 bits or 2 bits per weight — so the same network fits in a fraction of the memory and can be loaded and moved around far faster.

ASSISTANT: At full 32-bit precision, those weights take 280 gigabytes. At 16 bits — half precision, the usual starting point for quantization — they take 140 gigabytes. Dropping to 4 bits cuts that to about 35 gigabytes, which is roughly 40 gigabytes once you add the overhead of the scaling factors each quantization group needs; that's the level where a 70B model fits on a single 48-gigabyte GPU or a decent desktop with enough RAM. At 2 bits it's about 17.5 gigabytes, plus overhead, so somewhere in the neighborhood of 20 gigabytes — small enough for a laptop, but at the edge of what's usable.

Note that these figures are weights only. You also need memory for the key-value cache that holds attention state as you generate text, which grows with context length and batch size, and if you're training or fine-tuning rather than just running inference, gradients and optimizer state add a great deal more. Quantization mainly shrinks the static part of the bill.

The trade-offs are real, and they get worse steeply as you go down. Fewer bits means less numerical resolution, so weights that were distinct start collapsing onto the same value; accuracy loss is usually reported as an increase in perplexity, a rise in loss on held-out text. Four-bit quantization — formats like GPTQ, AWQ, or NF4 used by bitsandbytes — is generally close to lossless for a model this large, often within a percent or two of the original, and it's what most people actually run. Two-bit is where things break down: quality drops noticeably, and the practical results depend heavily on method. Plain round-to-nearest at 2 bits is usually bad; techniques that keep a small number of layers or weights at higher precision, or that learn the quantization grid rather than just rounding to it, hold up much better. A useful rule of thumb from the recent literature is that around three bits per parameter is roughly where you get near-original quality, so 2-bit means accepting a genuine degradation in exchange for the memory savings.

Beyond raw accuracy, quantized models tend to be weaker on tasks that depend on precise arithmetic or long chains of reasoning, and small models suffer far more than large ones — a 70B model at 4 bits generally beats a much smaller model at full precision. Speed is a mixed picture: you get big gains in memory bandwidth and can use longer contexts or bigger batches, but the dequantization step costs compute, so throughput per token doesn't necessarily improve, and it depends on whether the format has real kernel support on your hardware.
