Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

ASSISTANT: The core idea is that a stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will produce in the future, and interest rates are the price of waiting for that cash. A discounted cash flow model takes each future year's cash flow and divides it by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of how many years out it lands. The discount rate is anchored to the risk-free Treasury yield, plus a premium for the risk of owning equities instead of bonds. When the Fed raises its policy rate, Treasury yields rise, the discount rate rises, and every future dollar is worth less today.

The reason technology gets hit harder than, say, energy or consumer staples is duration. In bond investing, duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in rates, and long-maturity bonds swing far more than short ones. Equities work the same way, but duration here means how far into the future a company's cash flows are concentrated. A mature business paying steady profits this year is a short-duration asset. A high-growth software or biotech company whose earnings are expected to arrive mostly in years five through fifteen is a long-duration asset, priced more like a thirty-year bond than a two-year bill.

The math is non-linear, and that's the part worth internalizing. A dollar expected ten years from now discounted at five percent is worth about sixty-one cents today. Raise the discount rate to ten percent and that same dollar is worth about thirty-nine cents, a decline of roughly thirty-seven percent for what is only a five-percentage-point move in rates. The further out the cash flow, the worse it gets, because the discount factor compounds over more years. For many growth companies, terminal value alone accounts for something like seventy to eighty percent of the estimated intrinsic value, which means most of the price tag sits on cash flows a decade or more away, exactly where rate changes bite hardest.

The 2022 hiking cycle is the clearest recent demonstration. The Fed moved the funds rate from near zero to about four and a half to nearly five percent in roughly a year, the fastest tightening since 1980. The Nasdaq Composite fell about thirty-three percent while the S&P 500 fell about nineteen to twenty percent, and information technology as a sector fell around twenty-eight percent. That gap wasn't sentiment; it was duration. Within tech the damage was uneven in a very telling way. Microsoft, generating something like seventy billion dollars of annual free cash flow, fell about twenty-eight percent because its near-term profits act as an anchor. Unprofitable high-multiple software companies fell sixty to eighty percent. Zoom, which had traded around fifty times revenue at its peak, dropped roughly sixty-three percent in a single year. The Ark Innovation ETF, built almost entirely on five-to-ten-year growth scenarios, fell about seventy-five percent from its early 2021 peak.
