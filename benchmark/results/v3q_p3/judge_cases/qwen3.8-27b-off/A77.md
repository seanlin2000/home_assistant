Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths (blue) far more than long ones (red), about the inverse fourth power of wavelength (Rayleigh scattering), so blue light bounces around the sky and reaches you from every direction. The sky is not violet because the sun gives off less violet, some is absorbed high up, and eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset the light crosses far more air on its long, low path, so most of the blue is scattered out before it reaches you and the reds and oranges are left; dust and haze strengthen the effect. USER: Why is the sky blue during the day but red and orange during sunset? Explain the physics in a way that someone without a science background can understand.

ASSISTANT: Great question — this one's all about how sunlight interacts with the air around us, and it's called Rayleigh scattering. Let me walk you through it.

First, some setup. Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colors of the rainbow bundled together. Each color has a different wavelength: blue and violet are short and zippy, while red and orange are long and lazy.

Now, sunlight doesn't just travel in a straight line from the sun to your eyes — it has to pass through the atmosphere, which is full of tiny gas molecules like nitrogen and oxygen. When light bumps into these molecules, it scatters, meaning it bounces off in all directions. Here's the key part: short wavelengths scatter way more than long ones. Blue light gets scattered roughly five to six times as much as red light.

So during midday, when the sun is high overhead and your eyes are looking away from it, you're seeing all that scattered blue light bouncing around the sky from every direction. That's why the whole dome looks blue. The violet scatters even more than blue, but our eyes are less sensitive to violet, and some of it gets absorbed higher up in the atmosphere, so we mostly notice the blue.

Now for sunset. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you — think of it as traveling a longer path instead of a short one. Over that long journey, all the blue and green light gets scattered out of the beam before it reaches your eyes. What's left? The reds and oranges, which scatter less and manage to punch through. That's why the sky near the sun glows warm colors at dusk and dawn.

You might notice sunsets are sometimes extra vivid — that's usually because there's more dust, smoke, or pollution in the air, which scatters even more of the shorter wavelengths and leaves an even richer red behind. Volcanic ash is famous for producing spectacular sunsets for exactly this reason.

So the short version: blue sky comes from short-wavelength light scattering in every direction overhead, and red sunsets come from that same scattering filtering out everything but the long, lazy reds over a much longer path through the air.
