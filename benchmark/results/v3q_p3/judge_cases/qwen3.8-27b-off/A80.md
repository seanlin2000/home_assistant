USER: What does it mean to quantize a 70-billion-parameter language model to 4-bit or 2-bit precision? How much memory would the weights require in each case, and what trade-offs does quantization introduce?

ASSISTANT: Quantizing a language model means replacing its full-precision floating-point weights with lower-precision numbers so they take less room and can be crammed into hardware that couldn't hold them otherwise. A 70-billion-parameter model trained in standard precision stores each weight as a 16-bit or 32-bit float, but quantization maps those values onto a coarser grid of integers, typically 8 bits, 4 bits, or even 2 bits per weight, using tricks like group-wise scaling to preserve as much accuracy as possible.

For the memory math: 70 billion parameters times 4 bits each works out to about 35 gigabytes for the weights alone, since four bits is half a byte. At 2-bit precision you'd cut that in half again to roughly 17.5 gigabytes. For comparison, the same model at 16 bits would need around 140 gigabytes, and at 32 bits about 280 gigabytes, which is why quantization is what makes a 70B model runnable on a workstation GPU or even a high-end laptop instead of a server cluster.

The trade-offs are mostly about quality versus efficiency. Going from 16 bits to 4 bits usually costs only a small amount of accuracy for most models, and well-tuned 4-bit quantization is often considered a sweet spot where you keep nearly all the original capability at a quarter of the memory. Dropping to 2 bits is much more aggressive: the weights lose a lot of information, so you typically see noticeably degraded outputs, more incoherent or repetitive text, and worse performance on reasoning tasks. Quantization also introduces some overhead from storing scale factors and metadata for each group of weights, which can eat into part of the savings at very low bit counts. And while quantized models run faster in memory-bandwidth-bound situations because they move fewer bytes, they often need specialized kernels to actually realize speedups on real hardware.

So the practical rule of thumb is that 4-bit is a safe, well-understood choice for running large models locally, while 2-bit is more of an experimental territory where you're trading meaningful quality for fitting into very tight memory budgets.
