Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: That's a great question, and it comes down to how we value any business that promises to pay you in the future. The standard way to figure out what a company is worth is to take all the cash it will generate over the next decade or two, bring each of those future dollars back to today's value, and add them up. That's the discounted cash flow idea, and the rate you use to pull future money back into the present is closely tied to short-term interest rates set by the Fed.

Here's the key: when rates go up, that discount rate goes up too. And raising a number in the denominator of a fraction makes the whole thing smaller, so every single future dollar is worth less in today's terms. The effect isn't uniform though. A dollar coming next year barely changes value, but a dollar promised ten or fifteen years from now gets hit much harder because you're compounding that higher discount rate over many more periods.

This is exactly why tech stocks are so sensitive. Most big technology companies are what investors call long-duration assets. Their entire investment pitch isn't about the cash they generate today; it's about explosive growth that will pay off years from now. A company whose value is mostly in its distant future cash flows looks dramatically cheaper when you discount those flows at a higher rate. The same math also raises the hurdle: when the Fed tightens, safe assets like bonds and savings accounts start paying more, so investors demand a higher return to put money into risky growth stocks instead.

There's one important caveat worth mentioning. Not all tech stocks behave this way. Mature companies that already generate big cash flows today, like a utility-style business, are much less rate-sensitive because most of their value is in near-term cash. The pressure really hits the young, high-growth names whose value lives far out on the timeline. So it's not "tech" as a category so much as long-duration growth that gets squeezed when rates rise.
