Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
Choppy audio: the speaker may also be paired with the laptop, so the laptop competes for the connection (multipoint); 2.4 GHz interference from Wi-Fi and the laptop's own Bluetooth; distance or obstacles; a weak Bluetooth chip or codec in the projector. Steps, one change at a time: turn off the laptop's Bluetooth or unpair the speaker from it, move the devices closer, put the laptop on 5 GHz Wi-Fi, update the firmware, and test a wired audio link or a low-latency transmitter. Suggestions: a darker room or balanced brightness, a game mode, correct focus and seating distance, and breaks. Should separate the two problems and give an order of tests.

# Exchange 1

USER: My projector is connected to a JBL Bluetooth speaker. Sometimes the audio becomes choppy when I connect my laptop, and playing video games on the projector causes more eye strain than playing on my monitor. What could cause these problems, and how would you systematically troubleshoot them?

ASSISTANT: Let me take these one at a time, since they're separate problems.

The choppiness when your laptop joins is almost certainly radio interference. Bluetooth works on 2.4 gigahertz, the same band as most home Wi-Fi. When your laptop connects, it either starts using the same crowded band, or it's on Bluetooth itself and the speaker has to juggle two sources. The fix to try first is the cheapest: move the speaker closer to the projector, keep it away from the Wi-Fi router, and if your router supports 5 gigahertz Wi-Fi, move the laptop's connection over there so 2.4 gigahertz is quieter for the speaker. If the choppiness persists, skip the radio altogether and wire it — a 3.5-millimeter audio cable or an optical cable from the projector to the speaker eliminates the whole class of problem and is worth doing for a permanent setup. Also check the speaker's firmware, since JBL has shipped updates that fix exactly this kind of drop-out.

The eye strain is expected to some degree, and the main culprits are refresh rate and image quality. A monitor, especially one you're gaming on, is probably 120 or 144 hertz with a sharp, high-contrast panel. A projector is usually 60 hertz, softer, and you're looking at a much larger image with more ambient light reflecting off the screen. The refresh rate difference is a big one — the choppier frame updates genuinely tire your eyes. You can mitigate it by dimming the room to improve contrast, making sure the projection is square and in focus rather than keystone-distorted, and using the 20-20-20 rule during long sessions. If the projector supports 120 hertz, enabling it helps a lot. But if gaming comfort is a priority, the honest answer is that the monitor is the right tool for it.

To troubleshoot systematically, change one variable at a time and note what happens. For the audio: wired connection first, since that either fixes it or proves the cause is wireless. Then firmware, then router channel. For the eye strain: compare the same game on the monitor at 60 hertz versus its normal rate — if the strain drops, refresh rate is your limiting factor; if it's about the same, it's the image size and room light, so work on those. That way you're not guessing which fix actually worked.
