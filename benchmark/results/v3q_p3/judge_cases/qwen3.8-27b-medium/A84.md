Reference sketch (what a correct answer must contain):
A stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at a rate that builds on the risk-free rate. When the Fed raises rates the discount rate rises, and cash flows far in the future lose much more present value than near ones (as with long-dated bonds). Growth and technology companies get most of their value from profits expected years ahead, so they are long-duration assets and their valuations fall more. Also: bonds become a more attractive alternative, borrowing costs rise for unprofitable companies, and valuation multiples compress (2022, when the Nasdaq fell about a third, is the standard example). A worked figure helps but is optional: 100 dollars due in 10 years is worth about 74 today at 3 percent and about 56 at 6 percent.

# Exchange 1

USER: Why does the Federal Reserve raising interest rates generally put downward pressure on technology stock valuations? Explain the relationship between interest rates, discounted cash flows, and long-duration growth stocks.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and it's really about how we assign value to future money.

The core idea is that a stock's fair value is, at bottom, the present value of the cash it will generate over its life. To get that present value, we discount all those future cash flows back to today using some rate that reflects how much investors require to be patient. When the Fed raises rates, that discount rate goes up, and here's the key asymmetry: cash flows that are far in the future get punished much more than cash flows that are near.

Think of it mathematically. A dollar in two years discounted at a slightly higher rate barely changes in value. But a dollar in twenty or thirty years, discounted through many more compounding periods, drops sharply. So a company worth mostly on cash it will earn tomorrow — a mature utility, say — is relatively insensitive to the rate. A young tech company whose projected revenue might peak in ten or fifteen years is built out of those distant, heavily discounted dollars. When rates rise, a large chunk of its model literally shrinks. That's what people mean by "duration" when they're not talking about bonds: long-duration growth stocks behave like long-maturity bonds, because most of their value sits out on the far end of the timeline.

A couple of reinforcing effects make it worse. First, higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, and many growth companies fund expansion with debt or cheap capital. Their margins and their ability to buy their way to scale take a hit. Second, and this is more of a valuation channel than a cash flow one: when risk-free assets like Treasury bills pay more, investors don't need to take on risky, far-out, speculative bets just to get a return. The required risk premium effectively moves the other way, and capital rotates out of stretched growth multiples into cash, bonds, or value stocks. So you get both the mechanical present-value compression and the behavioral repricing of risk happening at once.

The flip side is why growth stocks are usually the first to rally when the Fed is expected to cut: the discount rate falls, the distant cash flows re-inflate, and the same investors come hunting for equity risk again. That's why tech indexes tend to be so tightly correlated with rate expectations — the market is constantly re-running that discounting model in real time.

One honest caveat: this is the general theory, and real markets don't follow it perfectly. A company with genuinely dominant economics, rising real earnings, and pricing power can still rise into higher rates. The mechanism I described is about the valuation multiple applied to those earnings — the denominator effect — which is exactly what got crushed in the late 2020s tech drawdowns.
