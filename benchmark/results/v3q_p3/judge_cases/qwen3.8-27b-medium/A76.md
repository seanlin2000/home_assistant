USER: How did the Cold War end? Explain the three most important events that led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and distinguish between the causes and the immediate triggers.

ASSISTANT: The Cold War ended, in large part, from the inside out, and the Soviet Union's dissolution followed within a year. I think of the story in three layers: deep causes, a turning point, and a final trigger.

The first and deepest cause was Gorbachev's own reforms. Starting in the mid-eighties, he introduced Glasnost, which meant opening up political speech and ending the monopoly on information, and Perestroika, which meant restructuring the economy away from central planning. The intent was to save the system, but the effect was to remove the two pillars that had held it together: censorship and coercion. Once people could talk openly about shortages, corruption, and national grievances, the old social contract unraveled. Economically, the Soviet system was already in trouble. It had been bleeding resources into the arms race and into propsing up Eastern Europe for decades, and by the early eighties growth had essentially stalled. Gorbachev didn't create that malaise, but his reforms made it impossible to paper over.

The second event, the real turning point, was the collapse of the Eastern Bloc in 1989. The fall of the Berlin Wall in November of that year is the symbol, but the bigger story was that Gorbachev withdrew the implicit threat of Soviet tanks that had kept satellite regimes in power. Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany all changed hands in a matter of months, mostly through peaceful negotiation. This did two devastating things to the Soviet leadership. It destroyed the idea that communism was a growing system, and it stripped the Soviet Union of the buffer zone that had been a core strategic guarantee since World War Two. The military-industrial complex, the party's most powerful constituency, watched its entire project evaporate almost overnight.

The third event, the immediate trigger, was the failed coup of August 1991. Hardline officials in the military, the security services, and the party apparatus decided that the reforms were going too far and tried to oust Gorbachev, who was at the time in a dacha on the Crimean coast. They set up a "State Committee on the State of Emergency," but it never really got moving. The plotters were hesitant, they made contradictory announcements, and crucially, Boris Yeltsin, then the head of the Russian republic, stood on the roof of the Moscow White House and denounced the coup. Ordinary Muscovites gathered around him, and the tanks rolled home after about three days. The coup's failure was the point of no return. It discredited the old party and military leadership completely, handed enormous political power to figures like Yeltsin, and accelerated the centrifugal pull of the republics. Within a few months, Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belavezha Accords, declaring the USSR effectively defunct and creating the Commonwealth of Independent States in its place. Gorbachev resigned on December twenty-fifth, and the Soviet flag came down the next day.

So to draw the line you asked about: the causes were structural and slow-moving, decades of economic stagnation, the unsustainable cost of superpower rivalry, and the rigidity of a one-party system. Gorbachev's reforms were the match, but the building was already rotten. The immediate trigger was the coup, a desperate, clumsy attempt by the old guard to reverse the process, which backfired so spectacularly that the system simply had no mechanism left to reassert itself. It wasn't one person or one moment. It was a structure that lost its ability to hold itself together, followed by a final shock that made the collapse immediate rather than gradual.
