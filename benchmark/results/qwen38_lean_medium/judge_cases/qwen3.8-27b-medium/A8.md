# Exchange 1

USER: For a personal AI assistant, compare these two designs: one large LLM handles every request, versus a smaller router that sends simple commands to deterministic tools and only uses a larger LLM for complex questions. Which would you recommend and why?

ASSISTANT: I would recommend the router-and-tools design, with one important twist on how you implement the router. Here's my reasoning.

The case for the router is pretty strong for a personal assistant specifically. You are going to fire off the same handful of requests dozens of times a day: turn on the lights, what time is it, set a reminder, play some music. Pushing "turn on the living room lights" through a forty-billion-parameter model just to get back a JSON tool call is wasteful in latency, in money, and in reliability. A deterministic path from voice to API call takes a few milliseconds and does exactly the same thing every time, which matters when the command is "lock the front door" or "cancel the alarm at 6 a.m." You do not want a 99.5 percent probability on your front door. A small rule-matcher or a tiny classifier handles those known patterns, and the rest falls through to the larger model.

The case against a purely monolithic design is mostly about the long tail of failure modes. A big model is brilliant at novelty, yes, but it is also the component that goes down when the provider has a brownout, that hits a rate limit during your morning routine, or that quietly reformats a command in a way your smart-home bridge does not expect. If "play jazz on the kitchen speakers" is a hard-wired tool call, it works even on the day the LLM API is having a bad Tuesday.

Now the twist, and this is where I would push back on the naïve version of the router design. Do not build a separate small model whose only job is to guess whether a sentence is "simple" or "complex." That guess is exactly where you get the worst failures: "dim the kitchen lights a little, then put on some soft jazz, and remind me to call my sister in twenty minutes" is three simple commands in a sentence that a router might classify as complex and send to the big model, or it might classify as simple and then choke on the multi-step structure. Instead, let the large model do the routing as part of its normal tool-calling pass, and layer deterministic shortcuts on top of it for the very highest-frequency, very low-ambiguity patterns. In practice that means a small set of regex or intent matches for the twenty or thirty commands you will actually say every day, with everything else going straight to the LLM, which sees the full tool list and decides for itself. You get the speed and determinism where it counts, you do not pay for a second model to be wrong, and the fallback is clean: if the shortcuts do not match, the LLM handles it, and the LLM still has tools, so it is not stuck with a free-text answer when it could have actually turned the light on.

One more practical point. Keep the "simple" set narrow and boring. Device on and off, time, a basic calendar lookup, a music playback command. Resist the temptation to route "what should I make for dinner" or "summarize this email" through a fast path, because the moment the user's phrasing drifts a little from the template you encoded, the deterministic path either fails or, worse, does something plausible-sounding but wrong. The large model is where you want all the ambiguity to live.

So the short version: router-and-tools, where the router is mostly a handful of hard-coded fast paths and the real routing is the LLM's own tool selection, and the large model always keeps tool access so it is not just a chat box sitting behind the fast paths. That gets you the sub-second responsiveness and determinism you want for the things you say fifty times a day, without building a brittle classifier that has to guess where the boundary between simple and complex actually is.
