GPU pricing has gone through sharp shocks before, most memorably the cryptocurrency mining boom of 2017-2018 and again in 2021, when scarcity and scalping pushed GeForce and Radeon cards to double or triple their list prices. The bottleneck is not finished-card assembly or even GPU die supply from TSMC. ASSISTANT: The short answer is that this isn't a graphics-chip shortage — it's a memory shortage, and it's the memory half of the card that's exploding in cost. Here's the chain as best I can piece it together from recent reporting.

The root cause is AI data center demand. Hyperscalers have locked up most of the world's high-bandwidth memory output for AI accelerators, and Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron have reportedly sold out their 2026 HBM capacity. Because HBM is built on the same wafer capacity as ordinary DRAM — and GDDR, the memory that sits on a consumer GPU — less of everything is left for graphics cards. Contract prices for conventional DRAM have been climbing roughly 20% per quarter, and TrendForce was even projecting a 58 to 63 percent jump in one quarter of 2026. Memory is now the dominant cost in a modern GPU, so a memory price spike becomes a card price spike almost directly.

On top of that, the memory is the specific scarce type the newest cards need. The RTX 50 series uses GDDR7, which is the tightest class of memory, since advanced capacity is being steered toward server DRAM and HBM instead. NVIDIA's own CFO put it on the record that memory scarcity is being driven by the AI build-out itself, and NVIDIA sells bundled GPU-plus-memory kits to its board partners, so the cost flows straight through to retail.

Two secondary factors make it worse. One, partners are reporting genuine supply shortfalls — MSI told investors there's about a 20 percent GPU supply gap from NVIDIA and warned of further price increases, which shrinks the pool of cheap cards and pushes everyone toward the pricier ones. Two, NVIDIA is reportedly reallocating its own silicon toward higher-margin pro and data center cards, which tightens availability of top consumer models like the 5090 and 5080 specifically.

It's worth noting what it's *not*: it's not the GPU die or TSMC fab capacity, and tariffs appear to be a minor factor — the 2026 tariff actions that get blamed don't actually cover graphics cards, by their own text. And unlike the 2021 crypto-driven shortage, this one is backed by multi-year hyperscaler spending commitments, so it's less likely to reverse quickly. On timing, the new memory fabs from Micron and others don't start meaningful output until mid-2027 or 2028, so most analysts are treating 2026 through 2027 as a sustained high-price period rather than a spike that pops soon.

One caveat: the specific price numbers you'd see quoted — like a 41 percent jump in Europe over the summer or a 5070 selling $320 over launch price — are real tracked data, but street prices move weekly, so treat any single figure as a snapshot rather than a fact.
