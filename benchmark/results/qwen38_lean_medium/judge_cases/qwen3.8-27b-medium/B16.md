ASSISTANT: So, is a four percent annual increase in Long Island City competitive right now? The short answer: it's in the middle of the road — arguably better than the neighborhood's current pace, and slightly worse than the citywide pace.

Here's what the numbers say. As of late 2026, the median asking rent in Long Island City is around $4,595 a month, and it's only up about one percent year over year. Other trackers put the average rent in LIC up somewhere between 1.7 and 2.3 percent over the past year. So locally, rents have been growing more slowly than four percent. But zoom out to all of New York: the median asking rent hit a record $3,785 in the third quarter of 2026, up 4.5 percent year over year, and studios and smaller one- and two-bedroom apartments — which is probably what you're in — rose 7.1 percent. Queens overall was up 3.9 percent.

So if you sign a two-year lease with a fixed four percent bump in year two, you're getting a below-average increase relative to where the broader city is heading, and above the pace LIC has shown recently. There's also a real structural reason to expect upward pressure: a rent freeze on roughly a million rent-stabilized apartments took effect October 1st and runs through September 2027, which economists from Realtor.com explicitly said could push market rents higher as landlords try to recoup. In a rising market, a locked-in four percent cap is genuinely decent protection. I'd call it a fair, if not spectacular, deal — not one I'd walk away from, but also not something to celebrate.

Now the risks of using advertised rents as your comparison, which are worth being honest about. First, asking rent is not what people actually pay. A lot of listings come with concessions — free months, discounted first months — that don't show up in the headline figure, and Apartment List's own data tracks how many listings are running specials. The real effective rent is often below the advertised number, so a market that looks like it's growing four and a half percent might be growing less once you count free months. Second, advertised rents skew toward what landlords hope to get, especially when a unit has been sitting vacant. A listing can be marked up for weeks before it gets cut, which flatters the median. Third, asking-rent data mostly captures market-rate listings on major sites; it under-represents rent-stabilized units, small buildings that don't advertise, and renewal deals, so it's not a full picture of what a tenant would actually pay. Fourth, the LIC figures are for September 2026, two months old, and medians of a few hundred dollars move fast in this market.

One practical suggestion: compare the four percent increase not to citywide averages but to what a comparable unit in your specific building or street would ask for on a new lease in year two. If your lease renewal is likely to be at or below what a new market lease would cost, the deal is solid — and you can check that by looking at comps near your address rather than the neighborhood median.
