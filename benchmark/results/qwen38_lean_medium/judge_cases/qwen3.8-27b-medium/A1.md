# Exchange 1

USER: Why can a 16 GB GPU sometimes run an MoE model with 30 billion total parameters, but not necessarily a 30 billion parameter dense model? Explain the difference between active parameters and the memory required to store the model.

ASSISTANT: Great question, and it comes down to separating two different things that people often conflate: the memory needed to *store* the weights, and the compute and memory needed to *use* them on each token.

First, storage. The memory to hold a model's weights is determined by total parameter count times the number of bits per parameter — nothing about "active" parameters helps here. A 30 billion parameter model in full 16-bit precision is about 60 gigabytes of weights, whether it's dense or an MoE, because every expert's weights still have to live in memory; a token might not touch 95% of them, but they can't be deleted. So in 16-bit, neither a 30B dense model nor a 30B MoE fits in 16 gigabytes at all.

So where does the 16 gigabyte card come from? Two things. First, quantization: at 4 bits per parameter, 30 billion parameters take roughly 15 to 16 gigabytes, and both model types benefit equally from that. Second, and this is the real difference: in an MoE model, each token only activates a small subset of the experts, often just three or four billion parameters out of the 30. That means the arithmetic per token, and the intermediate activations you have to hold in memory while computing, are far smaller — closer to what a dense model of a few billion parameters would cost. On a 16 gigabyte card, after the quantized weights are loaded you have only a couple of gigabytes left for the key-value cache and activations, and the MoE's small active footprint squeezes in there. A dense 30 billion model at the same quantization has all 30 billion weights active every single token, so its activations and compute overhead are much larger, and it blows past that leftover budget or runs far too slowly to be practical.

To put it plainly: total parameters set the memory bill, and active parameters set the per-token cost. An MoE model is expensive to store but cheap to run; that's exactly the tradeoff the architecture is designed to buy, and it's why a modest card can serve a huge MoE that would be out of reach for an equally sized dense model.
